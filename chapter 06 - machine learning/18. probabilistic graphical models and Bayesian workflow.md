# Probabilistic Graphical Models and the Bayesian Workflow

*A probabilistic model writes down how data could have been generated, and inference runs that story backwards. This file covers how graphs make large joint distributions manageable (Bayesian networks and Markov random fields), exact inference by variable elimination and belief propagation, approximate inference by sampling (Gibbs sampling and Hamiltonian Monte Carlo, with the diagnostics that tell you when they fail) and by optimisation (variational inference and EM), latent variable and hierarchical models, and the workflow of checking, comparing and improving models, with probabilistic programming tools that automate the inference*

- The uncertainty file (Gaussian processes and uncertainty) introduced Bayesian inference, Metropolis-Hastings, Hamiltonian Monte Carlo and variational inference for a single model. This file is about **building** models: structuring many variables, choosing an inference method that suits the structure, and checking whether the model is any good.

- The appeal of probabilistic modelling is that assumptions are explicit, uncertainty propagates automatically, prior knowledge enters naturally, and missing data, measurement error and grouped structure are handled within one framework. The costs are that inference can be slow or approximate, and that the model is only as good as its assumptions, which is why checking is part of the method.

## Graphs for Joint Distributions

- A joint distribution over $n$ binary variables needs $2^n - 1$ numbers, hopeless beyond a few dozen variables. **Conditional independence** (chapter 05) makes it manageable: most variables interact directly with only a few others, and a graph records which.

- A **Bayesian network** is a directed acyclic graph in which each variable depends directly only on its parents. The joint distribution factorises as

$$p(x_1, \ldots, x_n) = \prod_{i=1}^{n} p\big(x_i \mid \text{parents}(x_i)\big)$$

- In the classic alarm network (Pearl), a burglary or an earthquake can set off an alarm, which may prompt two neighbours to call. Five binary variables would need 31 numbers in general; the network needs 10. The arrows often follow causal intuition, and the same graphs, with interventions added, are the causal graphs of chapter 06's causal inference file.

- The graph alone determines which independences hold (**d-separation**), using the same three patterns as in causal inference: chains and forks are blocked by conditioning on the middle variable, while a **collider** $A \rightarrow C \leftarrow B$ is the reverse. Burglary and earthquake are independent, but once the alarm is known to have rung, learning there was an earthquake makes a burglary much less likely. This is **explaining away**, computed exactly in the first coding task.

- A **Markov random field** (MRF, or undirected graphical model) uses an undirected graph and expresses the joint distribution as a normalised product of non-negative **potentials** on cliques (fully connected groups of variables):

$$p(\mathbf{x}) = \frac{1}{Z} \prod_{c} \psi_c(\mathbf{x}_c), \qquad Z = \sum_{\mathbf{x}} \prod_c \psi_c(\mathbf{x}_c)$$

- MRFs suit symmetric interactions with no natural direction: neighbouring pixels, interacting spins in physics (the **Ising model**), or amino acids in contact (chapter 19's protein design file). Each variable is independent of all others given its neighbours (its **Markov blanket**). The **partition function** $Z$ sums over all configurations and is usually intractable, which makes learning MRFs hard. **Conditional random fields** (CRFs) are MRFs conditioned on inputs, long used for sequence labelling and segmentation (chapter 07's classic NLP file, chapter 08). **Factor graphs**, bipartite graphs of variables and factors, represent both kinds uniformly and are what inference algorithms operate on.

- Hidden Markov models (chapter 05), Kalman filters (chapter 06's time series file), Gaussian mixtures and topic models are all graphical models; many classical algorithms are special cases of general inference on graphs.

## Exact Inference

- Inference means computing **marginals** ($p(x_i \mid \text{evidence})$) or the **most probable configuration**. Summing the full joint is exponential. **Variable elimination** pushes sums inside the product, eliminating one variable at a time:

$$p(b, j, m) = p(b) \sum_e p(e) \sum_a p(a \mid b, e)\, p(j \mid a)\, p(m \mid a)$$

- Its cost is exponential only in the size of the largest intermediate factor, set by the graph's **treewidth**; good elimination orders matter, and finding the best is itself hard.

- **Belief propagation** (sum-product message passing) computes all marginals at once by passing messages along edges: each node tells each neighbour what it believes about that neighbour, given everything on its own side. On trees it is exact in two passes, and it is the HMM forward-backward algorithm in general form; replacing sums by maxima gives the most probable configuration (Viterbi). The **junction tree** algorithm makes it exact on any graph by clustering variables into a tree, at a cost exponential in treewidth.

- On graphs with loops, running the same updates anyway (**loopy belief propagation**) has no guarantee of converging or being correct, yet often works remarkably well: it decodes the LDPC and turbo error-correcting codes used in Wi-Fi, 5G and deep-space communication.

## Approximate Inference by Sampling

- **Forward (ancestral) sampling** draws from a Bayesian network by sampling parents before children. With evidence, **likelihood weighting** fixes observed variables and weights each sample by the probability of the evidence; it degrades when the evidence is improbable, because few samples get meaningful weight (the first coding task).

- **Gibbs sampling** updates one variable (or block) at a time by drawing it from its conditional distribution given all the others. In a graphical model that conditional depends only on the Markov blanket, so each update is cheap and local, and the chain's stationary distribution is the posterior. The second coding task denoises an image with an Ising MRF, comparing Gibbs sampling with **iterated conditional modes** (greedily setting each pixel to its most probable value) and mean-field inference. Gibbs is also natural when every conditional is a standard distribution, as in the hierarchical model of the third coding task (conjugate priors, chapter 05). It struggles when variables are strongly correlated, because changing one at a time moves slowly along a narrow ridge.

- **Hamiltonian Monte Carlo** (HMC) uses the gradient of the log posterior to make long, informed moves, and **NUTS** (the no-U-turn sampler, Hoffman and Gelman, 2014) tunes the trajectory length automatically. NUTS is the default in Stan, PyMC and NumPyro for continuous parameters. Discrete parameters cannot use gradients and are summed out analytically or handled with Gibbs steps.

### When sampling fails, and how to tell

- MCMC gives the right answer only in the limit, and a chain that has not converged gives a wrong answer with no error message. Standard diagnostics:
    - **Multiple chains** from dispersed starting points, and **R-hat** (the Gelman-Rubin statistic): the ratio of between-chain to within-chain variance, which should be very close to 1 (below about 1.01).
    - **Effective sample size** (ESS): correlated draws carry less information than independent ones; ESS estimates the equivalent number of independent draws from the autocorrelation, and should be at least several hundred for stable estimates.
    - **Trace plots**, which should look like stationary noise, not trends or long excursions.
    - **Divergences** in HMC: the numerical integrator breaks down in regions of high curvature, a sign that the sampler cannot explore part of the posterior.

- Hierarchical models produce a notorious geometry, **Neal's funnel**: when the group-level standard deviation is small, the group effects must all be close to the mean, so the posterior narrows into a neck that a sampler with a fixed step size cannot enter. The fourth coding task shows HMC on the funnel reporting too few small values (a biased answer that looks plausible), with R-hat, ESS and divergences flagging the problem. The cure is **reparameterisation**: write $\theta_j = \mu + \tau z_j$ with $z_j \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1)$ (the **non-centred** form). The model is unchanged, but the sampler sees independent standard normals. Reparameterising is often more effective than any amount of extra sampling.

## Approximate Inference by Optimisation

- **Variational inference** (VI) picks the member of a tractable family $q$ closest to the posterior by maximising the ELBO, the lower bound on the log evidence introduced in the uncertainty file. Variants:
    - **Mean-field** VI assumes $q$ factorises over variables, $q(\mathbf{x}) = \prod_i q_i(x_i)$; **coordinate ascent VI** updates each factor in turn, and in an MRF each update replaces neighbours by their expected values, as in the second coding task. Because it minimises $\text{KL}(q \,\|\, p)$, which heavily penalises putting mass where $p$ has little, mean-field VI tends to be **overconfident**, underestimating variances and ignoring correlations; the coding task measures this against Gibbs sampling.
    - **Stochastic VI** uses minibatches of data, and **black-box VI** estimates ELBO gradients by Monte Carlo with the reparameterisation trick, so it applies to almost any differentiable model.
    - **Amortised inference** trains a neural network to output the variational parameters for each data point, which is exactly the encoder of a variational autoencoder (chapter 08's generative models).
    - **Expectation propagation** minimises the reverse divergence locally and tends to give better-calibrated variances, at the cost of weaker convergence guarantees.

- **Expectation-maximisation** (EM), used for Gaussian mixtures (chapter 06's clustering file), is coordinate ascent on the same ELBO: the E-step sets $q$ to the exact posterior over latent variables given the current parameters, and the M-step maximises over parameters. When the exact posterior over latents is intractable, replacing the E-step by a variational one gives **variational EM**.

- The **Laplace approximation** fits a Gaussian at the posterior mode using the curvature there (the uncertainty file). It is fast and often good for large datasets, where posteriors become nearly Gaussian.

- Rough guidance: MCMC (NUTS) when accuracy matters and the model has up to hundreds of thousands of parameters; VI when the data or model is very large and some approximation is acceptable; exact inference when the graph has low treewidth; Laplace for a quick first answer.

## Latent Variable Models

- Many models explain observed data through unobserved (latent) structure:
    - **Mixture models**: each point comes from one of $K$ components (chapter 06's clustering file).
    - **Hidden Markov models** and **state-space models**: a latent sequence generates the observations (chapter 05, chapter 06's time series file).
    - **Factor analysis** and **probabilistic PCA**: a few continuous latent factors generate high-dimensional data with noise (chapter 02's PCA as a limiting case).
    - **Topic models**: latent Dirichlet allocation (Blei, Ng and Jordan, 2003) represents each document as a mixture of topics and each topic as a distribution over words, and was the standard tool for exploring text collections before neural embeddings.
    - **Deep latent variable models**: variational autoencoders and diffusion models (chapter 08) are latent variable models whose conditional distributions are neural networks.

- Latent variable posteriors are often **multimodal**: relabelling the components of a mixture gives an identical likelihood (**label switching**), which complicates both sampling and interpretation.

## Hierarchical Models

- Data often come in groups: patients within hospitals, students within schools, users within regions, repeated measurements within people. A **hierarchical** (multilevel) model gives each group its own parameter and lets the group parameters share a common distribution, whose parameters are also learned:

$$y_{ij} \sim \mathcal{N}(\theta_j, \sigma^2), \qquad \theta_j \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu, \tau^2), \qquad \mu, \tau \sim \text{priors}$$

- The posterior mean of each group parameter is a precision-weighted compromise between the group's own average and the overall mean,

$$\mathbb{E}[\theta_j \mid \text{data}] \approx \frac{(n_j / \sigma^2)\, \bar{y}_j + (1/\tau^2)\, \mu}{n_j / \sigma^2 + 1/\tau^2},$$

- so groups with little data are **shrunk** strongly towards the mean and groups with much data keep their own estimates. This **partial pooling** sits between estimating every group separately (no pooling, noisy for small groups) and ignoring the groups (complete pooling, which misses real differences), and it learns from the data how much to pool, through $\tau$. The third coding task builds a Gibbs sampler for this model and shows partial pooling estimating hospital effects more accurately overall than either extreme.

- Shrinkage is not a Bayesian quirk: Stein (1956) proved that when estimating three or more means, shrinking towards a common value beats using each sample mean on average, a surprising result in classical statistics. Hierarchical models also handle the multiple comparisons problem gracefully: extreme estimates from small groups are pulled back automatically. They are the standard approach for small-area estimation, sports and education statistics, meta-analysis, and A/B tests across many segments (chapter 18).

## The Bayesian Workflow

- Gelman et al. (2020) describe modelling as an iterative loop rather than a single fit:
    1. **Build** a model from domain knowledge, starting simple.
    2. **Prior predictive checks**: simulate data from the prior alone. If the prior implies absurd data (human heights of kilometres, probabilities stuck at 0 or 1), revise it. **Weakly informative priors** rule out the absurd while letting the data dominate.
    3. **Fit** the model and **check the computation**: R-hat, ESS, divergences, and ideally **simulation-based calibration** (fit the model to data simulated from it and confirm the true parameters are recovered at the expected rates).
    4. **Posterior predictive checks**: simulate replicated datasets from the fitted model and compare them with the real data on statistics that matter for the application. A statistic far outside the replicated range shows how the model fails. In the fifth coding task, a Poisson model reproduces the mean of count data but not its variance or its number of zeros; a negative binomial model reproduces all three.
    5. **Compare and expand**: compare candidate models by their **expected log predictive density** on held-out data, estimated by cross-validation or efficiently by **PSIS-LOO** (Pareto-smoothed importance sampling leave-one-out, Vehtari et al., 2017) or WAIC. **Bayes factors** compare marginal likelihoods but are very sensitive to the priors, which makes them less reliable for model choice in practice.
    6. Use the posterior for **decisions** by minimising expected loss under it (Bayesian decision theory), which turns uncertainty into actions, as in the cost-based thresholds of chapter 06's practical data file.

- The workflow's habits (simulate from the model, check it against data, compare by predictive performance) apply to all of machine learning, not just Bayesian models.

## Probabilistic Programming

- **Probabilistic programming languages** let you write the generative model as code and get inference automatically. **Stan** (its own language, with NUTS and excellent diagnostics) is the standard for statistical modelling; **PyMC** and **NumPyro** (on JAX, fast on accelerators) are Python libraries with NUTS and VI; **Pyro** (on PyTorch) emphasises deep probabilistic models and stochastic VI. A hierarchical model in NumPyro is a few lines:

```
def model(group, y=None):
    mu = numpyro.sample("mu", dist.Normal(0, 5))
    tau = numpyro.sample("tau", dist.HalfNormal(2))
    with numpyro.plate("groups", n_groups):
        z = numpyro.sample("z", dist.Normal(0, 1))          # non-centred parameterisation
    theta = mu + tau * z
    numpyro.sample("y", dist.Normal(theta[group], sigma), obs=y)
```

- The library computes gradients of the log density by automatic differentiation, runs NUTS, and reports diagnostics. Packages such as ArviZ handle posterior predictive checks and LOO comparisons. These tools have made Bayesian modelling routine in epidemiology, ecology, pharmacology, sports analytics, marketing mix modelling and much of science.

1. A Bayesian network: exact inference by enumeration and by variable elimination, explaining away, and approximate inference by likelihood weighting.
```python
import numpy as np
import itertools

# A Bayesian network: Burglary and Earthquake can each set off an Alarm; John and Mary may call when it rings.
# Joint distribution = product of each variable's probability given its parents:
#   P(B, E, A, J, M) = P(B) P(E) P(A | B, E) P(J | A) P(M | A)
P_B = np.array([0.999, 0.001])                       # index 0 = false, 1 = true
P_E = np.array([0.998, 0.002])
P_A = np.zeros((2, 2, 2))                            # P_A[b, e, a]
for b, e, p in [(1, 1, 0.95), (1, 0, 0.94), (0, 1, 0.29), (0, 0, 0.001)]:
    P_A[b, e] = [1 - p, p]
P_J = np.array([[0.95, 0.05], [0.10, 0.90]])         # P_J[a, j]
P_M = np.array([[0.99, 0.01], [0.30, 0.70]])         # P_M[a, m]

# 32 entries: small enough to build the whole joint table by broadcasting (axes B, E, A, J, M)
joint = (P_B[:, None, None, None, None] * P_E[None, :, None, None, None] * P_A[:, :, :, None, None]
         * P_J[None, None, :, :, None] * P_M[None, None, :, None, :])
print(f"joint table sums to {joint.sum():.6f}; 5 binary variables need 31 numbers in general,"
      f" this network needs {1 + 1 + 4 + 2 + 2}")

AX = dict(B=0, E=1, A=2, J=3, M=4)
def query(var, **evidence):
    """P(var = true | evidence) by summing the joint over everything else (inference by enumeration)."""
    t = joint
    idx = [slice(None)] * 5
    for k, v in evidence.items(): idx[AX[k]] = slice(v, v + 1)
    t = t[tuple(idx)]
    keep = AX[var]
    marg = t.sum(axis=tuple(i for i in range(5) if i != keep))
    return marg[1] / marg.sum()

print(f"\nP(burglary)                                  = {query('B'):.4f}")
print(f"P(burglary | John and Mary both call)       = {query('B', J=1, M=1):.4f}")
print(f"P(burglary | alarm)                         = {query('B', A=1):.4f}")
print(f"P(burglary | alarm, earthquake)             = {query('B', A=1, E=1):.4f}   <- explaining away")
print(f"P(earthquake | alarm)                       = {query('E', A=1):.4f}")
print(f"P(earthquake | alarm, burglary)             = {query('E', A=1, B=1):.4f}")
print(f"B and E independent a priori: P(B | E) = {query('B', E=1):.4f} = P(B)")

# Variable elimination: the same answer without building the joint, by pushing sums inside the product
#   P(B, j, m) = P(B) sum_E P(E) sum_A P(A | B, E) P(j | A) P(m | A)
f_A = P_J[:, 1] * P_M[:, 1]                         # factor over A after fixing J = 1, M = 1
f_BE = (P_A * f_A[None, None, :]).sum(2)            # eliminate A -> factor over (B, E)
f_B = (f_BE * P_E[None, :]).sum(1) * P_B            # eliminate E
print(f"\nvariable elimination: P(burglary | J, M)   = {f_B[1] / f_B.sum():.4f}")

# Approximate inference by likelihood weighting: sample the unobserved variables top-down from the network,
# and weight each sample by how likely the observed evidence is.
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
def likelihood_weighting(n):
    b = rng.random(n) < P_B[1]
    e = rng.random(n) < P_E[1]
    a = rng.random(n) < P_A[b.astype(int), e.astype(int), 1]
    w = P_J[a.astype(int), 1] * P_M[a.astype(int), 1]   # evidence J = 1, M = 1
    return (w * b).sum() / w.sum()
exact = query('B', J=1, M=1)
print(f"\n{'samples':>8s} {'estimate':>9s} {'abs error (mean of 50 runs)':>29s}")
for n in (100, 1000, 10000, 100000):
    est = [likelihood_weighting(n) for _ in range(50)]
    print(f"{n:8d} {np.mean(est):9.4f} {np.mean(np.abs(np.array(est) - exact)):29.4f}")

print("\nEven when both neighbours call, a burglary stays unlikely (about 28%) because burglaries are so rare."
      "\nLearning there was an earthquake makes a burglary much less likely: two independent causes become"
      "\ndependent once their common effect is observed ('explaining away'). Exact inference is exponential in"
      "\ngeneral, so large networks use sampling. Here the evidence is rare (few samples get much weight), so small"
      "\nsample sizes are badly off; with enough samples the error falls roughly like 1/sqrt(samples).")
```

2. Image denoising with an Ising Markov random field: iterated conditional modes, Gibbs sampling and mean-field variational inference, and how confident each is.
```python
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

# A Markov random field for image denoising. Pixels x_i in {-1, +1}; neighbouring pixels prefer to agree
# (an Ising prior), and each observed noisy pixel y_i is evidence about x_i:
#   p(x | y) ∝ exp( J * sum_{neighbours i~j} x_i x_j  +  h * sum_i x_i y_i )
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
n = 96
yy, xx = np.mgrid[:n, :n]
clean = np.where(((xx - 35) ** 2 + (yy - 40) ** 2 < 22 ** 2) | ((xx > 55) & (xx < 85) & (yy > 20) & (yy < 80)), 1, -1)
flip = 0.2
noisy = np.where(rng.random((n, n)) < flip, -clean, clean)
h = 0.5 * np.log((1 - flip) / flip)                 # evidence strength implied by a 20% flip rate
J = 0.8

def neighbour_sum(x):
    s = np.zeros_like(x, dtype=float)
    s[1:] += x[:-1]; s[:-1] += x[1:]; s[:, 1:] += x[:, :-1]; s[:, :-1] += x[:, 1:]
    return s

checker = (xx + yy) % 2 == 0                         # pixels of one colour have no neighbours of the same colour,
                                                     # so each half can be updated in parallel
def icm(x, sweeps=10):
    """Iterated conditional modes: set every pixel to its most probable value given its neighbours."""
    x = x.copy()
    for _ in range(sweeps):
        for mask in (checker, ~checker):
            field = J * neighbour_sum(x) + h * noisy
            x[mask] = np.where(field[mask] >= 0, 1, -1)
    return x

def gibbs(x, sweeps=200, burn=50):
    """Gibbs sampling: draw each pixel from its exact conditional; average samples for posterior marginals."""
    x = x.copy(); total = np.zeros((n, n))
    for t in range(sweeps):
        for mask in (checker, ~checker):
            field = J * neighbour_sum(x) + h * noisy
            p_plus = 1 / (1 + np.exp(-2 * field))       # P(x_i = +1 | neighbours, y_i)
            x[mask] = np.where(rng.random(mask.sum()) < p_plus[mask], 1, -1)
        if t >= burn: total += x
    return total / (sweeps - burn)                      # posterior mean of each pixel, in [-1, 1]

def mean_field(sweeps=50):
    """Mean-field variational inference: replace each neighbour by its expected value m_j."""
    m = noisy.astype(float)
    for _ in range(sweeps):
        m = np.tanh(J * neighbour_sum(m) + h * noisy)
    return m

err = lambda x: np.mean(np.sign(x + 1e-9) != clean)
x_icm = icm(noisy)
post = gibbs(noisy)
m = mean_field()
print(f"noisy image:                       {err(noisy):.1%} of pixels wrong")
print(f"ICM (greedy local optimum):        {err(x_icm):.1%}")
print(f"Gibbs sampling, posterior marginal {err(post):.1%}")
print(f"mean-field variational inference:  {err(m):.1%}")
unsure = np.abs(post) < 0.5
print(f"pixels Gibbs is unsure about (|posterior mean| < 0.5): {unsure.mean():.1%},"
      f" of which wrong after rounding: {np.mean(np.sign(post[unsure]) != clean[unsure]):.0%}")
edge = neighbour_sum(clean) * clean < 4                     # pixels with a differently coloured neighbour
near_edge = edge | np.roll(edge, 1, 0) | np.roll(edge, -1, 0) | np.roll(edge, 1, 1) | np.roll(edge, -1, 1)
print(f"share of those within one pixel of an edge: {near_edge[unsure].mean():.0%} (edges cover {near_edge.mean():.0%} of the image)")
print(f"confidence (|posterior mean|) on those pixels: Gibbs {np.abs(post[unsure]).mean():.2f},"
      f" mean field {np.abs(m[unsure]).mean():.2f}")
print(f"confidence on the pixels each method gets wrong: Gibbs {np.abs(post[np.sign(post) != clean]).mean():.2f},"
      f" mean field {np.abs(m[np.sign(m) != clean]).mean():.2f}")

fig, ax = plt.subplots(1, 4, figsize=(14, 3.6))
for a, img, t in zip(ax, [noisy, x_icm, post, m], ["noisy observation", "ICM", "Gibbs posterior mean", "mean field"]):
    a.imshow(img, cmap="gray", vmin=-1, vmax=1); a.set_title(t); a.axis("off")
plt.tight_layout(); plt.show()

print("\nAll three use only local computations, because in an MRF each pixel depends on the rest only through"
      "\nits neighbours. ICM jumps to a nearby mode; Gibbs explores the posterior and gives calibrated per-pixel"
      "\nuncertainty, concentrated along edges; mean field is fast and deterministic but overconfident, a general"
      "\nproperty of mean-field approximations, which ignore the correlations between variables.")
```

3. A hierarchical model of hospital effects with a hand-written Gibbs sampler: no pooling, complete pooling and partial pooling, and how shrinkage depends on sample size.
```python
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

# Hierarchical (multilevel) model: 40 hospitals, each with its own true effect theta_j, measured on very
# different numbers of patients. How should each hospital's effect be estimated?
#   y_ij ~ Normal(theta_j, sigma^2),   theta_j ~ Normal(mu, tau^2),   priors on mu and tau^2
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
J, sigma = 40, 2.0
mu_true, tau_true = 1.0, 0.6
theta = rng.normal(mu_true, tau_true, J)
n = rng.choice([2, 3, 5, 10, 20, 60], J)
data = [rng.normal(theta[j], sigma, n[j]) for j in range(J)]
ybar = np.array([d.mean() for d in data])

# Gibbs sampler: every conditional distribution here is a standard one, so we can sample them in turn
def gibbs(iters=4000, burn=1000, a0=1.0, b0=0.1):
    mu, tau2 = ybar.mean(), ybar.var()
    keep_theta, keep_mu, keep_tau = [], [], []
    for t in range(iters):
        prec = n / sigma**2 + 1 / tau2                                   # theta_j | mu, tau, data
        th = rng.normal((n * ybar / sigma**2 + mu / tau2) / prec, 1 / np.sqrt(prec))
        mu = rng.normal(th.mean(), np.sqrt(tau2 / J))                      # mu | theta, tau (flat prior)
        tau2 = 1 / rng.gamma(a0 + J / 2, 1 / (b0 + 0.5 * ((th - mu) ** 2).sum()))   # tau^2 | theta, mu
        if t >= burn:
            keep_theta.append(th); keep_mu.append(mu); keep_tau.append(np.sqrt(tau2))
    return np.array(keep_theta), np.array(keep_mu), np.array(keep_tau)

th_s, mu_s, tau_s = gibbs()
partial = th_s.mean(0)
lo, hi = np.percentile(th_s, [5, 95], axis=0)
print(f"posterior: mu = {mu_s.mean():.2f} (true {mu_true}), tau = {tau_s.mean():.2f} (true {tau_true},"
      f" 90% interval {np.percentile(tau_s, 5):.2f}-{np.percentile(tau_s, 95):.2f})")
print(f"90% intervals cover the true theta for {np.mean((theta >= lo) & (theta <= hi)):.0%} of hospitals")

estimates = {"no pooling (each hospital's own mean)": ybar,
             "complete pooling (one overall mean)": np.full(J, ybar.mean()),
             "partial pooling (hierarchical model)": partial}
print(f"\n{'estimator':40s} {'error, small hospitals':>23s} {'error, large hospitals':>23s} {'overall':>8s}")
small = n <= 5
for name, est in estimates.items():
    rmse = lambda m: np.sqrt(np.mean((est[m] - theta[m]) ** 2))
    print(f"{name:40s} {rmse(small):23.2f} {rmse(~small):23.2f} {rmse(np.ones(J, bool)):8.2f}")

shrink = 1 - (partial - mu_s.mean()) / (ybar - mu_s.mean())
print("\naverage shrinkage towards the overall mean by hospital size:")
for k in np.unique(n):
    print(f"  {k:3d} patients: {np.median(shrink[n == k]):.0%}")

fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(8, 4))
for j in range(J):
    ax.plot([0, 1], [ybar[j], partial[j]], color="#3498db", alpha=0.6, lw=0.8 + n[j] / 30)
ax.axhline(mu_s.mean(), color="#e74c3c", ls="--", label="estimated overall mean")
ax.set_xticks([0, 1]); ax.set_xticklabels(["own mean (no pooling)", "partial pooling"])
ax.set_ylabel("estimated effect"); ax.set_title("Shrinkage: thin lines are small hospitals"); ax.legend()
plt.tight_layout(); plt.show()

print("\nNo pooling trusts each small hospital's noisy average; complete pooling ignores real differences."
      "\nThe hierarchical model learns how much hospitals truly vary (tau) and shrinks each estimate towards the"
      "\noverall mean by an amount set by its own sample size: small hospitals a lot, large ones much less."
      "\nThis 'borrowing strength' is the James-Stein effect, and it is why multilevel models are standard for"
      "\ngrouped data (schools, hospitals, regions, A/B tests across many segments). The between-group spread tau"
      "\nis itself uncertain with 40 noisy groups, and when it is underestimated the intervals undercover a little.")
```

4. Hamiltonian Monte Carlo written by hand on Neal's funnel: centred against non-centred parameterisations, with R-hat, effective sample size and divergences as diagnostics.
```python
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
from scipy.stats import norm

# Hamiltonian Monte Carlo, written by hand, on Neal's funnel: the shape of the posterior of a hierarchical
# model when groups have little data. v is the log-variance of the group effects x_1..x_9:
#   v ~ Normal(0, 3^2),   x_i | v ~ Normal(0, e^v)
# Centred parameterisation samples (v, x); the non-centred one samples (v, z) with x = e^(v/2) z, z ~ N(0, 1).
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
D, chains, iters, burn = 9, 4, 2000, 500

def centred(q):                                     # q[:, 0] = v, q[:, 1:] = x; returns log p and its gradient
    v, x = q[:, 0], q[:, 1:]
    ss = (x ** 2).sum(1)
    logp = -v ** 2 / 18 - ss / (2 * np.exp(v)) - D * v / 2
    g = np.column_stack([-v / 9 + ss / (2 * np.exp(v)) - D / 2, -x / np.exp(v)[:, None]])
    return logp, g
def non_centred(q):                                 # q[:, 0] = v, q[:, 1:] = z: independent normals
    return -q[:, 0] ** 2 / 18 - (q[:, 1:] ** 2).sum(1) / 2, np.column_stack([-q[:, 0] / 9, -q[:, 1:]])

def hmc(target, eps=0.2, L=15):
    q = rng.normal(0, 1, (chains, D + 1))
    out, n_div = [], 0
    lp, g = target(q)
    for t in range(iters):
        p = rng.normal(0, 1, q.shape)
        q_new, p_new, g_new = q.copy(), p + 0.5 * eps * g, None
        with np.errstate(all="ignore"):
            for l in range(L):                      # leapfrog integration of Hamiltonian dynamics
                q_new = q_new + eps * p_new
                lp_new, g_new = target(q_new)
                if l < L - 1: p_new = p_new + eps * g_new
            p_new = p_new + 0.5 * eps * g_new
            H_old = -lp + 0.5 * (p ** 2).sum(1)
            H_new = -lp_new + 0.5 * (p_new ** 2).sum(1)
            dH = np.nan_to_num(H_new - H_old, nan=np.inf)
        div = dH > 100                              # energy blew up: the integrator diverged
        accept = (rng.random(chains) < np.exp(-np.clip(dH, -50, 50))) & ~div
        q[accept], lp[accept], g[accept] = q_new[accept], lp_new[accept], g_new[accept]
        if t >= burn:
            out.append(q[:, 0].copy()); n_div += div.sum()
    return np.array(out).T, n_div                   # chains x draws of v

def split_rhat(x):
    """Gelman-Rubin R-hat on split chains: near 1.00 when chains agree; above ~1.01 signals a problem."""
    h = x.shape[1] // 2
    s = np.vstack([x[:, :h], x[:, h:2 * h]])
    W = s.var(1, ddof=1).mean(); B = h * s.mean(1).var(ddof=1)
    return np.sqrt(((h - 1) / h * W + B / h) / W)
def ess(x):
    """Effective sample size: draws / (1 + 2 * sum of autocorrelations), summing pairs until they turn negative."""
    m, n = x.shape
    xc = x - x.mean()
    var = np.mean(xc ** 2)
    acf = [1.0] + [np.mean(xc[:, :n - k] * xc[:, k:]) / var for k in range(1, min(n, 500))]
    s, k = 0.0, 1
    while k + 1 < len(acf) and acf[k] + acf[k + 1] > 0:
        s += acf[k] + acf[k + 1]; k += 2
    return m * n / (1 + 2 * s)

true_p = norm.cdf(-3, 0, 3)
print(f"truth: v ~ N(0, 9), so P(v < -3) = {true_p:.1%} and the sd of v is 3.0\n")
print(f"{'parameterisation':16s} {'P(v < -3)':>10s} {'sd of v':>8s} {'R-hat':>6s} {'ESS of v':>9s} {'divergences':>12s}")
results = {}
for name, target in [("centred", centred), ("non-centred", non_centred)]:
    v, n_div = hmc(target)
    results[name] = v
    print(f"{name:16s} {np.mean(v < -3):10.1%} {v.std():8.2f} {split_rhat(v):6.2f} {ess(v):9.0f} {n_div:12d}")

fig, ax = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(11, 3.5))
for name, c in [("centred", "#e74c3c"), ("non-centred", "#3498db")]:
    ax[0].plot(results[name][0][:600], color=c, lw=0.7, label=name)
    ax[1].hist(results[name].ravel(), bins=60, density=True, alpha=0.5, color=c, label=name)
grid = np.linspace(-10, 10, 200)
ax[1].plot(grid, norm.pdf(grid, 0, 3), "k", label="true N(0, 9)")
ax[0].set_title("trace of v (one chain)"); ax[0].legend()
ax[1].set_title("samples of v"); ax[1].legend()
plt.tight_layout(); plt.show()

print("\nIn the centred form the posterior is a funnel: wide at the top, extremely narrow in the 'neck' where v"
      "\nis small. A step size that suits the wide part is far too big for the neck, so the sampler diverges or"
      "\nrejects there and rarely visits it: it reports far too few small values of v, a biased answer that"
      "\nlooks plausible. Divergences, R-hat and a low effective sample size are the warning"
      "\nsigns. The non-centred form describes the same model, but the sampler sees independent normals.")
```

5. The Bayesian workflow on count data: Poisson and negative binomial models, posterior predictive checks, and model comparison by cross-validated log predictive density.
```python
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
from scipy.stats import nbinom, poisson, gamma
from scipy.special import logsumexp

# The Bayesian workflow on count data: fit a model, check it against the data with posterior predictive
# checks, and compare models by how well they predict held-out data.
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
y = rng.negative_binomial(2, 2 / (2 + 4.0), 150)        # daily counts, mean 4, more spread than Poisson
print(f"data: mean {y.mean():.2f}, variance {y.var():.2f}, zeros {np.mean(y == 0):.1%}")

# Model 1: Poisson(lambda) with a Gamma(1, 0.1) prior -> the posterior is Gamma (conjugate)
def poisson_posterior(y, n_draws=4000):
    return gamma.rvs(1 + y.sum(), scale=1 / (0.1 + len(y)), size=n_draws, random_state=rng)

# Model 2: negative binomial with mean mu and dispersion r (variance mu + mu^2 / r), posterior on a grid
mu_grid, r_grid = np.geomspace(1, 15, 120), np.geomspace(0.2, 50, 120)
M, R = np.meshgrid(mu_grid, r_grid, indexing="ij")
def nb_logpmf(y, mu, r): return nbinom.logpmf(y, r, r / (r + mu))
def nb_posterior(y, n_draws=4000):
    loglik = nb_logpmf(y[:, None, None], M[None], R[None]).sum(0)     # flat prior on log mu and log r
    w = np.exp(loglik - loglik.max()); w /= w.sum()
    idx = rng.choice(w.size, n_draws, p=w.ravel())
    return M.ravel()[idx], R.ravel()[idx]

lam = poisson_posterior(y)
mu, r = nb_posterior(y)
print(f"Poisson posterior: lambda = {lam.mean():.2f}")
print(f"Negative binomial posterior: mu = {mu.mean():.2f}, r = {np.median(r):.2f} (small r = strong overdispersion)")

# Posterior predictive checks: simulate replicated datasets from each fitted model and compare statistics
reps = 1000
y_pois = rng.poisson(lam[:reps, None], (reps, len(y)))
y_nb = rng.negative_binomial(r[:reps, None], r[:reps, None] / (r[:reps, None] + mu[:reps, None]), (reps, len(y)))
print(f"\n{'statistic':18s} {'observed':>9s} {'Poisson: replicated (p-value)':>31s} {'NegBin: replicated (p-value)':>30s}")
for name, f in [("variance", lambda a: a.var(-1)), ("share of zeros", lambda a: np.mean(a == 0, -1)),
                ("maximum", lambda a: a.max(-1)), ("mean", lambda a: a.mean(-1))]:
    obs, sp, sn = f(y), f(y_pois), f(y_nb)
    pp = lambda s: min(1.0, 2 * min(np.mean(s >= obs), np.mean(s <= obs)))   # two-sided predictive p-value
    print(f"{name:18s} {obs:9.2f} {np.median(sp):19.2f} ({pp(sp):.3f}) {np.median(sn):18.2f} ({pp(sn):.3f})")

# Model comparison: expected log predictive density on held-out data, by 10-fold cross-validation
folds = np.array_split(rng.permutation(len(y)), 10)
elpd = {"Poisson": 0.0, "negative binomial": 0.0}
for test in folds:
    train = np.setdiff1d(np.arange(len(y)), test)
    l = poisson_posterior(y[train], 2000)
    m_, r_ = nb_posterior(y[train], 2000)
    for yi in y[test]:                                   # log of the posterior-averaged predictive probability
        elpd["Poisson"] += logsumexp(poisson.logpmf(yi, l)) - np.log(len(l))
        elpd["negative binomial"] += logsumexp(nb_logpmf(yi, m_, r_)) - np.log(len(m_))
print(f"\nheld-out log predictive density (higher is better): "
      + ", ".join(f"{k} {v:.1f}" for k, v in elpd.items()))
print(f"difference: {elpd['negative binomial'] - elpd['Poisson']:.1f} nats over {len(y)} held-out points")

fig, ax = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(11, 3.6))
ax[0].hist(y_pois.var(1), bins=30, alpha=0.6, label="Poisson replicates", color="#e74c3c")
ax[0].hist(y_nb.var(1), bins=30, alpha=0.6, label="negative binomial replicates", color="#3498db")
ax[0].axvline(y.var(), color="k", lw=2, label="observed"); ax[0].set_title("posterior predictive check: variance")
ax[0].legend()
k = np.arange(0, 25)
ax[1].bar(k, np.bincount(y, minlength=25)[:25] / len(y), color="#ccc", label="data")
ax[1].plot(k, poisson.pmf(k, lam.mean()), "o-", color="#e74c3c", label="Poisson")
ax[1].plot(k, np.exp(nb_logpmf(k, mu.mean(), np.median(r))), "s-", color="#3498db", label="negative binomial")
ax[1].legend(); ax[1].set_title("fitted distributions")
plt.tight_layout(); plt.show()

print("\nBoth models get the mean right, so checking the mean alone would pass both. The Poisson model assumes"
      "\nvariance equals the mean; its replicated datasets never show the observed spread or the many zeros, so"
      "\nthe check rejects it. The negative binomial reproduces every statistic and predicts held-out days far"
      "\nbetter. Checks reveal how a model fails; held-out predictive density says which model to prefer.")
```
