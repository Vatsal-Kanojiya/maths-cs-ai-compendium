# Interview Preparation

*ML engineer interviews test four things: whether you understand the fundamentals, whether you can design a system that works in production, whether you can write solid code under time pressure, and whether you have actually shipped something. This file maps the compendium to those questions, gives a framework and two worked walkthroughs for ML system design, a template for take-home exercises, and a way to talk about the project you built in the milestones*

- Typical loops for ML engineer roles combine some of: a coding round (data structures and algorithms, chapter 14, or practical data manipulation in pandas or SQL), an ML fundamentals round, an ML system design round, a take-home exercise or pair-programming session, and a project deep dive with behavioural questions. Backend engineers moving into ML usually do well on coding and system design and need the most preparation on fundamentals and on explaining modelling decisions.

## A Framework for ML System Design

- System design questions are open on purpose ("design a system to detect fraudulent payments"). Interviewers look for structure, sensible trade-offs and awareness of what breaks in production, more than for one right answer. Work through these steps, saying your assumptions out loud:
    1. **Clarify the goal.** Who uses the output and what decision does it drive? What is the business metric (losses prevented, time saved, revenue)? What are the constraints: latency, scale, cost, regulation, explainability?
    2. **Frame the ML problem.** Classification, regression, ranking, forecasting? What exactly is predicted, for which unit, at what moment? Is ML needed at all, or would rules work first?
    3. **Data and labels.** Where do labels come from, how delayed and how noisy are they? What is available at prediction time (point-in-time correctness)? Volume, freshness, privacy.
    4. **Offline metric.** One that tracks the business metric: precision at a fixed review capacity, recall at a tolerable false-positive rate, NDCG for ranking, calibrated probabilities when downstream code uses them.
    5. **Baseline, then model.** Start with a heuristic or a simple model; justify any added complexity by what it buys.
    6. **Features.** Which signals, computed where (batch or real time), stored how (feature store), and how training-serving skew is prevented.
    7. **Serving.** Batch predictions or an online service; latency budget; throughput; fallbacks when the model or a feature is unavailable.
    8. **Evaluation beyond offline.** Shadow mode, canary, A/B test with a guardrail metric, how long and on what traffic.
    9. **Monitoring and retraining.** Input drift, prediction drift, delayed-label performance, alerting, retraining trigger and champion-challenger promotion.
    10. **Risks.** Fairness, feedback loops (the model changes the data it later learns from), adversaries, privacy, failure modes and their cost.

- Spend most of the time on the steps where the specific problem is hard; for fraud that is labels and adversaries, for recommendations it is candidate generation and feedback loops. Chapter 18's design examples (recommendation, search, ads, fraud) cover four classic questions in depth; the two walkthroughs below are different on purpose.

### Walkthrough 1: Routing Customer Support Tickets

- **Goal.** A software company receives 20,000 support tickets a day and wants each sent to the right team (billing, technical, account, sales) without manual triage. Metric: share of tickets reaching the right team first time, and time to first response. Constraint: a wrong route costs a few hours, not money lost, so moderate errors are acceptable if the model knows when it is unsure.
- **Framing.** Multi-class text classification on the ticket subject and body, plus metadata (product, customer plan). Predict at creation time.
- **Labels.** The team that finally resolved each historical ticket. They are noisy: some tickets were rerouted for reasons unrelated to content. Check agreement on a sample of tickets labelled by two people to estimate the ceiling.
- **Baseline and model.** Keyword rules from the support leads, then TF-IDF with logistic regression, then a fine-tuned small transformer (milestone 8 is exactly this). With tens of thousands of labelled tickets, fine-tuning a small model usually beats prompting a large one on cost and latency; an LLM is a strong option for zero-shot classification while labels are scarce.
- **Serving.** Online, triggered by ticket creation; a few hundred milliseconds is fine, so a quantised model on CPU suffices. Route automatically only when the top probability exceeds a threshold chosen so that, say, 95% of auto-routed tickets are correct; send the rest to a human triage queue. This **human-in-the-loop** design turns calibration into a business lever.
- **Evaluation.** Offline accuracy and the accuracy-coverage curve at different thresholds; then a shadow period comparing model routes with human routes; then a gradual rollout measuring reroutes and response time.
- **Monitoring.** New products and outages change the vocabulary suddenly; track the share of tickets below the confidence threshold and the reroute rate as early signals. Retrain monthly, or when the low-confidence share rises.
- **Risks.** Tickets contain personal data (mask it before logging); urgent issues (security, outages) need rules that bypass the model.

### Walkthrough 2: Predicting Delivery Times

- **Goal.** A food delivery app shows an estimated delivery time at checkout. Metric: customer satisfaction and cancellations; underestimates hurt more than overestimates. Constraints: under 100 ms latency, millions of requests a day.
- **Framing.** Regression of minutes from order to delivery, predicted at checkout. Because the costs are asymmetric, predict a quantile (for example the 70th percentile) rather than the mean, with a quantile (pinball) loss.
- **Data and labels.** Historical orders with actual delivery times; labels arrive within an hour, so feedback is fast. Features must be point-in-time correct: restaurant load **at order time**, not averaged over the day.
- **Features.** Static (restaurant, distance, cuisine), near-real-time (orders in the restaurant's queue, couriers nearby, current traffic and weather) from a streaming feature store, and historical aggregates (this restaurant's typical preparation time by hour). Training-serving skew is the main risk: the streaming features must be computed the same way offline.
- **Model.** Gradient boosting with quantile loss is a strong default for this tabular problem. Decompose if useful: preparation time plus travel time, each with its own model and features.
- **Serving.** An online service fed by the feature store; a fallback (historical median for the restaurant and distance) when real-time features are missing.
- **Evaluation.** Offline: pinball loss and the share of orders arriving after the estimate, sliced by city, hour and distance. Online: an A/B test with cancellations and satisfaction as the primary metrics and actual lateness as a guardrail.
- **Monitoring and feedback loops.** Weather, holidays and promotions shift the distribution quickly; because labels arrive within an hour, monitor actual error directly. Note the feedback loop: a long estimate can cause cancellations, so the observed orders are not a random sample of all requests.

## Take-Home Exercises

- A take-home is a small, realistic task (here is a dataset, build a model and explain it) with a time limit. Reviewers judge judgement and communication more than the final score. A structure that works:

```
take-home/
├── README.md          # the most important file: read in five minutes
├── notebooks/
│   └── 01-exploration.ipynb
├── src/
│   ├── data.py        # loading and cleaning, as functions
│   ├── features.py
│   └── train.py       # one command reproduces the result
├── tests/
│   └── test_data.py   # a few tests on the cleaning logic
└── requirements.txt   # pinned versions
```

- The README should answer, in this order: the problem as you understood it and the assumptions you made; how to run everything in one or two commands; the result, with a baseline for comparison and an honest uncertainty (cross-validated mean and spread); the key decisions and why (metric, validation scheme, model choice); what you found in the data, including problems; what you would do next with more time; and how long you spent.

- What reviewers look for: a baseline before anything complex; validation that matches how the model would be used (no leakage, a time-based split for temporal data); a metric justified by the problem; readable, reproducible code; and the judgement to stop. Five well-explained decisions beat fifty untested ideas.

- Common mistakes: tuning on the test set; reporting only accuracy on imbalanced data; a notebook that runs only in the author's order; no README; unexplained deep learning on a small tabular dataset; ignoring the time limit.

## Talking About Your Project

- The milestones produce exactly what a project deep dive asks about. Keep a short log in your project README as you go: what each milestone changed, why, and the before and after numbers. Then prepare a two-minute story using that log:
    - **The problem and the data**, and one surprising thing you found (missingness that carried signal, a proxy for a sensitive attribute).
    - **A decision you made and its alternative**, for example gradient boosting over logistic regression, with the cross-validated evidence and the paired test.
    - **Something that went wrong and how you found it**: a skew test that failed, a drift alert, a feature that did not help.
    - **How it runs**: the service, the tests in CI, the monitoring and the promotion rule.
    - **What you would do next.**

- Interviewers follow up on whatever you mention, so mention only what you can explain in depth. Numbers, trade-offs and failures you fixed are more convincing than a list of tools.

## Checklist

- Before applying:
    - The tabular project in a public repository with a clear README, CI passing, and the milestone log.
    - The text project's model on the Hugging Face Hub, with a model card stating the data, metrics and limitations.
    - One sentence for each milestone that you can expand into five minutes.
- Before each interview:
    - Two-minute answers to the fundamentals table, practised aloud.
    - Two system designs worked through on paper using the framework, timed at 40 minutes.
    - A coding warm-up: a few problems from chapter 14 and a pandas or SQL exercise.
    - Questions for the interviewer: how models are deployed and monitored, who owns them after launch, how success is measured.
