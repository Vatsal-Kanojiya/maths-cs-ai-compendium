Warps Are Kanban Cards

Part 1 found two shop-floor laws running a CPU core. A line runs at the rate of its slowest station, and work in process equals throughput times lead time. A GPU obeys both laws with every number moved. The first now has three stations, the second counts its cards in warps, and the rack that holds the cards is the register file.

This is the middle part of Chapter 16. It covers source file 04 (GPU architecture and CUDA) and file 05 (Triton, TPUs and Pallas). Part 1 was the CPU and closed with seven calculations it said this part owes you; each is paid below, by computation, and the section says which. Part 3 takes RISC-V, Vulkan and the chapter’s end matter, and §19 says exactly what it owes.

The headline is §05. The source quotes a memory latency of about 400 cycles and a bandwidth of 3.3 TB/s. Little’s law multiplies them: about 667 KB must be on its way at every instant, which is 39.5 warp-loads in flight on each of the 132 multiprocessors. Each warp in flight is a kanban card, and the cards live in the register file. Its 65,536 registers hold 40 warps only if each thread uses 51 registers or fewer. A kernel that uses 128 gets 16 cards, and the law caps it at 40% of its memory bandwidth unless each warp keeps several loads going at once. A simulated multiprocessor lands on that bound.

The roofline moved too (§03). The ridge rises from part 1’s 2.56 FLOP/B to 20.3, so the source’s own 16×16 shared-memory tile, at 4 FLOP/B, sits on the delivery side at a fifth of the roof. No tile with one thread per output can reach the ridge: the block limit of 1,024 threads caps the tile at 32 (§08).

There is no GPU in the build container. Hardware figures are the source’s, or are marked as reported. Everything else is derived, simulated in Python or in your browser, or run on the CPU: the source’s JAX and Pallas code ran under JAX 0.11.2 on the CPU, with Pallas in its interpreter mode. Checks are tagged [Ch16p2 sNN] in verify_claims.py.

Lanes, not cores

src 04. GPU and CUDA ↗

The source sets the two designs side by side. A CPU spends its transistors on making one thread fast: large caches, branch prediction, out-of-order execution (part 1 §04). A GPU spends them on arithmetic units and runs thousands of slow threads at once. One is built to finish a single job quickly; the other to finish many jobs per second. Its table gives the CPU 4–128 cores at 3–5 GHz, 1–5 TFLOPS in FP32 and 50–100 GB/s of memory, and the GPU 1,000–20,000 cores at 1–2.5 GHz, 30–80 TFLOPS and 1–3 TB/s.

Then it counts the wrong thing. “Over 10,000 CUDA cores” against 4–128 CPU cores is called a 100–1000× advantage. A CUDA core is one floating-point lane, a spindle in part 1’s drilling head. The thing that corresponds to a CPU core is the streaming multiprocessor (SM): it has its own instruction issue, registers and on-chip memory, and on the GPU used below it drives 128 FP32 lanes. A CPU core drives lanes too: part 1’s had two FMA units of 8 lanes each, 16 in all, and an AVX-512 core has 32. Count lanes and a 128-core AVX-512 server has 4,096 against the GPU’s 16,896, a factor of 4.1; a 4-core AVX2 laptop has 64, a factor of 264. The clock pulls the other way. The source’s own table settles it: its peaks differ by 6–80× and its bandwidths by 10–60×, not the 10–30× it states ([Ch16p2 s01]).

The reference GPU used on this page

Every calculation below uses one H100-class part, built from the source’s own figures where it gives them. From the source: 3.3 TB/s of HBM (the top of its range), about 400 cycles to HBM and 200 to L2, about 6 TB/s from L2 and 20 TB/s from shared memory, 256 KB of registers and up to 228 KB of shared memory per SM, at most 255 registers per thread and 1,024 threads per block, warps of 32, and 989 TFLOP/s from its tensor cores. Reported, not in the source: 132 SMs, 16,896 FP32 lanes, a 1.98 GHz clock, 4 warp schedulers per SM, and at most 64 warps resident per SM.

Its FP32 peak is then a calculation, with a fused multiply-add counted as two operations as part 1 §06 insists: \(16{,}896 \times 1.98\ \text{GHz} \times 2 = 66.9\) TFLOP/s, inside the source’s 30–80. Counting an FMA once gives 33.5, also inside it, so the range alone cannot say which convention the source used.

The source’s memory hierarchy is part 1 §07’s staging again, with one level the CPU did not have. Sheet 13 named the vocabulary; it carries over.

!Where “latency against throughput” needs care

The source’s slogan is that a CPU is designed for latency and a GPU for throughput. Sheet 17 §13 found that “latency” is a lead time and “throughput” a rate, and Little’s law ties them: work in process = rate × lead time. A GPU’s individual memory access is slow, 400 cycles; it gets its rate by keeping a great deal of work in process. How much is the subject of §05, and the answer is a number, not a slogan.

CUDA in shop terms

src 04. GPU and CUDA ↗

CUDA is NVIDIA’s way of programming its GPUs. You write a kernel, one function that every thread runs, each on its own piece of the data. Threads come in three levels. A thread handles one element. A block of up to 1,024 threads runs on one SM, shares that SM’s fast shared memory, and can wait for all its members at a barrier. A grid is every block of one launch. Inside a block the hardware runs threads 32 at a time as a warp: one instruction, 32 lanes, part 1’s drilling head with 32 spindles.

Each thread finds its element from its position: idx = blockIdx.x * blockDim.x + threadIdx.x. It is a serial number in mixed radix, the way a part numbered “pallet 3, slot 17” on 256-slot pallets is part \(3 \times 256 + 17\); every thread gets a different number and no number is skipped ([Ch16p2 s02]). The launch vector_add<<<grid_size, block_size>>> asks for \(\lceil n/256 \rceil\) blocks, so for \(n = 10^6\) it starts 3,907 blocks, 1,000,192 threads, and the test if (idx < n) switches off the last 192. That is part 1 §05’s last stroke with its spare drills removed.

Memory is explicit. cudaMalloc reserves space in the GPU’s own memory, cudaMemcpy copies across the link between host and GPU, and the kernel works only on what has been copied over.

!The source’s first kernel prints 1.0

vector_add fills a with 1.0 and b with 2.0, adds them into d_c on the GPU, and copies the answer back with cudaMemcpy(h_c, d_a, bytes, cudaMemcpyDeviceToHost). That copies d_a, the untouched input. The program prints c[0] = 1.000000 (expected 3.0). With d_c in that call it prints 3.000000. No GPU was needed to find it; the copies were replayed on host arrays ([Ch16p2 s02]).

!A launch does not wait

The source says that by default “the CPU launches a kernel, waits for it to finish, then launches the next one.” The GPU does run one stream’s work in order, but the launch call returns to the CPU at once. The source’s own benchmarks show it: each records a start event, launches ten or a hundred kernels, records a stop event and then calls cudaEventSynchronize(stop), five times across the file, to wait for work the CPU would otherwise have run ahead of. A blocking cudaMemcpy waits too, which is why task 1’s timer (§04) measures what it does.

The barrier. __syncthreads() stops every thread of a block until all of them have reached it. A transfer line with a rotary table indexes only when every station has finished its operation, so the index waits for the slowest station; the barrier waits for the slowest thread in the same way. The tiled kernel of §08 calls it twice per step: once so that nobody computes on a kit still being stocked, once so that nobody restocks a kit still in use.

✓Same structure: the indexing transfer line

The time between indexes is the longest station time, exactly as the time through a barrier is the longest thread time. Where it breaks: a transfer line indexes every cycle by design, while a kernel puts a barrier only where threads must exchange data, and threads in other blocks never wait for it. A whole-grid barrier is a different and much costlier thing; the usual one is the end of the kernel.

The GPU roofline: the same line, every number moved

src 04. GPU and CUDA ↗

Debt 1 of 7. Part 1 §06 showed that the roofline is a two-station serial line: a delivery station that brings bytes at the memory bandwidth BW, a machining station that does FLOPs at the peak rate, and a product mix \(I\), the FLOPs a kernel does per byte it moves. The line runs at the slower station, \(\min(\text{Peak}, \text{BW}\times I)\), and balances at the ridge \(I^* = \text{Peak}/\text{BW}\). On the reference GPU of §01:

$$\begin{aligned} \text{Peak} &= 16{,}896 \times 1.98\ \text{GHz} \times 2 = 66.9\ \text{TFLOP/s}, \\ I^{*} &= \frac{66.9\ \text{TFLOP/s}}{3.3\ \text{TB/s}} = 20.3\ \text{FLOP/B}. \end{aligned}$$

Part 1’s single core had a ridge of 2.56. Its eight cores sharing 50 GB/s had 20.48. The GPU is balanced almost exactly like that eight-core CPU, at 523 times its machining rate and 66 times its delivery rate. So a kernel that was waiting on memory speeds up 66× on the GPU, and one that was waiting on arithmetic speeds up 523×. Here are part 1’s operations placed on the new roof:

The roofline is the same formula; only the two station rates changed, and the ridge moved from 2.56 to 20.3 with them. Every tiled matmul with \(2.56 \le b/4 < 20.3\), every tile edge from 11 to 81, changes station: machining-bound on part 1’s core, delivery-bound here. Move to the tensor cores and the machining station jumps to 989 TFLOP/s, the ridge to 300 FLOP/B, and the convolution changes station as well: 46.6% of that roof with 4-byte operands, 93% with 2-byte ones, still just short of the ridge ([Ch16p2 s03]).

!Where it breaks

The \(\min\) assumes the stations overlap. Part 1’s core overlapped them by out-of-order execution; a GPU does it by switching to another warp while one waits, and §05 counts how many warps that takes. A GPU also has two machining stations, the FP32 lanes and the tensor cores, and which one serves a kernel depends on its instructions and number format, not on the kernel’s intensity. And the naive matmul’s 0.25 FLOP/B assumes every operand comes from HBM. On real hardware the L1 and L2 caches catch part of its re-reads (reported), so 0.25 is the floor for that kernel, not its measured intensity.

The receiving dock: PCIe as a third station

src 04. GPU and CUDA ↗

Debt 7 of 7. Before the stores can deliver anything, the material has to come in at the dock. A GPU’s data starts in the host’s memory and crosses the PCIe link. Part 1 §09 derived its rate: 16 GT/s per lane (reported), 128b/130b coding, sixteen lanes, \(31.5\ \text{GB/s}\) in each direction. The HBM behind it is 104.7 times faster. With the dock in front, the line has three stations in series:

$$\text{attainable} = \min\!\left(\text{Peak},\ \ \text{BW}_{\text{HBM}}\, I,\ \ \text{BW}_{\text{PCIe}}\, I_{\text{PCIe}}\right),$$

where \(I_{\text{PCIe}}\) is the FLOPs done per byte that crosses the link. A kernel that reads its input from HBM once per use, with the data copied over once per use, has \(I_{\text{PCIe}} = I\), and then the dock binds whenever it is the slowest of the three: always, for anything that is not already compute-bound at more than \(66.9/0.0315 = 2{,}124\) FLOP per byte.

!Task 1 measures the dock, not the GPU

The ReLU task times the copy in, the kernel and the copy out together, then prints “Bandwidth” as \(2 \times\) bytes / time. The array is \(2^{24}\) floats, 64 MiB. Each copy takes \(67.1\ \text{MB} / 31.5\ \text{GB/s} = 2.13\) ms; the kernel moves 134 MB inside the GPU at 3.3 TB/s in 40.7 µs. The copies are 99.1% of the timed interval, and the line prints 31.2 GB/s, the link’s own rate, a hundredth of the memory it claims to describe ([Ch16p2 s04]). The source says the task “teaches the host↔device transfer bottleneck”; it does, provided you read the printed number as the dock’s. With ordinary malloc memory on the host the copies are usually slower still (reported).

For any elementwise kernel the ratio is fixed: the same bytes cross the dock and the stores, so the copy takes 104.7 times as long as the kernel, whatever the size. A matrix product does better, because its work grows as \(N^3\) while its operands grow as \(N^2\). With \(A\) and \(B\) sent over and \(C\) sent back, in float32:

$$\frac{2N^{3}}{\text{Peak}} = \frac{12N^{2}}{\text{BW}_{\text{PCIe}}} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad N^{*} = \frac{6\,\text{Peak}}{\text{BW}_{\text{PCIe}}} = 12{,}741 .$$

Below about 12,700 the dock binds even at the FP32 roof. The source’s task 2 multiplies 1024×1024 matrices: sent across each time, the link would take 0.399 ms against 0.032 ms of arithmetic, 12.4 times longer. Its benchmark copies once and runs the kernel ten times, which is what the GPU wants: material that arrives once and is used many times.

✓Identity: a receiving dock ahead of the stores, and streams as line overlap

A third station in series enters the same \(\min\). The source’s cure is streams: split the work into chunks and let the copy of chunk 2 overlap the kernel on chunk 1. That is Sheet 13 part 1 §13’s pipeline. With \(c\) chunks the time is one chunk’s trip through every stage plus \(c-1\) more of the longest stage. For the ReLU task, with copies in both directions running at once (the link is full duplex; a GPU needs a copy engine for each direction, reported), 8 chunks take 2.40 ms against 4.30 ms in sequence, 1.79×. No number of chunks beats 2.02×, because one direction alone needs 2.13 ms. Overlap hides the shorter stations; it cannot shorten the longest.

!Where it breaks

The dock is a station only for material that crosses it per use. A model’s weights are copied once and then read from HBM for every step; activations are made and consumed on the GPU. In training the dock carries only each new batch, so it binds for small models and cheap steps and almost never otherwise. Real overlap also needs page-locked host memory, which cudaMemcpyAsync from ordinary memory does not get (reported). PCIe 5.0 doubles the rate (reported); links between GPUs, which the source names (NVLink), are further docks this page does not model.

Warps are kanban cards

src 04. GPU and CUDA ↗

Debt 2 of 7, and the headline. Part 1 §08 derived the best-case bound for a closed line with \(w\) jobs in it, raw process time \(T_0\) and bottleneck rate \(r_b\):

$$\text{TH}(w) \le \min\!\left(\frac{w}{T_0},\ r_b\right), \qquad w_0 = r_b\,T_0 .$$

It ran a CPU core twice: eight FMA chains kept two units busy, and about fifteen cache misses in flight saturated one core’s memory. A GPU’s memory loop is the same closed line, much larger. The bottleneck is HBM at 3.3 TB/s. At 1.98 GHz that is 1,666.7 bytes per cycle for the chip, 12.63 per SM. The raw process time is the source’s 400-cycle latency. So

$$\begin{aligned} w_0 &= r_b\,T_0 = 12.63\ \tfrac{\text{B}}{\text{cycle}} \times 400\ \text{cycles} = 5{,}051\ \text{B per SM} \\ &= 39.5\ \text{warp-loads of 128 B, on each of 132 SMs.} \end{aligned}$$

Chip-wide that is 666,667 bytes on their way at every instant. Part 1’s core needed 14.6 lines of 64 bytes, 934 bytes; one SM needs 5.4 times that, and there are 132 of them. The source’s L2 row gives a similar figure at its level, 6 TB/s × 200 cycles = 606,061 bytes ([Ch16p2 s05]).

Who holds those 39.5 loads? A warp that has issued a load and is waiting for it. A coalesced load brings 128 bytes, one float for each of the warp’s 32 threads (§07), so one waiting warp is one card in the loop, and the SM needs about 40 of them circulating. It can hold at most 64 warps (reported), so a kernel with one load in flight per warp needs 62% occupancy. The source’s profiling rule, that occupancy below 50% means “too few threads to hide memory latency”, is this calculation done roughly, and it depends on the kernel: with four loads in flight per warp, ten warps do it.

The register file is the card rack

Here the GPU does something no CPU does. A warp stays resident with all its registers while it waits, so switching to another warp costs nothing, and the scheduler can pick a ready warp every cycle. The price is that every resident warp’s registers must fit in the SM’s register file at once. The source gives 256 KB per SM, which is 65,536 registers of 4 bytes. A warp is 32 threads, so with \(r\) registers per thread

$$\text{warps resident} \le \min\!\left(64,\ \left\lfloor \frac{65{,}536}{32\,r} \right\rfloor\right).$$

!Where the cards picture breaks

Nobody chooses the card count. A CONWIP planner sets it. Here it is whatever the register file and shared memory leave room for, after the compiler has decided how many registers each thread needs, and it moves in whole blocks: a block of 256 threads is 8 warps admitted together or not at all. A card can carry several orders. A warp that issues four independent loads before waiting is four cards in one; at 16 warps that alone lifts the simulated SM from 40% to 100%. That is how register-hungry kernels survive (§08). The switching is done by hardware, every cycle. On part 1’s CPU the cards were accumulators, fixed by the compiler before the program ran. And real latency varies. Let it range uniformly over 200–600 cycles and the simulated SM falls to 94.4% of the bound at 40 warps, against 98.8% at 16 and the full bound at 64. Variability costs most near the knee, as on any real line.

The same law sizes a CPU prefetch, which file 04 also covers. A software prefetch must be issued far enough ahead that the data arrives as it is needed: distance = consumption rate × latency. Part 1 measured one core streaming about 10 GB/s with a 210 ns trip to memory, which gives 2,100 bytes, about 525 floats ahead. The source prefetches 64 floats ahead, 256 bytes, which covers 25.6 ns: enough for a cache level, not for memory. Its own advice to tune the distance “to match the memory latency and loop iteration time” is Little’s law, unnamed.

Divergence: thirty-two spindles, one feed

src 04. GPU and CUDA ↗

Debt 4 of 7. Part 1 §05 drew SIMD as a multi-spindle drilling head: one feed drives every spindle, so holes of two depths in one group take two strokes, each with the other kind’s drills removed. A warp is a head with 32 spindles. When the threads of one warp disagree at an if, the warp runs one side with the disagreeing lanes switched off, then the other side with the rest switched off. The source calls this warp divergence. Let \(p\) of the 32 lanes take path A, which costs \(a\), and the rest take path B, which costs \(b\). The warp’s time is exact:

$$t_{\text{warp}} = a\,[\,p > 0\,] + b\,[\,p < 32\,], \qquad \text{useful share} = \frac{p\,a + (32-p)\,b}{32\;t_{\text{warp}}},$$

where a bracket is 1 when its condition holds and 0 otherwise. The source’s example sends even threads one way and odd threads the other. Every warp holds both, so every warp pays \(a + b\), and with equal paths half the lane-slots do work: “this halves performance”, as it says. The same 50/50 split laid out differently costs nothing. Send the first half of \(2^{24}\) threads one way and the second half the other: the boundary falls between warps, and 0 of 524,288 warps diverge, against all 524,288 for even and odd ([Ch16p2 s06]). What a branch costs depends on how its condition is laid out across the lanes, not on how often it is true.

A rare branch is a sampling plan with c = 0

Now let a branch be taken by a fraction \(q\) of threads, scattered at random, as an error check or a clipped value would be. A warp must run path A if any one of its 32 lanes needs it:

$$P(\text{warp runs A}) = 1 - (1-q)^{32}.$$

That is the rejection probability of Sheet 15 §16’s single sampling plan with \(n = 32\) and acceptance number \(c = 0\): a lot is rejected if the sample holds even one defective, and a warp runs the branch if it holds even one lane that wants it. Some values: \(q = 0.001\) gives 3.1%, \(q = 0.01\) gives 27.5%, \(q = 0.05\) gives 80.6%, \(q = 0.1\) gives 96.6%. A branch taken by one thread in a hundred is run by more than a quarter of all warps; 400,000 simulated warps gave 27.4%. If the rare path costs 100 and the common one 10, a warp takes 37.5 on average, against a per-lane average of 10.9: 3.44 times the work the threads actually need.

Loops diverge the same way. A loop whose trip count differs by lane runs, for the whole warp, as many times as its longest lane needs. A stroke lasts as long as its deepest hole. With trip counts 1 to 32 across the lanes, the warp makes 32 trips and 51.6% of lane-trips do work. Nest five two-way branches on the bits of the thread number and the 32 lanes take 32 different paths: one lane in 32 works at a time.

✓Same structure, exact count: the drilling head with 32 spindles

Part 1’s stroke count carries over unchanged: a group pays for every branch any member takes. Two pieces of your QC and machining training compute it exactly. The chance that a warp pays for a rare branch is the c = 0 plan’s rejection probability with a sample of 32, and a loop costs what its deepest hole costs. Neither is an analogy; both are the same expression.

What SIMT adds beyond a CPU mask. On a CPU, part 1’s masked code is written for the whole register: compute both sides, blend with a mask. On a GPU you write ordinary code for one thread, and the hardware forms the masks, runs each side and rejoins the lanes after the branch. That is the “single instruction, multiple threads” of the source. It also lets every lane run a loop of its own length, and load from an address of its own, which §07 prices. Since the Volta generation each thread also keeps its own place in the program, so the hardware may interleave the two sides of a branch (reported). The lanes still share one feed: interleaving changes the order of the strokes, not their number.

!Where the drilling head breaks

A head’s spindles sit at a fixed pitch; a warp’s lanes can each point anywhere in memory, and the cost of a scattered request is counted in pallets (§07), not strokes. A head waiting for its next part stands idle; a waiting warp is swapped for another at no cost (§05). And part 1 said a head has no path from one spindle to the next. A warp has one: a shuffle instruction passes values between lanes, so part 1’s butterfly sum takes five shuffles across 32 lanes. The head picture covers the arithmetic and the branch count; it does not cover addressing, switching or exchange.

!Task 3 should print a speedup of about 1.0

The divergence task times an even/odd kernel against a branchless one. Each launch reads and writes \(2^{24}\) floats: 134 MB, 40.7 µs at 3.3 TB/s. Its arithmetic is one multiply-add per element, 0.50 µs at the FP32 roof. Doubling the arithmetic for divergence, and multiplying it by ten for overhead, still leaves it under the memory time: the kernel sits at 0.25 FLOP/B, far left of the ridge, and divergence spends issue slots the line was not short of. The prediction is a printed speedup near 1.0, not 2. A short if/else like this is also often compiled into predicated instructions with no branch at all (reported). One more thing the run would do: the even elements double each launch from zero, \(x_k = 2^k - 1\), and overflow to infinity at launch 128, the 28th launch of the branchless kernel. It does not change the timing; it is still worth a glance at the data a benchmark leaves behind ([Ch16p2 s06]).

The kit in shared memory

src 04. GPU and CUDA ↗

Debt 3 of 7. Part 1 §07 showed that tiling a matrix product is kitting: stage a kit at the machine so that many operations happen per trip to the stores. A \(b \times b\) tile does \(2b^3\) FLOPs for \(2b^2\) floats fetched, so its intensity is \(b/4\) FLOP per byte, a volume over a surface. On the CPU the bin was a cache that chose for itself what to evict. The source’s CUDA kernel stocks the bin by hand. Each block of \(16 \times 16\) threads copies one 16×16 tile of \(A\) and one of \(B\) into __shared__ arrays, waits at __syncthreads(), does 16 multiply-adds per thread from the bin, waits again, and moves along \(k\).

The count is exact. For the source’s task, \(N = 1024\), the tiled kernel loads \(2N^3/16 = 134{,}217{,}728\) floats from global memory and the naive kernel \(2N^3 = 2{,}147{,}483{,}648\): the ratio is 16, the tile size, as the source says. Run part 1’s own lab case, a 32×32 product in 8×8 tiles, through a planned bin and it fetches 8,192 words plus 1,024 written, 9,216, exactly the formula \(n^2 + 2n^3/b\), with a bin of two tiles, 128 words. Part 1 found that an LRU cache needed 512 words of room to match that figure; at the bare kit size it fetched 12,240. A planned bin does not need the slack ([Ch16p2 s08]).

Now check the kit against the GPU’s ridge. The source’s tile gives \(16/4 = 4\) FLOP/B against a ridge of 20.3, so the kernel is delivery-bound at 13.2 TFLOP/s, a fifth of the roof (§03). To clear the ridge needs \(b \ge 4 \times 20.3\), so \(b \ge 82\). The source’s design gives each thread one output, so an 82-tile needs 6,724 threads in a block, and a block may hold 1,024. The largest legal tile is 32×32, at 8 FLOP/B and 39.5% of the roof. No tile with one thread per output can reach the ridge.

The way out is to give each thread several outputs. A block of 256 threads computing a 128×128 tile, 64 outputs per thread, moves \(B_K(B_M + B_N)\) elements per step of depth \(B_K\) for \(2B_MB_NB_K\) FLOPs:

$$I = \frac{2\,B_M B_N B_K}{s\,B_K\,(B_M+B_N)} = \frac{2\,B_M B_N}{s\,(B_M+B_N)} = \frac{2 \times 128 \times 128}{4 \times 256} = 32\ \text{FLOP/B},$$

with \(s\) bytes per element; a square tile with \(s = 4\) gives back \(b/4\). At 32 FLOP/B the block tile clears the FP32 ridge. Its kit, with \(B_K = 32\), is \((128 \times 32 + 32 \times 128) \times 4\) bytes = 32 KiB, well inside the source’s 228 KB. The three tile shapes in the source’s Triton autotune list, with 2-byte elements, reach 64, 51.2 and 51.2 FLOP/B. All three clear the FP32 ridge; none comes near the tensor-core ridge of 300. What closes that gap is that neighbouring blocks read the same tiles and the L2 cache serves the repeats, so HBM sees far less than each block asks for. This page does not model the L2.

✓Same structure: kitting with a planned bin, and the two-bin system

Shared memory is a line-side bin the planner stocks, so part 1’s complaint about LRU, that nobody chooses what leaves the bin, is answered: the count is exact at the kit’s own size. The next step is double buffering: two kits, one being used while the other is refilled. That is the two-bin system, and its rule is Little’s law: a bin must last at least as long as its refill takes. One SM does 256 FLOPs per cycle, so a 16-tile step (8,192 FLOPs) lasts 32 cycles against a 400-cycle refill: 12.5 kits must be in flight, which only many resident blocks can supply. A 128×128×32 step lasts 4,096 cycles, so two bins are plenty.

!Where kitting breaks, and where §05 comes back

A kit is consumed; a tile is reused by every thread in the block, which is where \(b/4\) comes from. The two barriers per step also stop the whole cell, the transfer-line index of §02, unless another block is resident to use the time. And the big tile has a price that §05 already named: 64 outputs per thread need at least 64 accumulator registers, so the rack holds 32 warps at best and about 16 in practice. Too few cards for one load each. Fast kernels therefore issue their next kit’s loads early and keep several in flight per warp: the double buffer is how a register-hungry kernel buys back its cards.

Debt 5 of 7, first half. Part 1 §§09–10 met two matrix engines on CPUs. ARM’s SME adds one 16×16 outer product per instruction, 256 multiply-accumulates (MACs); Intel’s AMX multiplies a 16×32 tile by a 32×16 tile, 8,192 MACs. Sheet 02 showed why both work: a matrix product is a sum of outer products, column of \(A\) times row of \(B\),

$$AB = \sum_{k} \mathbf a_k\, \mathbf b_k^{\mathsf T}.$$

A tensor core is NVIDIA’s version. The source says one “computes a 4×4 matrix multiply (D = A×B + C) in one cycle”, where regular cores “would need 64 FMA operations”. Both numbers are right: \(4 \times 4 \times 4 = 64\) MACs, four outer products of length-4 vectors summed. But that is one tensor core’s work per cycle. The instruction in the source’s own code, wmma::mma_sync, is issued by a whole warp on fragments that are commonly 16×16×16 (reported), 4,096 MACs per instruction, spread over several tensor cores and cycles.

The per-cycle figure explains the source’s generation table. Volta had 640 tensor cores at 1.53 GHz (both reported): \(640 \times 64 \times 2 \times 1.53\ \text{GHz} = 125.3\) TFLOP/s, the table’s 125, with an FMA counted as two. Tensor cores multiply 16-bit inputs, so that is an FP16 rate. The table labels it “125 TFLOPS TF32”, while its next row lists TF32 as Ampere’s innovation: Volta had no TF32. The A100’s 312 and the H100’s 989 are also reported elsewhere as 16-bit rates, with TF32 at half the first and FP8 at twice the second, so the precision column looks shifted by one row throughout (reported; not derivable here). Per SM, Volta had 64 FP32 lanes and 8 tensor cores (reported): 512 against 64 MACs per cycle, 8×; on the reference GPU 989 against 66.9 TFLOP/s is 14.8×. The source’s “8–16× the rate of FP32 CUDA cores” holds at both ends ([Ch16p2 s09]).

The systolic array is a pipeline of outer products

src 05. Triton, TPUs ↗

Debt 5 of 7, second half. Google’s TPU does its matrix products in a matrix multiply unit (MXU), which the source describes as a 128×128 or 256×256 systolic array: a grid of multiply-accumulate cells through which data flows from the edges, each cell doing one multiply-add per cycle and passing its inputs to its neighbours. No threads, no warps, no divergence. The name is from the heart: data pulses through the grid on every beat.

Here is the simplest version, simulated exactly. Cell \((i, j)\) keeps \(c_{ij}\). Row \(i\) of \(A\) enters from the left, delayed by \(i\) cycles; column \(j\) of \(B\) enters from the top, delayed by \(j\) cycles. Each cell passes its \(a\) to the right and its \(b\) downward. Because of the delays, at cycle \(t\) cell \((i, j)\) holds the pair with index \(k = t - i - j\), and does

$$c_{ij} \;\mathrel{+}=\; a_{ik}\, b_{kj}, \qquad k = t - i - j, \quad 0 \le k < K .$$

Every cell meets every \(k\) exactly once, on a diagonal wavefront. In wave \(k\) every cell adds \(a_{ik} b_{kj}\), which is the whole outer product \(\mathbf a_k \mathbf b_k^{\mathsf T}\), skewed in time across the grid. So the array computes \(\sum_k \mathbf a_k \mathbf b_k^{\mathsf T}\), Sheet 02’s sum, one outer product per wave: SME’s instruction of part 1, pipelined in space. The simulation checks it for four shapes: \(C = AB\) exactly, every wave equal to its outer product, and the pass taking exactly \(K + 2n - 2\) cycles on an \(n \times n\) array ([Ch16p2 s10]).

Those extra \(2n - 2\) cycles are the array filling and draining: the last cell starts \(2n - 2\) cycles after the first. Of the \(n^2 (K + 2n - 2)\) multiply-add slots, \(n^2 K\) do work:

$$\text{utilisation} = \frac{K}{K + 2n - 2}.$$

For a 128×128 array, a stream of 8 rows uses 3.1% of the slots, 128 rows 33.5%, 1,024 rows 80.1%. A 256×256 array at 8 rows uses 1.5%. The TPU’s array is reported to hold its weights in place and stream the activations through, rather than the arrangement simulated here. Then the stream is the rows of the batch, and the same fill-and-drain count applies to its length: a batch of one uses 1 slot in 255.

✓Identity: a line filling and draining

Sheet 13 part 1 §13 timed \(L\) items through a line of depth \(D\) as \(D + L - 1\) cycles. The wavefront crosses \(2n - 1\) diagonals, so \(D = 2n - 1\) and the pass takes \((2n - 1) + K - 1 = K + 2n - 2\) cycles. A systolic array is a deep line that pays its fill and drain on every pass, which is why it rewards long streams and large batches, and why the source can say it is “extremely energy-efficient for matrix multiplication” and nothing else: a non-matrix operation gets none of the grid, and goes to a separate vector unit.

!Where it breaks

The formula is for one isolated pass. A well-fed array starts the next tile’s wave while the last one drains, which hides much of the fill (reported), and loading the weights into a weight-stationary array costs cycles this count leaves out (reported). The bound that survives is the one that matters for small batches: no arrangement makes a single row keep 16,384 cells busy.

The rest of the TPU. TPUs pioneered bfloat16: the 8 exponent bits of float32 with 7 bits of fraction instead of 23. Its largest value is \(3.39 \times 10^{38}\), float32’s range, against float16’s 65,504, so gradients do not overflow; it gives up precision instead, about 2.4 significant figures against float32’s 7.2, as Sheet 17 §02 tabulates. The source’s other TPU facts (16 GB of HBM on v5e, 95 GB on v5p, an inter-chip network for pods, Google Cloud only) are reported here, as is its GPU-against-TPU table, which is a list of judgements rather than calculations.

Triton and Pallas: writing for the whole head

CUDA has you write the program for one thread, one spindle, and lets the hardware gang 32 of them into a warp. Triton, OpenAI’s kernel language, has you write it for a whole block of data at once: offsets = block_start + tl.arange(0, BLOCK_SIZE) is a vector of 1,024 positions, and x + y adds 1,024 pairs. That is part 1’s view of SIMD, writing for the whole drilling head, with the compiler deciding how the work maps onto warps, shared memory and coalesced loads.

The boundary is handled by a mask: mask = offsets < n_elements, and every load and store carries it. For the source’s \(10^6\) elements in blocks of 1,024 the launch asks for \(\lceil 10^6/1024 \rceil = 977\) programs, and the last one has 576 live lanes: 99.96% of lanes do work. The source compares the mask to AVX-512’s mask registers. It is closer still to SVE’s svwhilelt predicate of part 1 §09, which switches on exactly the lanes whose index is below \(n\): the same test, written once for every stroke ([Ch16p2 s11]).

The source’s softmax kernel gives each row to one program and keeps the whole row in registers between its passes, which is fusion (§12); it needs the block to be at least as long as the row, since the source shows no launcher that guarantees it. Its autotuning tries a list of tile shapes on the real machine and keeps the fastest, which is trial cuts before a production run. §08 computed what those three shapes are worth on the roofline.

!The source’s Pallas kernel drops the tail. Run, it leaves NaN.

Pallas is JAX’s kernel language, the source’s only route to custom TPU kernels. Its add_pallas launches grid=(x.shape[0] // 128,): floor division, and no mask. For 1,000 elements that is 7 blocks covering 896; the last 104 elements are never written. For 100 elements the grid is empty and nothing is written. The example was run here as printed, under JAX 0.11.2 on the CPU with Pallas in its interpreter mode: 896 of 1,000 outputs correct, every one from index 896 on left as NaN, uninitialised memory ([Ch16p2 s11]). It is correct only when the length is a multiple of 128. The Triton example a page earlier rounds up (triton.cdiv) and masks; the Pallas one needs the same two things.

Fusion, priced

src 05. Triton, TPUs ↗

Debt 6 of 7. Part 1 §01 counted the bytes fusion saves: after a matrix product writes its output once, each of \(k\) elementwise steps reads and writes an output-sized array again, so unfused traffic on that array is \((1 + 2k)\) passes against 1 fused. Done-in-one machining: one setup, one trip out. The source calls fusion “the single most impactful GPU optimisation for ML” and says that for memory-bound operations it “eliminates the traffic entirely”. Both claims can be priced.

The source’s Triton task 2 computes \(\max(xw + b, 0)\) on \(10^7\) floats. Unfused, PyTorch runs three kernels: x*w reads 2 arrays and writes 1, +b reads 2 and writes 1, relu reads 1 and writes 1. That is 8 arrays of 40 MB against the fused kernel’s 4 (three in, one out): 2.0×, 97 µs against 48.5 µs at 3.3 TB/s. For a chain of elementwise operations, all on the delivery side of the ridge, the traffic ratio is the time ratio.

A real layer. Take a transformer’s feed-forward up-projection: 4,096 tokens, 4,096 features in, 16,384 out, in bfloat16 on the tensor cores, followed by a bias and an activation (\(k = 2\)). The matmul does \(2 \times 4096 \times 4096 \times 16384 = 5.5 \times 10^{11}\) FLOPs: 555.9 µs at 989 TFLOP/s. Its own traffic, with perfect staging, is 302 MB, 91.5 µs, hidden under the arithmetic. The output is 134 MB, and the two unfused elementwise passes move four times that: 162.7 µs more.

Once the matmul is compute-bound the gain no longer depends on the token count at all. With \(s\) bytes per element and \(d\) input features,

$$\frac{t_{\text{unfused}}}{t_{\text{fused}}} = 1 + \frac{k\,s\,\text{Peak}}{\text{BW}\; d} = 1 + \frac{k\,s\,I^{*}}{d} = 1 + \frac{2 \times 2 \times 299.7}{4096} = 1.293 .$$

The ridge is in the numerator: the faster the machining station, the more the elementwise tail costs in proportion, so fusion matters more on tensor cores (1.29×) than on FP32 lanes (1.04×), and more for narrow layers than wide ones. For a single token, as in decoding, the matmul is delivery-bound by its weights and the tail is a few kilobytes: 1.001×. There the saving that remains is the launches, a few microseconds each (reported).

✓Same structure: done-in-one machining, with the saving priced at the bottleneck

Removing a trip to the stores saves exactly that trip’s handling, and the line speeds up only if handling was the constraint. When the tail is all there is, as in the elementwise task, the full traffic ratio arrives as time. When a compute-bound matmul leads the line, fusion removes work from a station that was already waiting on another, and the gain is capped at \(1 + ksI^*/d\).

Softmax fuses for the same reason. The source says F.softmax launches three kernels (maximum, exponentiate-and-sum, divide). Reported elsewhere, PyTorch’s built-in softmax is already a single kernel; the count fits a softmax composed of separate operations, which is what Triton’s own tutorial compares against. For that composition the traffic is 3 row-reads and 2 row-writes against the fused kernel’s 1 and 1, 2.5×; written as five separate operations it is 4.0×. The source’s “2–4× faster” brackets both.

!JAX task 3 cannot be one kernel; compiled, it is four

The task chains x*2, +1, a ReLU and x / jnp.sum(x), and prints “XLA fuses the 4 operations into 1 kernel”. The divide needs the sum of all \(10^7\) values before it can produce its first output, so no single pass can do it: the earliest plan reads \(x\) once to sum, then again to scale. Compiled here by XLA (CPU back end), the function became four fused kernels: one writing the full ReLU array, a reduction, a scalar reciprocal, and one that re-reads \(x\), recomputes and scales. Eager mode makes 9 passes over 40 MB arrays, 360 MB; that plan makes 5, 200 MB; the least possible is 3, 120 MB ([Ch16p2 s12]). The GPU back end may choose differently (reported), but not one pass. Flash attention: the tile that never goes back to stores

src 05. Triton, TPUs ↗

Attention computes \(\text{softmax}(QK^{\mathsf T}/\sqrt d)\,V\) for \(n\) tokens. The middle matrix is \(n \times n\). The source’s figure checks: at \(n = 128\text{K} = 131{,}072\), \(n^2 \times 4\ \text{B} = 68{,}719{,}476{,}736\) bytes, 64 GiB, for one head of one sequence ([Ch16p2 s13]). Flash attention (Dao and others, 2022, reported) never builds it. It loads a block of \(Q\) rows into shared memory, streams blocks of \(K\) and \(V\) past it, and keeps only a running result per row.

The traffic is countable. The standard method writes the \(n \times n\) scores, reads them to take the softmax, writes the probabilities and reads them again: about \(4n^2 + 4nd\) elements. The tiled method reads each \(Q\) block once and all of \(K\) and \(V\) once per \(Q\) block of \(B_r\) rows: \(2nd + 2n^2d/B_r\). For \(n = 4{,}096\), \(d = 64\), \(B_r = 128\): 68.2 million elements against 17.3 million, 3.94× less. Halve the block and \(K\) and \(V\) are read twice as often. The block is set by the shared memory, so traffic falls in proportion to the SRAM size, the source’s “reuse factor proportional to the SRAM size”: this is §08’s kit again, with \(B_r\) as the kit size.

The hard part is the softmax, which needs a row’s maximum before it can start. The online softmax keeps a running maximum \(m\), a running sum \(\ell\) and a running output, and when a new block raises the maximum it rescales what it already has:

$$m' = \max(m,\ \max_j s_j), \qquad \ell' = \ell\, e^{\,m - m'} + \sum_j e^{\,s_j - m'} .$$

In 64-column blocks over random scores, the result equals the one-pass softmax times \(V\) to \(4.4 \times 10^{-16}\), rounding ([Ch16p2 s13]).

✓Same structure: re-referencing readings to a new datum

A survey reduced to one benchmark must be re-referenced when a higher benchmark is adopted: every reading already taken shifts by the same amount. The online softmax does that, with one twist: its readings are exponentials, so the shift is a multiplication by \(e^{m - m'}\). The algebra is exact; the datum exists only to keep \(e^{s}\) from overflowing, the guard every softmax needs.

!“~100× faster” than HBM

The source explains the speed by SRAM being “~100x faster to access than HBM”. Its own memory table gives shared memory ~20 TB/s against HBM’s 1–3.3 TB/s, 6–20× in bandwidth, and ~5 against ~400 cycles, 80× in latency. A tile streamed through shared memory is limited by bandwidth, so the 6–20× is the figure that applies; the larger win is that the \(n \times n\) matrix never goes to HBM at all.

Same numbers, different sum

src 05. Triton, TPUs ↗

Sheet 15’s debt. Sheet 15 §21 said that a container image cannot make a GPU training run repeat exactly, because GPU arithmetic adds numbers in a varying order, and promised that this sheet would say why. Sheet 13 part 1 §11 supplied the cause: floating-point addition is not associative. In float32, where one unit in the last place (an ulp) at \(10^8\) is 8,

$$(1 + 10^{8}) - 10^{8} = 0, \qquad 1 + (10^{8} - 10^{8}) = 1 .$$

A GPU sums a long array in two stages. Each block reduces its own share in a fixed tree: 256 values to 128 to 64 down to 1, the pattern file 07 prints and part 1’s butterfly. That order never changes, so it gives the same bits every time. The block totals must then be combined. A second pass in a fixed order is repeatable. Adding each block’s total into one place with an atomic add, as soon as the block finishes, is not: blocks finish in an order that depends on timing. Library routines also split the same sum differently for a different GPU, batch size or version (reported).

Simulated, with the lab’s data: 65,536 float32 values in \([0, 1)\), exact sum 32,707.5328, where one ulp is \(2^{-9} = 0.00195\). Tree-summed in 256 blocks and combined in block order, the total is 0.8 ulp from exact. Combined in 200 random arrival orders, it comes out 14 different ways, spread over 13 ulp, a relative spread of \(7.8 \times 10^{-7}\). One running total over all 65,536 values, the order a single CPU loop uses, is 138.8 ulp low. On the source’s JAX array, \(10^7\) values of \(\max(2x + 1, 0)\), one running float32 total is 0.25% low, while NumPy’s pairwise sum is off by \(4.9 \times 10^{-9}\) ([Ch16p2 s14]).

✓Same structure: a tolerance stack, whose chain length the order sets

Sheet 17 §08 found that errors through a network stack like tolerances. A sum does the same: every addition may add up to half an ulp of the running total, and a value’s error is carried by every addition after it. A single running total is a chain of \(n - 1\) links, worst case \((n-1)u\) of the sum of magnitudes with \(u = 2^{-24}\): \(3.9 \times 10^{-3}\) at \(n = 65{,}536\). A tree is a chain of \(\log_2 n = 16\) links: \(9.5 \times 10^{-7}\). Reordering a sum is reassembling a stack in a different sequence: the nominal is unchanged, the stack-up is not.

!Where it breaks: rounding is not random

A tolerance stack describes parts that vary. Rounding does not vary: the same additions in the same order give the same bits, every time, on any machine that follows the IEEE rules. So a GPU sum is not noisy; it is a different calculation each time the order changes. That is why the cure is a fixed order, not more precision: deterministic modes in the frameworks remove the atomics and pin the algorithms, at some cost in speed (reported). Accumulating in float32 under 16-bit inputs (§09) narrows the spread but does not make it zero. In training, the last-digit differences feed every later step, which is why two runs drift apart instead of staying a few ulp apart (reported; this page does not simulate training).

Worked example: the source’s matmul, solved twice

src 04. GPU and CUDA ↗
The problem

File 04’s task 2 multiplies two \(1024 \times 1024\) float32 matrices. On the reference GPU (66.9 TFLOP/s FP32, 3.3 TB/s HBM, PCIe 31.5 GB/s each way): (a) what do the naive kernel and the source’s 16×16-tiled kernel attain? (b) What tile reaches the FP32 roof, and can the source’s one-thread-per-output design build it? (c) If \(A\) and \(B\) come from the host and \(C\) goes back on every call, which station binds?

Solved as a production line

Three stations in series: the dock (31.5 GB/s), the stores (3.3 TB/s) and the machines (66.9 TFLOP/s). The kit sets the product mix at the stores. A naive kernel fetches two floats per multiply-add, 0.25 FLOP/B, so the stores supply \(3.3 \times 0.25 = 0.825\) TFLOP/s of work and the product takes \(2 \times 1024^3 / 0.825 \times 10^{12} = 2.60\) ms. The source’s 16-kit makes it 4 FLOP/B: 13.2 TFLOP/s, 0.163 ms, still waiting on the stores. Balance needs 20.3 FLOP/B, a kit edge of 82, which needs 6,724 one-output operators in a cell that holds 1,024. So each operator must take 64 outputs: a 128×128 kit at 32 FLOP/B, which the machines now limit, 32.1 µs.

Now the dock. Each call brings in two matrices and sends one back, \(12N^2 = 12.6\) MB. The work per byte at the dock is \(2N^3/12N^2 = N/6 = 170.7\) FLOP/B, so the dock supplies \(0.0315 \times 170.7 = 5.38\) TFLOP/s of work: 8% of the machines. The transfers alone take 0.399 ms, twelve times the 128-kit’s arithmetic, and the choice of kit no longer matters. Keep the matrices on the GPU, or send larger ones: the dock stops binding at \(N^* = 12{,}741\).

Solved as a roofline

Attainable \(= \min(66.9,\ 3.3\,I,\ 0.0315\,I_{\text{PCIe}})\) TFLOP/s with \(I_{\text{PCIe}} = 170.7\). Naive, \(I = 0.25\): 0.825. Tiled 16: 13.2. Tiled 32, the largest legal one-output tile: 26.4. Block tile 128: 66.9, on the roof. With the dock in the line: \(\min(66.9, 105.6, 5.38) = 5.38\) TFLOP/s, whatever the kernel ([Ch16p2 s15]).

The two solutions are one calculation. The shop version says why the answer has its shape: a kit sized by volume over surface, a cell with a fixed number of operators, and a dock that sets the pace for material bought in fresh every time. The roofline version is quicker to draw. Use whichever lets you see the bottleneck first.

Question bank

24 prompts

Eight to recall, eight to apply, eight to judge. Answer before opening. The numerical answers are recomputed by the verifier under [Ch16p2 s17].

Recall

01What is a warp, and what does SIMT add to SIMD?

32 threads that issue one instruction at a time together: a drilling head with 32 spindles. With SIMT you write code for one thread and the hardware forms the masks, runs both sides of a branch and rejoins the lanes; each lane may also loop its own number of times and load from its own address.

02Give the source’s GPU memory levels with their latencies.

Registers ~0 cycles, shared memory ~5, L1 ~30, L2 ~200, HBM ~400. Ahead of them all, host memory across PCIe at 31.5 GB/s each way.

03What does __syncthreads() do, and why does the tiled matmul call it twice per step?

It holds every thread of the block until all arrive. Once so nobody computes on a kit still being stocked, once so nobody restocks a kit still in use. The time through it is the slowest thread’s, like a transfer line’s index.

04What is a coalesced load?

One whose 32 addresses fall in as few 128-byte transactions as possible: consecutive lanes on consecutive floats, one transaction. Cost is counted in distinct pallets touched.

05How many multiply-adds does a tensor core do per cycle, by the source, and how many does one 16×16×16 mma_sync cover?

\(4\times4\times4 = 64\) per core per cycle; \(16^3 = 4{,}096\) per warp instruction.

06How does an output-stationary systolic array compute \(AB\)?

Cell \((i,j)\) keeps \(c_{ij}\); rows of \(A\) enter from the left and columns of \(B\) from the top, each delayed one cycle per row or column. At cycle \(t\) the cell adds \(a_{ik}b_{kj}\) with \(k = t-i-j\). Wave \(k\) adds the outer product \(\mathbf a_k\mathbf b_k^{\mathsf T}\).

07What does Triton’s mask do, and which CPU feature is it?

It switches off the lanes whose offset is at or past the end of the array, so the last block needs no clean-up loop. It is SVE’s svwhilelt predicate (part 1 §09), or an AVX-512 mask built from the same test.

08Why is a GPU run not bit-exactly repeatable?

Float addition is not associative, and some GPU sums are combined in an order set by timing (atomic adds as blocks finish) or by library choices. Different orders give totals that differ in the last bits: 14 different totals in 200 runs in §14.

Apply

09A GPU has 50 TFLOP/s in FP32 and 2 TB/s. Ridge, and what does layer norm attain?

Ridge \(50/2 = 25\) FLOP/B. Layer norm at 0.625 FLOP/B: \(2 \times 0.625 = 1.25\) TFLOP/s, 2.5% of peak.

10A 32×32 shared-memory tile: intensity, shared memory, threads?

\(32/4 = 8\) FLOP/B; two tiles of \(32^2\) floats, 8 KiB; 1,024 threads, the most a block may have.

11Memory latency 500 cycles, 2 TB/s, clock 1.5 GHz, 100 SMs. How many 128-byte warp-loads must each SM keep in flight?

\(2\times10^{12}/1.5\times10^{9} = 1{,}333\) bytes per cycle; \(\times 500 = 666{,}667\) bytes; per SM 6,667 bytes, 52.1 warp-loads.

1265,536 registers per SM and 80 registers per thread. How many warps fit?

\(\lfloor 65{,}536/(32 \times 80) \rfloor = \lfloor 25.6 \rfloor = 25\).

13A branch is taken by 2% of threads at random. What share of warps must run it?

\(1 - 0.98^{32} = 0.476\): nearly half.

14A warp loads 32 floats at stride 8. How many 128-byte transactions, and what share of the bytes is used?

8 transactions, 12.5%. With 32-byte sectors also 12.5%.

15A 128×128 systolic array streams 64 rows in one pass. Utilisation?

\(64/(64 + 254) = 20.1\%\).

16A matmul followed by three elementwise operations: passes over the output array, unfused against fused?

\(1 + 2 \times 3 = 7\) against 1. The time saved depends on which station binds (§12).

Judge

17A kernel uses 128 registers per thread, keeps one load in flight per warp, and reaches 40% of HBM bandwidth. Launch more threads?

They will not become resident: the register file holds 16 warps, and Little’s law gives \(16 \times 128/404 = 5.07\) of 12.63 bytes per cycle, 40.1%, exactly what was measured. Either keep several loads in flight per warp (double buffering) or bring the registers down to 51 or fewer.

18The source’s divergence task would print a speedup near 1.0. Is divergence free?

No. That kernel sits at 0.25 FLOP/B, deep on the delivery side, so the doubled arithmetic was hidden behind memory. In a compute-bound kernel a divergent warp pays \(a + b\) in full.

19Should you fuse bias and activation into a large bf16 matmul, and what should you expect?

Yes, but expect at most \(1 + ksI^*/d\): 1.29× at \(d = 4096\) on the reference GPU. The \(1 + 2k\) cut applies to the tail’s traffic, not to the matmul’s time.

20A colleague offloads a 2048×2048 FP32 matmul to the GPU, copying both operands in and the result out every call. Worth it?

Not as written. The link takes 1.60 ms against 0.257 ms of arithmetic at the roof; the dock binds below \(N \approx 12{,}700\). Keep the data on the GPU between calls.

21Is a TPU’s systolic array a good fit for batch-1 inference?

Poor. One row through a 128×128 array keeps it 1/255 = 0.39% busy for that pass; the array needs long streams to amortise its fill and drain. A GPU at batch 1 is also limited, by reading the weights for every token (Sheet 17).

22Can a GPU training run be made bit-exact, and what does it cost?

On one machine, mostly: fix every reduction order (no float atomics, deterministic algorithms, the same library version and launch shapes). It costs speed (reported), and a different GPU model may still choose a different order.

23“Coalescing no longer matters because GPUs fetch 32-byte sectors.” Agree?

Partly. Large strides waste 87.5% instead of up to 97%. But a stride of 4 (an array of 16-byte structures) uses 25% in both models, so structure-of-arrays still moves 4× fewer bytes.

24The JAX task claims XLA fuses its four operations into one kernel. How would you check, and what is the best possible?

Compile it and read the optimised program: here it showed four fused kernels. The divide needs the whole sum first, so at least two passes are needed; the least traffic is 3 array passes, 120 MB, against eager mode’s 360 MB.
