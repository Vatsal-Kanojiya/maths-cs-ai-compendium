Lags, Tails, Qubits

Exponential smoothing of a price series and the membrane of a spiking neuron obey the same difference equation, and it is the one you wrote for a thermocouple. A minimum-variance portfolio is the beamformer of Sheet 09. Value at risk fails the way a series reliability calculation does. And a qubit, restricted to real amplitudes, has a Mohr’s circle.

01

Exponential smoothing is a first-order lag, tuned by a Kalman filter

src 19/01. finance ↗

The outline’s first finance bullet lists forecasting methods from ARIMA to transformers. The oldest of them is the one with a derivable core. Exponential smoothing keeps one number, the level \(s\), and updates it with each new observation \(x_k\):

$$s_k = (1-\alpha)\,s_{k-1} + \alpha\,x_k, \qquad 0 < \alpha \le 1, \qquad \text{forecast of } x_{k+1} = s_k$$

Sheet 07 §04 wrote this equation, \(x_k = a x_{k-1} + b u_k\), and named where you met it: a thermal mass, an RC circuit, a settling tank, sampled at a fixed step. It is a first-order lag with unit gain. Unroll it and the level is a weighted sum of the past, with weights \(\alpha(1-\alpha)^j\) on the observation \(j\) steps old. Those weights are the lag’s discrete impulse response; they sum to 1, which is the unit gain.

✓Identity: two properties of the weights you can compute as section properties

Treat the weights as a distribution of mass along the time axis, as Sheet 04 §01 did. Their centroid is the average age of the data inside the level: \(\sum_j j\,\alpha(1-\alpha)^j = (1-\alpha)/\alpha\), which is 4 samples at \(\alpha = 0.2\). The sum of their squares is how much white noise gets through: the level’s variance is \(\sigma^2\alpha/(2-\alpha)\), a ninth of \(\sigma^2\) at \(\alpha = 0.2\) ([Ch19 s01]).

A plain moving average of \(N\) points passes \(\sigma^2/N\), so the moving average with the same noise has \(N = (2-\alpha)/\alpha\) points, 9 at \(\alpha = 0.2\). Its average age is \((N-1)/2 = 4\), exactly the smoother’s. The two filters buy the same noise reduction for the same delay; the exponential one does it with one stored number instead of nine.

It is already on your quality-control chart

Sheet 04 §06 showed that every point on an \(\bar{X}\) chart is a hypothesis test. The EWMA chart of statistical process control plots exactly \(s_k\), with \(\alpha\) written \(\lambda\), so that a small sustained shift accumulates instead of hiding inside each sample’s noise. Its steady-state control limits follow from the noise factor above:

$$\mu_0 \pm L\,\sigma\sqrt{\frac{\lambda}{2-\lambda}} \;=\; \mu_0 \pm 1.000\,\sigma \quad\text{at } \lambda = 0.2,\ L = 3$$

A simulation of 200,000 in-control points gives a steady-state variance of 0.1107 against the formula’s \(1/9 = 0.1111\) ([Ch19 s01]). If your quality course stopped at Shewhart charts, this is the one it would have taught next.

The same recurrence is inside Sheet 06 §06: Adam’s \(m_t\) and \(v_t\) are exponential smoothers of the gradient and of its square. That section pointed back to Sheet 04 for the definition, which Sheet 04 never gave; it is here. Adam’s bias correction is the unit-gain property repaired. A smoother started at zero has weights summing to \(1-(1-\alpha)^t\), not 1, so after 50 steps with \(\beta_2 = 0.999\) it reads 0.44 where the mean square is 9.03, and dividing by \(1-\beta_2^{50}\) gives 9.026 ([Ch19 s01]).

The best smoothing constant is a Kalman gain

Every forecasting text says \(\alpha\) is chosen by judgement or by trial. There is a better answer. Suppose the true level wanders as a random walk, moving by a random step of variance \(q\) each period, and you see it through measurement noise of variance \(r\). That is a one-state system with \(F = 1\) and \(H = 1\), and Sheet 11 §09 gave its optimal estimator: predict \(\hat{x}^- = \hat{x}\) with \(P^- = P + q\), then correct with gain \(K = P^-/(P^- + r)\). The correction is \(\hat{x} \leftarrow \hat{x} + K(x_k - \hat{x})\), which is exponential smoothing with \(\alpha = K\). Once the filter settles, \(K\) is constant, and the steady state of \(P^-\) solves \((P^-)^2 = qP^- + qr\):

$$\alpha^\ast = \frac{\sqrt{\lambda^2 + 4\lambda} - \lambda}{2}, \qquad \lambda = \frac{q}{r}$$

The closed form matches the Riccati recursion to \(2\times10^{-16}\) for \(q/r\) from 0.001 to 100 ([Ch19 s01]). Three values are worth remembering:

The last row is the finance lesson. If a price were a pure random walk with no noise on top, the best forecast of tomorrow would be today’s price, and no smoothing could improve it. The one-step forecast error of any \(\alpha\) has a closed form,

$$\text{MSE}(\alpha) = \frac{(1-\alpha)^2 q + \alpha^2 r}{\alpha(2-\alpha)} + q + r,$$

whose minimum sits at \(\alpha^\ast\) and equals the Kalman filter’s innovation variance \(P^- + r\): 1.3702 at \(q/r = 0.1\). On a 400,000-step simulated series the measured errors were 1.5830, 1.3728 and 1.5498 at \(\alpha\) = 0.1, 0.270 and 0.6, against 1.5789, 1.3702 and 1.5476 from the formula ([Ch19 s01]).

✓Identity: the first-order sibling of the 0.707 servo

Sheet 11 §10 found that the Kalman filter for a measured position with white-noise acceleration settles into a second-order system with \(\zeta = 1/\sqrt2\) and \(\omega_n = (q/\rho)^{1/4}\). One integrator fewer gives this: a first-order lag whose time constant, in the continuous limit, is \(\sqrt{r_c/q_c}\). The discrete \(\Delta t/\alpha^\ast\) converges on it, 0.50502, 0.50050, 0.50005 at \(\Delta t = 10^{-2}, 10^{-3}, 10^{-4}\) against 0.5 ([Ch19 s01]). In both cases the estimator nobody tuned arrives at a definite filter, and in both the noise ratio sets only the speed.

One more line connects the outline’s ARIMA. Write the forecast error as \(e_k = x_k - s_{k-1}\). Then the differenced series obeys \(x_k - x_{k-1} = e_k - (1-\alpha)\,e_{k-1}\), which is the model ARIMA calls (0,1,1). Exponential smoothing is the forecast that model implies ([Ch19 s01]); the rest of the ARIMA family is not treated here.

!Where it breaks

A trend. The forecast is a flat line at the current level, so a series rising steadily is always under-forecast. Feed a ramp of slope 2 per step through \(\alpha = 0.25\) and the level settles 6.000 behind, which is slope \(\times(1-\alpha)/\alpha\) ([Ch19 s01]). It is the same velocity error as a thermocouple in a furnace that is heating up, which reads low by the heating rate times its time constant. Methods with a trend term exist (reported) and are not derived here.

The model. \(\alpha^\ast\) is optimal for a random walk plus white noise and for nothing else. Returns on real assets have heavy tails and volatility that comes in clusters (reported); neither is in the model. Sheet 11 §09’s innovation test is how you would find out: if the forecast errors are not white, the model is wrong, whatever \(\alpha\) you chose.

02

The minimum-variance portfolio is Sheet 09’s beamformer

src 19/01. finance ↗

The third finance bullet names mean–variance portfolio optimisation, due to Markowitz (reported). Hold \(n\) assets in proportions \(w_i\), with the whole budget invested, \(\sum w_i = 1\). The returns have a covariance matrix \(\Sigma\), which Sheet 04 §03 showed is built exactly like an inertia tensor: variances on the diagonal, covariances in place of products of inertia. The portfolio’s variance is the quadratic form \(w^{\mathsf T}\Sigma w\). Minimise it, subject to the budget:

$$\min_{w}\ w^{\mathsf T}\Sigma\,w \quad\text{subject to}\quad \mathbf{1}^{\mathsf T}w = 1 \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad w^\ast = \frac{\Sigma^{-1}\mathbf{1}}{\mathbf{1}^{\mathsf T}\Sigma^{-1}\mathbf{1}}, \quad \sigma^2_{\min} = \frac{1}{\mathbf{1}^{\mathsf T}\Sigma^{-1}\mathbf{1}}$$

Sheet 03 §07’s recipe gives it in two lines. Setting the gradient of \(w^{\mathsf T}\Sigma w - \lambda(\mathbf{1}^{\mathsf T}w - 1)\) to zero gives \(2\Sigma w = \lambda\mathbf{1}\), so \(w\) is proportional to \(\Sigma^{-1}\mathbf{1}\), and the budget fixes the scale. On 200 random covariance matrices the closed form met the budget exactly, and none of 20,000 other fully-invested portfolios did better ([Ch19 s02]).

✓Identity: it is the MVDR beamformer with every steering weight equal to one

Sheet 09 §19 minimised the output power of a microphone array, \(\mathbf{w}^{H}\Phi_{nn}\mathbf{w}\), subject to passing the target direction undistorted, \(\mathbf{w}^{H}\mathbf{d} = 1\), and got \(\Phi_{nn}^{-1}\mathbf{d}/(\mathbf{d}^{H}\Phi_{nn}^{-1}\mathbf{d})\). Put the return covariance in place of the noise covariance and \(\mathbf{d} = \mathbf{1}\), and it is the formula above, to the last bit: the computed difference is zero ([Ch19 s02]). The budget constraint is a steering vector. (Sheet 09 sent you to Sheet 03 §08 for the method; the Lagrange-multiplier section is §07.)

Sheet 03 §07 also said the multiplier is a shadow price, the rate at which the optimum changes as the constraint is relaxed. Here \(\lambda = 2\sigma^2_{\min}\), and the derivative of the minimum variance with respect to the budget, measured by finite difference, is the same number, 0.065208 for the two assets below ([Ch19 s02]).

Two assets make it concrete: volatilities 20% and 30%, correlation 0.2. The minimum-variance mix is 73.6% and 26.4%, with a volatility of 18.06%, lower than either asset alone. Adding the other half of the bullet, a target expected return as a second constraint, adds a second multiplier and traces the efficient frontier, a parabola in variance against return whose vertex is this portfolio ([Ch19 s02]).

Diversification is a tolerance stack

Put equal weights on \(n\) assets of equal volatility \(\sigma\), every pair correlated at \(\rho\). The portfolio return is the average of \(n\) random parts, and its variance is Sheet 04 §05’s stack-up with the covariance terms kept:

$$\sigma_p = \sigma\sqrt{\rho + \frac{1-\rho}{n}} \;\longrightarrow\; \sigma\sqrt{\rho} \quad (n\to\infty)$$

With \(\rho = 1\) between every pair, the stack formula collapses to \(\sigma_{\text{sum}} = \sum\sigma_i\), the arithmetic stack you were taught as the conservative method; for six random parts it gives 7.239 against 3.206 for RSS ([Ch19 s02]). So the choice between worst-case and RSS stacking is a choice of correlation, 1 or 0, and the honest answer for most assemblies lies between. Sheet 04 §05 named the shop-floor cause of correlation, a tool wearing through a shift; Sheet 17 §08 found that a rounding bias stacks linearly for the same reason. In a portfolio the common cause is the market itself, and the floor \(\sigma\sqrt{\rho}\) is what finance calls systematic risk.

Negative weights, and the dummy gauge

The riskier asset’s weight is proportional to \(\sigma_1^2 - \rho\sigma_1\sigma_2\), so it turns negative exactly when \(\rho > \sigma_1/\sigma_2\), which is 2/3 for 20% and 30% ([Ch19 s02]). At \(\rho = 0.8\) the best portfolio holds −23.5% of the riskier asset: it sells it short, using the correlated part to cancel the safer asset’s risk. At \(\rho = 1\) the weights (3, −2) cancel the risk completely. A half-bridge with a dummy gauge does the same thing with a strain measurement: the dummy sees only the temperature, which it shares perfectly with the active gauge, and its signal enters with a minus sign. Sheet 09’s beamformer does it with noise.

!Where it breaks: you never know \(\Sigma\)

A beamformer can measure its noise covariance for as long as it likes. A portfolio manager has, say, five years of monthly returns. The formula needs \(\Sigma^{-1}\), and Sheet 02 §03 warned what an inverse does to an ill-conditioned matrix. Take 20 assets that are in truth independent with equal risk, so the true \(\Sigma\) is the identity with condition number 1, and estimate it from 60 months: the estimate’s condition number has a median of 10.8. The “optimal” weights built from it carry 1.487 times the true minimum variance on average, and 1.863 times at the 95th percentile over 4,000 trials ([Ch19 s02]).

The average matches a reported closed form, \((T-2)/(T-N-1) = 58/39 = 1.487\), which grows without bound as the number of assets \(N\) approaches the number of observations \(T\). The optimiser cannot tell real structure from sampling noise, and it bets hardest on whichever it sees most clearly. Expected returns are harder still to estimate (reported), which is one reason the minimum-variance point, which needs no expected returns at all, is the part of the theory that survives contact with data.
