01

Exponential smoothing is a first-order lag, tuned by a Kalman filter

src 19/01. finance ↗

The outline’s first finance bullet lists forecasting methods from ARIMA to transformers. The oldest of them is the one with a derivable core. Exponential smoothing keeps one number, the level \(s\), and updates it with each new observation \(x_k\):

$$s_k = (1-\alpha)\,s_{k-1} + \alpha\,x_k, \qquad 0 < \alpha \le 1, \qquad \text{forecast of } x_{k+1} = s_k$$

Sheet 07 §04 wrote this equation, \(x_k = a x_{k-1} + b u_k\), and named where you met it: a thermal mass, an RC circuit, a settling tank, sampled at a fixed step. It is a first-order lag with unit gain. Unroll it and the level is a weighted sum of the past, with weights \(\alpha(1-\alpha)^j\) on the observation \(j\) steps old. Those weights are the lag’s discrete impulse response; they sum to 1, which is the unit gain.

✓Identity: two properties of the weights you can compute as section properties

Treat the weights as a distribution of mass along the time axis, as Sheet 04 §01 did. Their centroid is the average age of the data inside the level: \(\sum_j j\,\alpha(1-\alpha)^j = (1-\alpha)/\alpha\), which is 4 samples at \(\alpha = 0.2\). The sum of their squares is how much white noise gets through: the level’s variance is \(\sigma^2\alpha/(2-\alpha)\), a ninth of \(\sigma^2\) at \(\alpha = 0.2\) ([Ch19 s01]).

A plain moving average of \(N\) points passes \(\sigma^2/N\), so the moving average with the same noise has \(N = (2-\alpha)/\alpha\) points, 9 at \(\alpha = 0.2\). Its average age is \((N-1)/2 = 4\), exactly the smoother’s. The two filters buy the same noise reduction for the same delay; the exponential one does it with one stored number instead of nine.

It is already on your quality-control chart

Sheet 04 §06 showed that every point on an \(\bar{X}\) chart is a hypothesis test. The EWMA chart of statistical process control plots exactly \(s_k\), with \(\alpha\) written \(\lambda\), so that a small sustained shift accumulates instead of hiding inside each sample’s noise. Its steady-state control limits follow from the noise factor above:

$$\mu_0 \pm L\,\sigma\sqrt{\frac{\lambda}{2-\lambda}} \;=\; \mu_0 \pm 1.000\,\sigma \quad\text{at } \lambda = 0.2,\ L = 3$$

A simulation of 200,000 in-control points gives a steady-state variance of 0.1107 against the formula’s \(1/9 = 0.1111\) ([Ch19 s01]). If your quality course stopped at Shewhart charts, this is the one it would have taught next.

The same recurrence is inside Sheet 06 §06: Adam’s \(m_t\) and \(v_t\) are exponential smoothers of the gradient and of its square. That section pointed back to Sheet 04 for the definition, which Sheet 04 never gave; it is here. Adam’s bias correction is the unit-gain property repaired. A smoother started at zero has weights summing to \(1-(1-\alpha)^t\), not 1, so after 50 steps with \(\beta_2 = 0.999\) it reads 0.44 where the mean square is 9.03, and dividing by \(1-\beta_2^{50}\) gives 9.026 ([Ch19 s01]).

The best smoothing constant is a Kalman gain

Every forecasting text says \(\alpha\) is chosen by judgement or by trial. There is a better answer. Suppose the true level wanders as a random walk, moving by a random step of variance \(q\) each period, and you see it through measurement noise of variance \(r\). That is a one-state system with \(F = 1\) and \(H = 1\), and Sheet 11 §09 gave its optimal estimator: predict \(\hat{x}^- = \hat{x}\) with \(P^- = P + q\), then correct with gain \(K = P^-/(P^- + r)\). The correction is \(\hat{x} \leftarrow \hat{x} + K(x_k - \hat{x})\), which is exponential smoothing with \(\alpha = K\). Once the filter settles, \(K\) is constant, and the steady state of \(P^-\) solves \((P^-)^2 = qP^- + qr\):

$$\alpha^\ast = \frac{\sqrt{\lambda^2 + 4\lambda} - \lambda}{2}, \qquad \lambda = \frac{q}{r}$$

The closed form matches the Riccati recursion to \(2\times10^{-16}\) for \(q/r\) from 0.001 to 100 ([Ch19 s01]). Three values are worth remembering:

✓Identity: the first-order sibling of the 0.707 servo

Sheet 11 §10 found that the Kalman filter for a measured position with white-noise acceleration settles into a second-order system with \(\zeta = 1/\sqrt2\) and \(\omega_n = (q/\rho)^{1/4}\). One integrator fewer gives this: a first-order lag whose time constant, in the continuous limit, is \(\sqrt{r_c/q_c}\). The discrete \(\Delta t/\alpha^\ast\) converges on it, 0.50502, 0.50050, 0.50005 at \(\Delta t = 10^{-2}, 10^{-3}, 10^{-4}\) against 0.5 ([Ch19 s01]). In both cases the estimator nobody tuned arrives at a definite filter, and in both the noise ratio sets only the speed.

One more line connects the outline’s ARIMA. Write the forecast error as \(e_k = x_k - s_{k-1}\). Then the differenced series obeys \(x_k - x_{k-1} = e_k - (1-\alpha)\,e_{k-1}\), which is the model ARIMA calls (0,1,1). Exponential smoothing is the forecast that model implies ([Ch19 s01]); the rest of the ARIMA family is not treated here.

!Where it breaks

A trend. The forecast is a flat line at the current level, so a series rising steadily is always under-forecast. Feed a ramp of slope 2 per step through \(\alpha = 0.25\) and the level settles 6.000 behind, which is slope \(\times(1-\alpha)/\alpha\) ([Ch19 s01]). It is the same velocity error as a thermocouple in a furnace that is heating up, which reads low by the heating rate times its time constant. Methods with a trend term exist (reported) and are not derived here.

The model. \(\alpha^\ast\) is optimal for a random walk plus white noise and for nothing else. Returns on real assets have heavy tails and volatility that comes in clusters (reported); neither is in the model. Sheet 11 §09’s innovation test is how you would find out: if the forecast errors are not white, the model is wrong, whatever \(\alpha\) you chose.

The minimum-variance portfolio is Sheet 09’s beamformer

src 19/01. finance ↗

The third finance bullet names mean–variance portfolio optimisation, due to Markowitz (reported). Hold \(n\) assets in proportions \(w_i\), with the whole budget invested, \(\sum w_i = 1\). The returns have a covariance matrix \(\Sigma\), which Sheet 04 §03 showed is built exactly like an inertia tensor: variances on the diagonal, covariances in place of products of inertia. The portfolio’s variance is the quadratic form \(w^{\mathsf T}\Sigma w\). Minimise it, subject to the budget:

$$\min_{w}\ w^{\mathsf T}\Sigma\,w \quad\text{subject to}\quad \mathbf{1}^{\mathsf T}w = 1 \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad w^\ast = \frac{\Sigma^{-1}\mathbf{1}}{\mathbf{1}^{\mathsf T}\Sigma^{-1}\mathbf{1}}, \quad \sigma^2_{\min} = \frac{1}{\mathbf{1}^{\mathsf T}\Sigma^{-1}\mathbf{1}}$$

Sheet 03 §07’s recipe gives it in two lines. Setting the gradient of \(w^{\mathsf T}\Sigma w - \lambda(\mathbf{1}^{\mathsf T}w - 1)\) to zero gives \(2\Sigma w = \lambda\mathbf{1}\), so \(w\) is proportional to \(\Sigma^{-1}\mathbf{1}\), and the budget fixes the scale. On 200 random covariance matrices the closed form met the budget exactly, and none of 20,000 other fully-invested portfolios did better ([Ch19 s02]).

✓Identity: it is the MVDR beamformer with every steering weight equal to one

Sheet 09 §19 minimised the output power of a microphone array, \(\mathbf{w}^{H}\Phi_{nn}\mathbf{w}\), subject to passing the target direction undistorted, \(\mathbf{w}^{H}\mathbf{d} = 1\), and got \(\Phi_{nn}^{-1}\mathbf{d}/(\mathbf{d}^{H}\Phi_{nn}^{-1}\mathbf{d})\). Put the return covariance in place of the noise covariance and \(\mathbf{d} = \mathbf{1}\), and it is the formula above, to the last bit: the computed difference is zero ([Ch19 s02]). The budget constraint is a steering vector. (Sheet 09 sent you to Sheet 03 §08 for the method; the Lagrange-multiplier section is §07.)

Sheet 03 §07 also said the multiplier is a shadow price, the rate at which the optimum changes as the constraint is relaxed. Here \(\lambda = 2\sigma^2_{\min}\), and the derivative of the minimum variance with respect to the budget, measured by finite difference, is the same number, 0.065208 for the two assets below ([Ch19 s02]).

Two assets make it concrete: volatilities 20% and 30%, correlation 0.2. The minimum-variance mix is 73.6% and 26.4%, with a volatility of 18.06%, lower than either asset alone. Adding the other half of the bullet, a target expected return as a second constraint, adds a second multiplier and traces the efficient frontier, a parabola in variance against return whose vertex is this portfolio ([Ch19 s02]).

Diversification is a tolerance stack

Put equal weights on \(n\) assets of equal volatility \(\sigma\), every pair correlated at \(\rho\). The portfolio return is the average of \(n\) random parts, and its variance is Sheet 04 §05’s stack-up with the covariance terms kept:

$$\sigma_p = \sigma\sqrt{\rho + \frac{1-\rho}{n}} \;\longrightarrow\; \sigma\sqrt{\rho} \quad (n\to\infty)$$

Negative weights, and the dummy gauge

The riskier asset’s weight is proportional to \(\sigma_1^2 - \rho\sigma_1\sigma_2\), so it turns negative exactly when \(\rho > \sigma_1/\sigma_2\), which is 2/3 for 20% and 30% ([Ch19 s02]). At \(\rho = 0.8\) the best portfolio holds −23.5% of the riskier asset: it sells it short, using the correlated part to cancel the safer asset’s risk. At \(\rho = 1\) the weights (3, −2) cancel the risk completely. A half-bridge with a dummy gauge does the same thing with a strain measurement: the dummy sees only the temperature, which it shares perfectly with the active gauge, and its signal enters with a minus sign. Sheet 09’s beamformer does it with noise.

!Where it breaks: you never know \(\Sigma\)

A beamformer can measure its noise covariance for as long as it likes. A portfolio manager has, say, five years of monthly returns. The formula needs \(\Sigma^{-1}\), and Sheet 02 §03 warned what an inverse does to an ill-conditioned matrix. Take 20 assets that are in truth independent with equal risk, so the true \(\Sigma\) is the identity with condition number 1, and estimate it from 60 months: the estimate’s condition number has a median of 10.8. The “optimal” weights built from it carry 1.487 times the true minimum variance on average, and 1.863 times at the 95th percentile over 4,000 trials ([Ch19 s02]).

The average matches a reported closed form, \((T-2)/(T-N-1) = 58/39 = 1.487\), which grows without bound as the number of assets \(N\) approaches the number of observations \(T\). The optimiser cannot tell real structure from sampling noise, and it bets hardest on whichever it sees most clearly. Expected returns are harder still to estimate (reported), which is one reason the minimum-variance point, which needs no expected returns at all, is the part of the theory that survives contact with data.

03

Value at risk fails like a series system; expected shortfall is a centroid

src 19/01. finance ↗

The fourth finance bullet names value at risk, expected shortfall and Monte Carlo simulation. Let \(L\) be the loss on a position over some horizon, a random variable.

VaRα
The \(\alpha\)-quantile of \(L\): the smallest loss that is exceeded with probability at most \(1-\alpha\). A 95% VaR of 50 says “on 95% of days we lose 50 or less”.
ESα
Expected shortfall: the average loss over the worst \(1-\alpha\) of outcomes. Precisely, the mean of the quantile function over \((\alpha, 1)\), which also handles losses that come in lumps.

VaR is a percentile limit, the same kind of number as a tolerance limit set at a percentile of a process. Expected shortfall is a section property.

✓Identity: expected shortfall is the centroid of the tail

Sheet 04 §01 showed that a mean is a centroid. ES is the mean of the loss distribution restricted to its tail, so it is the centroid of the tail area, \(\int_{\text{tail}} x f(x)\,dx \big/ \int_{\text{tail}} f(x)\,dx\). For a normal loss, VaR99 is \(2.326\sigma\), and the centroid beyond it, computed by integrating the area, is \(2.665\sigma\), equal to the closed form \(\varphi(z)/(1-\alpha)\) ([Ch19 s03]). VaR says where the tail starts; ES says where its mass sits. ES97.5 works out at \(2.338\sigma\), almost exactly VaR99, a pairing reported in banking regulation.

The counterexample is a reliability calculation

A risk measure ought to be subadditive: putting two positions together should never be riskier than the two measured separately, \(\rho(X+Y) \le \rho(X) + \rho(Y)\). Otherwise it penalises diversification, and a firm could lower its reported risk by splitting into two firms. VaR fails this. Two loans, each losing 100 with probability 4%, independently:

✓Identity: “something is lost” is a series system

The probability that at least one of \(n\) independent loans defaults is \(1 - (1-p)^n\). That is Sheet 05 §03’s series reliability, read as a failure: the portfolio has a loss if loan 1 or loan 2 defaults, as a series system fails if any block fails. VaR at 95% is a pass–fail test of that probability against 5%. It is an assembly made of two parts that each pass a 5% reject limit while the assembly does not. With \(p = 4\%\) the second loan tips it; with \(p = 1\%\) it takes six, since \(1-0.99^5 = 0.049\) and \(1-0.99^6 = 0.059\) ([Ch19 s03]).

The test looks only at whether the tail probability crosses the line, never at how large the losses beyond it are. That is the whole defect, and ES, which averages over the tail, does not have it.

When does VaR behave? When losses are jointly normal, VaR is \(z\sigma\), and the standard deviation of a sum never exceeds the sum of standard deviations; 2,000 random normal pairs never broke subadditivity ([Ch19 s03]). So the failure lives exactly where Sheet 04 §05’s central limit theorem does not reach: lumpy, rare, all-or-nothing losses such as defaults, which are the ones a risk number exists to catch. ES is subadditive for every joint distribution (reported as a theorem). On 6,000 random dependent, lumpy pairs generated here, VaR95 failed 16 times and ES never ([Ch19 s03]).

Monte Carlo VaR is a sampling plan with a sparse tail

For a real portfolio the loss distribution has no closed form, so it is simulated: draw \(N\) scenarios, compute each loss, sort, and read off the \(\alpha\) point. The error of that estimate has a formula you have half met already:

$$\operatorname{SD}\!\left(\widehat{\text{VaR}}_\alpha\right) \approx \frac{\sqrt{\alpha(1-\alpha)/N}}{f(\text{VaR}_\alpha)}$$

The numerator is the binomial standard error of an estimated fraction, the one you use to judge a defect rate from a sample (Sheet 04 §§04–06). Dividing by the density \(f\) at the quantile converts an error in probability into an error in loss, and in the tail the density is small. For a normal loss at 99% with 10,000 scenarios the formula gives \(0.0373\sigma\); 2,000 repeated simulations measured \(0.0379\sigma\). The mean of the same samples is known to \(0.0099\sigma\), so matching its precision at the 99% point takes 13.9 times as many scenarios ([Ch19 s03]). Only 100 of the 10,000 scenarios lie in the tail.

✓Same economics: Monte Carlo tolerance analysis

A statistical tolerance analysis by simulation, building 10,000 virtual assemblies and counting those out of specification, estimates a 1% reject rate with a standard error of 0.000995, a tenth of the rate itself ([Ch19 s03]). Risk managers and tolerance analysts face the same arithmetic: the events you care about are rare, so the samples that inform you are few.

!Where it breaks

Every number in this section assumes the loss distribution is known, or that the simulation draws from the right one. Loans default together in a recession; the independence behind \(1-(1-p)^n\) is Sheet 05 §09’s common-cause failure in another industry. Getting the joint distribution right is the hard part of real risk modelling and is not attempted here. Credit scoring, the bullet’s last item, is a classification problem in the sense of Sheet 06 and is not treated.

A qubit, a gate, and a Mohr’s circle

src 20/01. quantum ML ↗

The first two quantum bullets list qubits, superposition, entanglement, measurement and the standard gates. Take quantum mechanics’ rules as given, since they are postulates rather than things this page can derive. A qubit’s state is a pair of amplitudes \((a_0, a_1)\), complex numbers with \(|a_0|^2 + |a_1|^2 = 1\). Measuring it gives 0 with probability \(|a_0|^2\) and 1 with probability \(|a_1|^2\), and leaves the qubit in the state it reported (the Born rule). Superposition means both amplitudes are non-zero. A gate is a \(2\times2\) unitary matrix, \(U^{\dagger}U = I\), the complex version of Sheet 02 §04’s orthogonal matrix, and for the same reason it preserves length: the probabilities still sum to 1 after any gate, checked to \(10^{-15}\) over 500 random two-qubit gates ([Ch20 s04]).

Several standard gates are real, and then they are matrices you have used as direction cosine matrices:

$$R_y(\theta) = \begin{bmatrix}\cos\frac{\theta}{2} & -\sin\frac{\theta}{2}\\ \sin\frac{\theta}{2} & \cos\frac{\theta}{2}\end{bmatrix}, \quad H = \frac{1}{\sqrt2}\begin{bmatrix}1 & 1\\ 1 & -1\end{bmatrix}, \quad X = \begin{bmatrix}0 & 1\\ 1 & 0\end{bmatrix}, \quad Z = \begin{bmatrix}1 & 0\\ 0 & -1\end{bmatrix}$$

\(R_y(\theta)\) is a rotation through \(\theta/2\). The Hadamard gate \(H\) is a reflection about the line at 22.5°, with determinant \(-1\) and \(H^2 = I\); \(X\) reflects about 45° and \(Z\) about the horizontal ([Ch20 s04]). So restrict the amplitudes to real numbers for a moment. The state is a unit vector \(\psi = (\cos a, \sin a)\) in a plane, and real gates turn or mirror it.

✓Identity: a real qubit state is a uniaxial stress

Form the density matrix \(\rho = \psi\psi^{\mathsf T}\), the object quantum mechanics uses to compute every probability:

$$\rho = \begin{bmatrix}\cos^2 a & \cos a\sin a\\ \cos a\sin a & \sin^2 a\end{bmatrix}$$

This is the stress tensor of a unit uniaxial tension along the direction \(a\). Then the probability of the outcome along a direction \(\beta\) is \(n_\beta^{\mathsf T}\rho\,n_\beta\), which is the normal stress on the plane whose normal is at \(\beta\):

$$P(\beta) = \sigma_x\cos^2\beta + \sigma_y\sin^2\beta + 2\tau_{xy}\sin\beta\cos\beta = \cos^2(a-\beta)$$

The Born rule for a real state is the stress-transformation formula, to \(10^{-16}\) over 1,000 random states and directions. That the two outcomes of any measurement have probabilities summing to 1 is the first stress invariant, \(\sigma_x + \sigma_y = \operatorname{tr}\rho = 1\) ([Ch20 s04]).

Draw Mohr’s circle for \(\rho\). A uniaxial stress has its circle through the origin: centre \(\tfrac12\), radius \(\tfrac12\), and the point for the \(x\) face at angle \(2a\). Physicists draw a qubit on the Bloch sphere, whose coordinates are \(x = 2\operatorname{Re}(a_0^{*}a_1)\), \(y = 2\operatorname{Im}(a_0^{*}a_1)\), \(z = |a_0|^2 - |a_1|^2\). For a real state, \(y = 0\) and \((x, z) = (\sin 2a, \cos 2a)\), which is exactly twice the Mohr point measured from the centre ([Ch20 s04]). The great circle of the Bloch sphere that real states live on is Mohr’s circle of the density matrix, drawn at double size.

✓Both diagrams double the angle, for the same reason

Sheet 12 §21 explained Mohr’s factor of 2: the stress is quadratic in the direction vector, \(\sigma = n n^{\mathsf T}\) for a uniaxial state, and a quadratic doubles angles. The density matrix is quadratic in the amplitudes in the same way. So the states \(|0\rangle\) and \(|1\rangle\), at 90° as amplitude vectors, sit at opposite ends of the Bloch circle, as two principal planes at 90° sit at opposite ends of Mohr’s circle. A phrase you may meet, that the Bloch sphere halves angles where Mohr’s circle doubles them, has the direction wrong: both diagrams double. What is halved is the angle inside the gate’s matrix. \(R_y(\theta)\) turns the amplitude vector by \(\theta/2\) and moves the Bloch point by \(\theta\), exactly as turning a stress element by \(\theta/2\) moves its Mohr point by \(\theta\) ([Ch20 s04]).

Hence \(R_y(360^\circ) = -I\): a full turn of the Bloch point flips the sign of both amplitudes, and changes no density matrix and no probability. Turning a stress element through 180° does the same to its direction vector, \(n \to -n\), and nothing to the stress. It takes \(R_y(720^\circ)\) to return the amplitudes to where they began.

Sheet 12 §21 put it in the language of labels: an object of label \(\ell\) picks up \(\ell\phi\) of phase when rotated by \(\phi\), with a scalar at 0, a vector at 1 and the stress deviator at 2. A qubit sits half a step below the bottom of that table. The gate that turns the Bloch sphere about its \(z\) axis by \(\phi\) multiplies the two amplitudes by \(e^{-i\phi/2}\) and \(e^{+i\phi/2}\) ([Ch20 s04]): the label is \(\tfrac12\). That is exact, and it is as far as the correspondence goes. Sheet 12’s labels were whole numbers because they describe tensors built from ordinary vectors, and no such tensor has label \(\tfrac12\); that is why a full turn can change a sign.

Mixtures carry over too. A mixed state, a qubit that is in one state or another with some probabilities, has a density matrix that is a biaxial stress, and the length of its Bloch vector is \((\sigma_1 - \sigma_2)/(\sigma_1 + \sigma_2)\), the Mohr diameter over the trace ([Ch20 s04]). The completely uncertain qubit, \(\rho = I/2\), is a hydrostatic stress: Mohr’s circle shrinks to a point at the centre of the Bloch sphere.

!Where it breaks: the third axis, and the count

Phase. Real amplitudes reach only the \(x\)–\(z\) circle. Complex ones fill the sphere, and the \(y\) axis comes from the imaginary part of \(\rho\), which a stress tensor does not have. The state \((1, i)/\sqrt2\), made by \(H\) and then the phase gate \(S = \operatorname{diag}(1, i)\), is a pure state on the \(y\) axis; yet the real part of its density matrix is \(I/2\), so its “Mohr’s circle” is a point, identical to the completely uncertain qubit’s ([Ch20 s04]). Relative phase is what quantum interference is made of (reported), and the analogy is blind to it.

More qubits. Two qubits have 4 amplitudes and \(n\) qubits have \(2^n\), one for every string of outcomes. Storing them takes 16 bytes each: 16 GiB at 30 qubits, 16 PiB at 50 ([Ch20 s04]). No section property grows like that. Entangled states, the ones that cannot be written as one amplitude pair per qubit, live in that space and have no counterpart on this page.

One shot is one bit. A measurement returns 0 or 1, not a probability. Probabilities are estimated from repeated runs, with Sheet 04’s binomial error: \(\sqrt{0.25/1000} = 0.0158\) at \(p = 0.5\) after 1,000 shots. §05 turns that into a cost.

Barren plateaus, with an exact case and a measured one

src 20/01. quantum ML ↗

A parameterised circuit is a fixed arrangement of gates whose rotation angles \(\theta\) are adjustable, playing the part of a network’s weights. Run it, measure, and average the outcomes to get a cost \(C(\theta)\); then adjust the angles by gradient descent, as in Sheet 03 §06. Variational classifiers, VQE and QAOA in the outline are all built this way (reported; none is treated here). Two questions decide whether it can work: how do you get a gradient out of a machine that only returns bits, and how big is that gradient?

The parameter-shift rule is an exact central difference

Each angle enters the amplitudes through \(\cos\frac{\theta}{2}\) and \(\sin\frac{\theta}{2}\), and every probability is quadratic in the amplitudes. So, as a function of one angle with the rest held fixed, the cost is a single sinusoid, \(C(\theta) = A + B\cos\theta + D\sin\theta\). For a sinusoid a central difference with any step \(h\) returns the slope times \(\sin h\):

$$\frac{C(\theta+h) - C(\theta-h)}{2} = \sin h\;\frac{dC}{d\theta} \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \frac{dC}{d\theta} = \frac{C\!\left(\theta+\frac{\pi}{2}\right) - C\!\left(\theta-\frac{\pi}{2}\right)}{2}$$

At \(h = \pi/2\) the factor is 1 and the difference is the derivative, with no truncation error at all. On a four-qubit, three-layer circuit all twelve shifted differences matched the analytic derivative to \(2\times10^{-10}\), the accuracy of the reference itself, and the \(\sin h\) law held for \(h = 0.9\) ([Ch20 s05]). The large step matters because every \(C\) is measured with shot noise. A textbook difference with a tiny \(h\) divides that noise by \(2h\); a quarter-period step does not amplify it at all. It is the same reason you would read the slope of a known sinusoid from two readings a quarter-period either side rather than two readings close together.

An exact barren plateau, with no entanglement in it

Now the size of the gradient. Start every angle at random, uniformly over a full turn, and ask how much \(\partial C/\partial\theta_1\) varies from one start to another. Its average is zero; its variance is the typical squared slope the optimiser will see. Take the simplest circuit there is, one \(R_y\) gate on each of \(n\) qubits and nothing connecting them, and the global cost “did any qubit fail to land on 0?”, \(C = 1 - \prod_i \cos^2(\theta_i/2)\). Then \(\partial C/\partial\theta_1 = \tfrac12\sin\theta_1\prod_{i>1}\cos^2(\theta_i/2)\), the factors are independent, and \(E[\sin^2\theta]/4 = 1/8\) while \(E[\cos^4(\theta/2)] = 3/8\):

$$\operatorname{Var}\frac{\partial C_{\text{global}}}{\partial\theta_1} = \frac18\left(\frac38\right)^{n-1}, \qquad \operatorname{Var}\frac{\partial\langle Z_1\rangle}{\partial\theta_1} = \frac12, \qquad \operatorname{Var}\frac{\partial C_{\text{local}}}{\partial\theta_1} = \frac{1}{8n^2}$$

The middle formula is for measuring qubit 1 alone; the last is the average of the \(n\) single-qubit costs. Sampled with 400,000 random starts for \(n\) = 1 to 6, all three agree with the formulas to within the sampling error, and the circuit simulator reproduces the global gradient draw by draw to \(2\times10^{-16}\) ([Ch20 s05]). Every added qubit multiplies the global variance by 3/8, although no qubit touches any other. A cost that asks one yes-or-no question of every qubit at once is almost always answered “yes”, and so it is flat in every direction. The same circuit with a local cost has a gradient that shrinks only as \(1/n^2\), or not at all.

✓Same structure: Sheet 03’s plateau, now with a number on it

Sheet 03 §08 warned that in many dimensions the trouble is not wrong minima but plateaus where the gradient is tiny and progress stops. A barren plateau is that, measured: the outline’s bullet calls it vanishing gradients, and here the rate of vanishing is a closed form.

Entangling circuits, measured on a simulator

Real variational circuits interleave rotations with entangling gates. The verification script simulates layers of \(R_y\) gates followed by a ladder of CZ gates, as many layers as qubits, storing all \(2^n\) amplitudes, for \(n\) = 2 to 10 with 3,000 random starts up to 8 qubits and 2,000 beyond ([Ch20 s05]):

!What ten qubits can show, and what they cannot

Seven points on a straight line in a log plot are evidence of exponential decay, not a proof of it. Theorems proving exponential decay for circuits random enough, and for local costs once circuits are deep enough, are reported; nothing on this page derives them. Ten qubits cannot tell you whether the single-qubit curve will steepen at 20 or stay gentle, and the depth used here, one layer per qubit, is a choice that changes the answer.

There is a subtler limit. The gradient’s distribution grows heavier-tailed with every qubit: its kurtosis is \(1.5(35/18)^{n-1}\) in the exact case, so the relative error of a variance estimated from 400,000 samples is 1.0% at 6 qubits, 3.9% at 10, and 107% at 20 ([Ch20 s05]). Measuring a vanishing quantity by sampling gets harder exactly as it vanishes, which is why the sampled check above stops at six qubits and the formula carries the rest.

What a plateau costs: Sheet 04’s sample-size formula

The global cost is estimated from shots whose outcomes are 0 or 1, each with variance at most \(\tfrac14\). The parameter-shift gradient is half the difference of two such estimates, so after \(N\) shots of each circuit its standard error is at most \(\sqrt{1/(8N)}\). Seeing a gradient of typical size \(\sqrt{\operatorname{Var}}\) above that noise needs

$$N \gtrsim \frac{1}{8\,\operatorname{Var}}\quad\text{shots per circuit, per angle, per step}$$

which is Sheet 04 §08’s margin of error \(E = z\sigma/\sqrt{N}\) solved for \(N\). For the exact global case that is 6,819 shots at 10 qubits, \(1.2\times10^{8}\) at 20 and \(7.5\times10^{20}\) at 50, growing by 8/3 for each qubit ([Ch20 s05]). An exponentially small gradient is not merely slow to descend; it is exponentially expensive to measure.

A spiking neuron is a furnace and a quench

src 20/02. neuromorphic ↗

The neuromorphic outline names integrate-and-fire models, conversion from ordinary networks, and the latency and accuracy of spiking networks. A leaky integrate-and-fire (LIF) neuron has one state, its membrane voltage \(V\), driven by an input current \(I\):

$$\tau\,\frac{dV}{dt} = -V + R\,I(t); \qquad \text{when } V \text{ reaches } V_{\text{th}}: \text{ emit a spike, reset } V \text{ to } 0$$

Between spikes it is a first-order lag. A spiking network passes these spikes, events in time, from neuron to neuron instead of passing numbers.

✓Identity: the GATE lumped-capacitance problem

A small body heated in a furnace, with Biot number below 0.1, obeys \(\rho c V\,dT/dt = hA(T_\infty - T)\): the same equation, with time constant \(\tau = \rho c V/(hA)\). A steel ball of 10 mm diameter with \(h = 100\) W/m²K has \(\tau = \rho c D/6h = 59.80\) s and \(\mathrm{Bi} = 0.0042\). In a furnace at 900 °C it takes

$$t = \tau\ln\frac{T_\infty - T_0}{T_\infty - T_{\text{th}}} = 59.80\,\ln\frac{900-30}{900-600} = 63.67\ \text{s}$$

to go from 30 °C to 600 °C ([Ch20 s06]). Quench it whenever it reaches 600 °C and put it back, and it is an LIF neuron firing every 63.67 s. In the neuron’s variables the drive is \(u = RI/V_{\text{th}}\), here \((900-30)/(600-30) = 1.526\), and the interval between spikes is \(\tau\ln\big(u/(u-1)\big)\), the same number. A spring and dashpot in parallel creeping under a step load is the same equation once more.

So the firing rate follows from the step response. With a refractory pause \(t_{\text{ref}}\) after each spike:

$$f(u) = \frac{1}{t_{\text{ref}} + \tau\ln\dfrac{u}{u-1}}\quad (u > 1), \qquad f = 0\quad (u \le 1)$$

An exact-step simulation agrees to \(10^{-13}\) for \(u\) from 1.05 to 20 ([Ch20 s06]). Below \(u = 1\) the ball never reaches the quench temperature and the neuron never fires. Just above it the rate rises with unbounded slope. Far above it, with no refractory pause, the rate approaches the straight line \((u - \tfrac12)/\tau\): at \(u = 20\) it is 19.4957/\(\tau\) against 19.5/\(\tau\). With \(t_{\text{ref}} = 2\) ms it saturates near \(1/t_{\text{ref}}\): 495 Hz at \(u = 1000\) ([Ch20 s06]).

Sampled at a fixed step \(\Delta t\) with the input held between samples, the membrane is §01’s smoother, \(V_k = (1-\alpha)V_{k-1} + \alpha u_k\), with \(\alpha = 1 - e^{-\Delta t/\tau}\). That is exact, to \(10^{-15}\); the forward-Euler value \(\alpha = \Delta t/\tau\) is off by 0.019 after 400 steps at \(\Delta t = 1\) ms, \(\tau = 20\) ms ([Ch20 s06]). Sheet 07 and Sheet 08 both met this trap, a discrete object claimed equal to a continuous one; the exact identity is the one with the exponential.

Conversion, and why the leak is taken out

One route to a spiking network is to train an ordinary one and convert it, letting each firing rate stand for an activation. Sheet 06’s activation is the ReLU, \(\max(0, z)\). Remove the leak, so the neuron integrates its input exactly, and on reset subtract the threshold instead of returning to zero. Driven by a constant input \(a\) per step against a threshold of 1, it fires exactly \(\lfloor aT\rfloor\) spikes in \(T\) steps when \(a > 0\) and none when \(a \le 0\), checked on 3,000 random inputs ([Ch20 s06]). Its rate is a ReLU, with an error below \(1/T\). Conversion methods use this non-leaky neuron (reported), and the derivation says why.

!Where it breaks: the leaky neuron is not a ReLU

At the same current the LIF neuron fires at 0.379 of the non-leaky rate at \(u = 1.1\), 0.721 at \(u = 2\), 0.896 at \(u = 5\) and 0.949 at \(u = 10\) ([Ch20 s06]). Its kink sits at \(u = 1\), not at zero; its slope there is infinite, not 1; and its straight part is offset by \(1/(2\tau)\). A network trained with ReLUs and run on leaky neurons runs with a different activation function.

Reset to zero breaks it too. Returning to 0 throws away whatever the voltage had overshot the threshold by, and the rate becomes \(1/\lceil 1/a\rceil\), a staircase: an input of 0.4 fires at 1/3, 17% low ([Ch20 s06]). Subtracting keeps the overshoot for the next spike.

✓Identity: rate coding is a frequency counter

A frequency counter counts pulses for a gate time \(T\) and reports count over time, with a resolution of one count, \(1/T\). A rate-coded neuron is read the same way. The mean error over random inputs is \(1/(2T)\): measured 0.0491, 0.00498 and 0.000509 for \(T\) = 10, 100 and 1,000 steps ([Ch20 s06]). One per cent resolution costs about a hundred time steps of waiting per layer. That is the latency–accuracy trade in the outline’s last bullet, derived; the energy side of the comparison is reported, not derived here.

!Where it breaks: a spike has no slope

Training by gradient needs the output to change smoothly with the input. A spike count is a step function: nudging the input by \(10^{-6}\) changed the count over 100 steps in none of 10,000 random cases ([Ch20 s06]). Sheet 03 §08 excused the ReLU’s kink because it is one point; a step function is flat everywhere else, so the gradient is zero almost everywhere. Surrogate-gradient training substitutes a smooth bump for the step’s derivative in the backward pass only, while the forward pass still spikes (reported). Learning from spike timing (STDP) is not treated.

Worked example: one critical casting, two suppliers

written for this sheet

A plant buys a critical casting. Supplier A’s monthly delivery falls short of the order by a random amount with a standard deviation of 8% of what was ordered from it; supplier B’s by 12%. The two shortfalls are correlated at 0.3, since both foundries buy sand from the same pit. Separately, each supplier has a 4% chance in a year of a total stoppage, a fire or a strike, independently of the other. Two questions. How should the order be split to make the monthly shortfall as steady as possible? And against a total stoppage, is dual-sourcing safer than buying everything from A?

Solved as the plant engineer

Steadiness. With a fraction \(w\) ordered from A, the shortfall is a weighted stack of two correlated deviations, Sheet 04 §05 with the covariance term kept: \(\sigma^2 = w^2\sigma_A^2 + (1-w)^2\sigma_B^2 + 2w(1-w)\rho\sigma_A\sigma_B\). Set its derivative to zero:

$$w = \frac{\sigma_B^2 - \rho\sigma_A\sigma_B}{\sigma_A^2 + \sigma_B^2 - 2\rho\sigma_A\sigma_B} = \frac{144 - 28.8}{64 + 144 - 57.6} = 0.766, \qquad \sigma = 7.467\%$$

Order 76.6% from A. The gain over A alone is small, 7.47% against 8%, and an even split is worse than A alone, 8.15% ([Ch19 s07]): with unequal suppliers and a shared cause, splitting evenly adds B’s larger wobble faster than it averages anything away.

Stoppage. Draw the reliability block diagram. “No castings at all” needs both suppliers down: a parallel system, probability \(0.04^2 = 0.0016\), against 0.04 for a single source, 25 times better. “Some castings missing” needs either down: a series system, \(1 - 0.96^2 = 0.0784\), nearly twice the single source’s 0.04. Dual sourcing trades a rare catastrophe for a more frequent partial loss, at the same expected loss of 4% of supply.

Solved as the risk manager

Steadiness is the minimum-variance portfolio of §02, with covariance \(\Sigma = \begin{psmallmatrix}64 & 28.8\\ 28.8 & 144\end{psmallmatrix}\): \(w^\ast = \Sigma^{-1}\mathbf{1}/(\mathbf{1}^{\mathsf T}\Sigma^{-1}\mathbf{1})\) gives 0.766 and 0.234, the same numbers, because it is the same calculation. Stoppage is a tail question, so the report gives VaR and ES at 95%, with the loss measured as the percentage of supply lost:

✓Same arithmetic, and two answers that are both right

The engineer’s stack-up and the manager’s portfolio formula are one calculation; the block diagram and the tail measures are another. What the example adds is that the two questions have different answers: 76.6 / 23.4 is the steadiest split and 50 / 50 the one that best protects against a stoppage. No formula chooses between them. Which risk the plant can least afford, a wobbling line or a stopped one, is a judgement, and a risk number that silently answers it for you, as VaR does here, is the dangerous kind.

Question bank

whole sheet

Twenty-four prompts in three tiers. Answer before opening.

Tier 1 — recall
R1Write exponential smoothing as a first-order lag. What are its weights, its gain, and the mean age of its data?

\(s_k = (1-\alpha)s_{k-1} + \alpha x_k\). The weights are \(\alpha(1-\alpha)^j\), its discrete impulse response; they sum to 1, the unit gain; their centroid, the mean age, is \((1-\alpha)/\alpha\).

R2Exponential smoothing is the steady state of which Kalman filter, and what is the best \(\alpha\)?

A random-walk level, \(F = H = 1\), seen through white noise. \(\alpha^\ast = (\sqrt{\lambda^2+4\lambda} - \lambda)/2\) with \(\lambda = q/r\): 0.618 when \(q = r\), and 1 when there is no measurement noise.

R3Give the minimum-variance weights and variance. Which formula from an earlier sheet is it?

\(w^\ast = \Sigma^{-1}\mathbf{1}/(\mathbf{1}^{\mathsf T}\Sigma^{-1}\mathbf{1})\), \(\sigma^2_{\min} = 1/(\mathbf{1}^{\mathsf T}\Sigma^{-1}\mathbf{1})\). Sheet 09’s MVDR beamformer with the steering vector all ones, by Sheet 03 §07’s Lagrange multiplier.

R4Define VaR and expected shortfall. Which one is a centroid?

VaRα is the \(\alpha\)-quantile of the loss. ESα is the mean loss over the worst \(1-\alpha\) of outcomes, which is the centroid of the tail area.

R5State the standard error of a Monte Carlo VaR and say what each factor does.

\(\sqrt{\alpha(1-\alpha)/N}\,/\,f(\text{VaR}_\alpha)\). The numerator is the binomial error of an estimated fraction; dividing by the density at the quantile turns a probability error into a loss error, and the density is small in the tail.

R6What is the density matrix of the real state \((\cos a, \sin a)\), read as a stress?

\(\psi\psi^{\mathsf T}\), a unit uniaxial tension along \(a\). Its Mohr’s circle has centre and radius \(\tfrac12\); the Bloch circle is the same circle at double size.

R7State the parameter-shift rule and say why it is exact.

\(dC/d\theta = [C(\theta+\pi/2) - C(\theta-\pi/2)]/2\). As a function of one angle the cost is a single sinusoid, and a central difference of a sinusoid returns \(\sin h\) times the slope; \(h = \pi/2\) makes the factor 1.

R8Write the LIF interspike interval, and the furnace problem it is.

\(t_{\text{ref}} + \tau\ln\big(u/(u-1)\big)\) with \(u = RI/V_{\text{th}}\). A lumped body heated from \(T_0\) towards \(T_\infty\) reaches \(T_{\text{th}}\) after \(\tau\ln\big((T_\infty-T_0)/(T_\infty-T_{\text{th}})\big)\), and quenching it at \(T_{\text{th}}\) is the reset.

Tier 2 — apply
A1A reading drifts as a random walk with step variance 0.01 and carries noise of variance 1. What smoothing constant, and what moving average removes the same noise?

\(\lambda = 0.01\), so \(\alpha^\ast = 0.0951\). The equal-noise moving average has \((2-\alpha)/\alpha = 20.0\) points.

A2An EWMA chart uses \(\lambda = 0.2\) and \(L = 3\). Where are its steady-state limits?

\(\mu_0 \pm 3\sigma\sqrt{0.2/1.8} = \mu_0 \pm 1.000\sigma\).

A3Assets of 20% and 30% volatility, correlation 0.2. Minimum-variance weights and volatility? Above what correlation is the second asset sold short?

73.6% and 26.4%, volatility 18.06%. Short when \(\rho > 0.2/0.3 = 2/3\); at 0.8 the weight is −23.5%.

A4Twenty-five equally weighted assets, each of volatility \(\sigma\), every pair correlated at 0.3. Portfolio volatility, and the floor?

\(\sigma\sqrt{0.3 + 0.7/25} = 0.573\sigma\). The floor is \(\sigma\sqrt{0.3} = 0.548\sigma\); 400 assets reach 0.549\(\sigma\).

A5Loans each default independently with probability 1%. How many before the 95% VaR of the pool is no longer zero?

Six: \(1 - 0.99^5 = 0.049\) is inside the 5%, \(1 - 0.99^6 = 0.059\) is not. It is series reliability crossing a 5% limit.

A6A real qubit at amplitude angle 30° is measured along 0°. Probability of 0, Bloch angle, and the stress reading?

\(\cos^2 30^\circ = 0.75\). The Bloch point is at 60°. On Mohr’s circle of \(\rho\) the plane at 0° carries a normal stress of 0.75, the same number.

A7Ten qubits, one \(R_y\) each, global cost. Gradient variance, and shots per circuit to see a typical gradient?

\(\tfrac18(3/8)^9 = 1.83\times10^{-5}\). About \(1/(8\times1.83\times10^{-5}) = 6{,}819\) shots per circuit, per angle, per step, and 8/3 times more for each added qubit.

A8An LIF neuron with \(\tau = 20\) ms is driven at \(u = 2\). Interval, rate, and the rate without the leak?

\(20\ln 2 = 13.86\) ms, so 72.1 Hz. Without the leak the rate is \(u/\tau = 100\) Hz; the leaky neuron fires at 0.721 of it.

Tier 3 — judge
J1A colleague picked a smoothing constant by eye on last year’s data and calls it optimal. Your response?

Optimal for what model? For a random walk in noise the best \(\alpha\) follows from \(q/r\), which can be estimated, or found by minimising one-step error. Then check that the forecast errors are white, Sheet 11’s innovation test; if there is a trend, no \(\alpha\) is optimal, because the flat forecast lags it.

J2“Diversification is free; just add assets.” When does it stop working?

When the common cause dominates. With \(\rho = 0.3\), 25 assets leave 0.573\(\sigma\) and 400 leave 0.549\(\sigma\), against a floor of 0.548\(\sigma\). It is RSS stacking meeting a tool-wear drift: averaging removes only the independent part.

J3An optimiser recommends a large short position. Do you trust it?

A short is legitimate when a correlated asset can cancel risk, like a dummy gauge. But the weights come from an estimated \(\Sigma^{-1}\): with 20 assets and 60 months the optimum carries 1.49 times the true minimum variance on average. Test how much the weights move between sub-periods before believing them, or constrain them.

J4After you split a loan between two borrowers, the risk report’s 95% VaR rises from 0 to 50. Did risk rise?

Not in any sense that matters. VaR is not subadditive: the chance of some loss rose past 5%, a series effect, while the chance of losing everything fell from 4% to 0.16%. ES fell from 80 to 51.6. Report ES alongside VaR.

J5“The Bloch sphere halves angles where Mohr’s circle doubles them.” Correct it.

Both diagrams double: orthogonal states sit 180° apart on the Bloch circle as principal planes do on Mohr’s. What is halved is the angle inside the gate: \(R_y(\theta)\) turns the amplitudes by \(\theta/2\) and the Bloch point by \(\theta\), as turning a stress element by \(\theta/2\) moves its Mohr point by \(\theta\).

J6Can Mohr’s circle stand in for a qubit in general?

No. It covers real amplitudes only, the \(x\)–\(z\) circle. The pure state \((1, i)/\sqrt2\) has a real density-matrix part of \(I/2\), a Mohr’s circle shrunk to a point, indistinguishable from complete uncertainty. Phase, entanglement and the \(2^n\) growth are all outside it.

J7A ten-qubit simulation shows a local cost’s gradient variance falling only 0.17 bits per qubit. Has it shown that local costs avoid barren plateaus?

No. It is ten qubits at one depth, and sampling error grows with \(n\). Reported theorems say sufficiently deep circuits plateau for local costs too. What the page does prove is narrower: for the circuit without entanglement, the global cost decays as \((3/8)^n\) and the averaged local cost only as \(1/n^2\).

J8A ReLU network converted to leaky spiking neurons loses accuracy. Name three causes this sheet predicts.

The leak bends the rate curve and moves its kink to \(u = 1\) (0.721 of the intended rate at \(u = 2\)); reset to zero turns rates into a staircase (0.4 fires at 1/3); and too few time steps leave a counting error of about \(1/(2T)\). The derivation says to use non-leaky neurons, reset by subtraction, and enough steps.

The map, and the end of the series

beyond the chapters

Every section of this coda was paid for by an earlier sheet. The map draws each debt. The first-order lag sits in the top row with an arrow into each chapter, because it is the one piece of mathematics both chapters turned out to share.

Where the series ends

Sheet 01 began with forces as vectors and Mohr’s circle as a change of basis. The last derivation in the series puts a qubit on that same circle. In between, nearly every sheet found that a piece of machine learning was a calculation a mechanical engineer had already done: a stiffness matrix, a tolerance stack, a reliability diagram, a servo, a line-balancing heuristic. This coda found it again, in two chapters that were never written.

It also found the series’ most persistent lesson one last time: the bridge is exact up to the point where the subject becomes new, and then it ends. Mohr’s circle holds every real qubit state and none of the phase. The furnace holds the neuron’s charging and none of its learning. The stack-up holds diversification and none of the estimation. Sheet 07 said this about attention and state-space models; Sheet 12 said it about graph attention. It is true of every page in between, and the break boxes are where each page says so.

Sheets 15 to 18 turn from mathematics to the engineering of software, hardware, inference and systems. The coda draws on them once, Sheet 17 §08’s rounding bias in §02, and does not summarise them. There is no Sheet 21.
