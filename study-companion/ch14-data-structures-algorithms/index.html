Counting the Work

Big O is an order-of-magnitude estimate, and you have made thousands of those. The CPM forward pass you drew for GATE is dynamic programming, line for line. A shear-force diagram is a prefix sum. And a hash table at 90% full hits the same cliff as a machine at 90% utilisation.

Sheet 13 left two debts for this one. Part 1 §06 stated recurrences and the Master Theorem and promised they would be done properly here. Part 1 §09 said the heuristics in your industrial engineering syllabus exist because the exact problems are NP-hard, and promised the algorithms behind them. This part pays both: §07 solves a recurrence exactly and finds the source’s own number off by a factor of 27,643, and §11 shows an NP-hard problem that is solved routinely, and why that does not rescue line balancing.

The headline is in §09. Dynamic programming is usually taught as a clever trick for interview puzzles. You learned it years ago under another name. The CPM forward pass, \(TE_j = \max_i (TE_i + t_{ij})\), taken in precedence order, is dynamic programming: the same recurrence, the same iteration order, the same backtracking to recover the answer. A project network with 40 parallel stages has more than a trillion paths, and the forward pass adds 160 numbers. That gap is the entire subject.

Three more correspondences are exact rather than decorative. A shear-force diagram is a prefix sum, and a range query is the difference of two shears (§18). The water trapped between bars is set by the lower spill point, and in two dimensions that becomes a minimax path problem the source’s method cannot see (§16). A hash table filling up behaves like a machine loading up: fine at 50%, bad at 90%, with a cliff whose shape you already know from queueing (§13).

01

What a pattern is, and what it is not

src 00. foundations ↗

The source opens with advice about interviews: learn about fifteen to twenty patterns (two pointers, sliding window, depth-first search, dynamic programming and so on), and learn to strip the story off a problem until the pattern shows. Its example is good. Two numbers summing to a target, two molecules whose energies sum to a threshold, two accounts that cover a debt: one problem, which is “look up a complement”.

You already work this way. A GATE question about a flywheel, a punch press and an engine cylinder can all be the same energy balance. Recognising the governing equation under the story is most of the skill, and the source is right that memorising solutions does not transfer while understanding why a method works does.

!Where the claim overreaches

The source says every problem, “no matter how novel it looks”, reduces to one or a combination of these patterns. That is a useful heuristic for interview questions, which are written to be solvable in 45 minutes. It is not a theorem, and it is false for problems in general. Sheet 13 part 1 §08 showed problems no algorithm solves at all, and its §09 a large class nobody knows how to solve efficiently. Treat the pattern list as the common cases of a catalogue, the way a design handbook covers common joints without claiming every joint is in it.

The “~40% of interview problems” figure for arrays and hash maps (file 01) is the source’s estimate. Nothing here measures it.

02

Big O is an order-of-magnitude statement

src 00. foundations ↗

An algorithm’s cost is a function of its input size \(n\): the number of basic steps it takes. Big O keeps the fastest-growing term of that function and throws away its constant. You have done exactly this in fluid mechanics, when a boundary-layer equation keeps the terms of order one and drops the terms of order \(\delta/L\). The formal definition:

$$\begin{aligned} f(n) = O\big(g(n)\big) \iff\ &\exists\, c > 0,\ n_0 \ \text{such that} \\ &f(n) \le c\,g(n)\ \text{for all}\ n \ge n_0 \end{aligned}$$

Read it carefully, because it says less than people use it for. It is an upper bound only. An algorithm that takes \(3n\) steps is \(O(n)\), and it is also, truthfully, \(O(n^2)\) and \(O(2^n)\). When the source writes that merge sort “is \(O(n\log n)\)” it means the bound is tight, which has its own symbol:

$$f(n) = \Theta\big(g(n)\big) \iff c_1\,g(n) \le f(n) \le c_2\,g(n)\ \text{for all}\ n \ge n_0$$

\(\Theta\) is the scaling law. The square–cube law says a beam’s self-weight stress grows as \(\Theta(L)\) when every dimension scales with \(L\): bounded above and below by a multiple of \(L\). That is the statement engineers mean, and it is usually what programmers mean when they write \(O\). The distinction matters in §07, where the source uses a correct upper bound as if it were the growth rate and overstates a cost by four orders of magnitude.

!An algorithm’s cost is not a problem’s difficulty

The source says Big O captures “the intrinsic difficulty of the problem”. It captures the cost of one algorithm. Sorting has an \(O(n^2)\) algorithm and an \(O(n\log n)\) one; the problem did not change between them. The difficulty of a problem is the cost of the best possible algorithm, a lower bound, and lower bounds are much harder to prove. §15 meets one the source asserts and nobody has proved.

Constants lose, exponents win

Dropping the constant is justified by what happens at large \(n\), and only there. Suppose a clever \(n\log_2 n\) method costs \(c\) times more per step than a simple \(n^2\) one. The clever method wins once \(n^2 > c\,n\log_2 n\), that is, once \(n > c\log_2 n\). The crossover grows barely faster than \(c\) itself: at \(c = 10\) it is \(n^* = 59\), at \(c = 100\) it is 996, at \(c = 1000\) it is 13,747 ([Ch14 s02]). A hundredfold handicap costs about a thousand in input size, and after that the higher exponent loses forever. This is the square–cube argument: no constant saves the larger exponent.

The same fact has a practical face. Below the crossover the simple method is faster, and real libraries use it there: several standard sorting routines switch to insertion sort, an \(n^2\) method, for short runs.

Reading an exponent off a log–log plot

You find the exponent of a heat-transfer correlation by plotting \(\log Nu\) against \(\log Re\) and reading the slope. You can find an algorithm’s exponent the same way: time it at \(n\) and \(2n\), and the slope is \(\log_2\!\big(T(2n)/T(n)\big)\). For \(n^k\) this is exactly \(k\). For \(n\log n\) it is not 1:

$$\text{slope} = \log_2\frac{2n\log_2 2n}{n\log_2 n} = 1 + \log_2\!\left(1 + \frac{1}{\log_2 n}\right)$$

That is 1.138 at \(n = 10^3\), 1.071 at \(10^6\) and still 1.047 at \(10^9\) ([Ch14 s02]). A log–log fit to an \(n\log n\) algorithm reports an exponent a little above 1 and drifting slowly down. If you did not know better you would call it \(n^{1.1}\), and you would be fitting the wrong model, the way a single power law fitted across laminar and turbulent data gives an exponent that belongs to neither. Exponential growth does worse: \(T(2n)/T(n) = 2^n\) is not constant at all, so \(2^n\) bends upward on log–log paper and is straight only on semi-log.

03

Hidden loops and hidden copies

src 00. foundations ↗

Counting steps goes wrong when one line of code hides a loop. The source’s first example is correct and worth remembering: x in some_list walks the list element by element, so it costs \(O(n)\), and putting it inside a loop over \(n\) items gives \(O(n^2)\). x in some_set is a hash lookup (§13) and costs \(O(1)\) on average. Converting the list to a set once, before the loop, turns an \(O(n^2)\) program into an \(O(n)\) one without changing a single result.

Its second example is the one to be careful with. Files 00 and 01 both say that building a string with s += c in a loop “copies the entire string each time”, so \(n\) additions cost \(1 + 2 + \dots + n = O(n^2)\). Measured, it does not ([Ch14 s03]):

  • A plain loop appending one character at a time: four times the length took 4.12× the time. That is linear. Quadratic would be 16×.
  • The same loop with one extra line, t = s, so that a second name refers to the string: four times the length took 23.96× the time. That is quadratic, plus some cache penalty.

The difference is an optimisation inside CPython, the standard Python interpreter. When nothing else refers to s, it extends the string in place instead of copying it, because nobody could observe the difference. The moment a second reference exists, it must copy, and the source’s arithmetic becomes exactly right. The timings above come from the machine that ran the verification script and move a little from run to run; the gap between 4× and 16× does not.

!The advice survives, for a different reason

Collecting pieces in a list and calling "".join(parts) once is still the right habit. Python’s own style guide, PEP 8, tells programmers not to rely on this in-place optimisation, because it is fragile even in CPython and absent from other Python implementations (reported from the guide, not measured here). So the source reaches the right rule with the wrong mechanism, and a reader who times it will think the rule is a myth. It is not. The fast path is an accident of one interpreter, and one extra reference switches it off.

The third trap in the source is sound: if a program sorts, \(O(n\log n)\), and then scans, \(O(n)\), the total is \(O(n\log n)\). The largest term governs a sum, which is the same judgement as ignoring the kinetic-energy term in a pipe-flow energy balance when the velocity is small.

04

Amortised cost is a setup spread over a lot

src 01. arrays and hashing ↗

A dynamic array is a list that can grow: Python’s list, C++’s vector. Underneath it is a fixed block of memory with some spare slots. An append drops the new item into the next spare slot, which costs one step. When there are no spare slots, the array allocates a bigger block and copies every item across, which costs as many steps as there are items. Then appends are cheap again until the new block fills.

✓This is setup cost amortised over a lot

A press that needs a two-hour die change and then runs a lot of 100 parts carries 1.2 minutes of setup per part. Nobody says the press “takes two hours per part”. The copy is the die change; the cheap appends are the run. Amortised cost is the total cost of a sequence of operations divided by their number, and that is precisely how a setup cost is loaded onto each part.

The source says the array doubles its size when full, so a resize happens at 1, 2, 4, 8, … items, and the copies up to \(n\) items total at most \(1 + 2 + 4 + \dots\) \(< 2n\). Two copies per append at worst, whatever \(n\) is: \(O(1)\) amortised. For any growth factor \(g > 1\) the same geometric sum gives

$$\frac{\text{copies}}{\text{appends}} \;\le\; \frac{g}{g-1}$$

and the simulation reaches that bound exactly: a worst-ever ratio of 9.00, 3.00 and 2.00 for \(g = 1.125, 1.5, 2\) ([Ch14 s04]).

!Python does not double

The source names Python’s list as an example of doubling. Watching CPython’s actual memory use as a list grows, its capacity steps through 4, 8, 16, 24, 32, 40, 52, 64, 76, 92, … and at large sizes each step is 1.125 to 1.131 times the last ([Ch14 s04]). It over-allocates by about an eighth. That is still geometric, so append is still \(O(1)\) amortised; the price is about 8 copies per append over a long run instead of 1. The claim the source needs is true. The example it gives is not.

!Where the setup analogy breaks, and why the lots must grow

A die change costs the same two hours whether the next lot is 100 parts or 1,000. This “setup” does not: it copies everything already on hand, so it gets dearer as the array grows. That is why the lots must grow geometrically. Grow by a fixed 100 slots each time and doubling \(n\) multiplies the total copies by 4.00 ([Ch14 s04]): the cost is quadratic and each append costs \(O(n)\). A fixed lot size amortises a fixed setup. A setup that grows with stock needs a lot that grows with it.

Bigger lots mean fewer setups and more idle capacity, which is the inventory trade-off. Averaged over sizes from 100 to about 300,000, the fraction of allocated slots standing empty is 5.7% at \(g = 1.125\), 17.7% at 1.5, 27.8% at 2 and 39.6% at 3 ([Ch14 s04]). The curve has the shape of an EOQ cost curve. It does not have an EOQ optimum, because nobody has put a price on a copy against an idle slot; libraries pick different factors because they weigh memory and time differently.

One more distinction the source makes correctly: amortised is not worst case. The single append that triggers a copy of 260,000 items takes 260,000 steps. Amortised \(O(1)\) is a guarantee about the total of any sequence of appends, not about each one. A 1 kHz control loop that must finish every cycle in one millisecond cares about the worst single step, and a dynamic array growing inside it will eventually miss a deadline.

05

Space, and the stack you do not see

src 00. foundations ↗

Memory is counted the same way as time. The part that is easy to miss is the call stack: every function call that has started and not yet returned keeps a small record of where it was and what its variables held. Sheet 13 part 3 §03 called this the bench, where half-finished jobs sit until they are picked up again. A recursion that goes \(d\) calls deep keeps \(d\) records on the bench at once, so it uses \(O(d)\) memory even if each call allocates nothing else.

That makes the depth of a recursion a memory cost, and it is where the source’s one-line statement “Quicksort is \(O(\log n)\) space” needs a condition. Quicksort picks a pivot, splits the data into the items below it and the items above it, and sorts each side by calling itself (part 2 of this sheet builds it properly). Its depth depends on how evenly the pivot splits ([Ch14 s05]):

  • Written the plain way, with the last item as the pivot, on already sorted input of 2,000 items: every split is 1,999 against 0, and the recursion goes 1,999 calls deep. That is \(O(n)\) space.
  • The same code on shuffled input: 23 deep, about \(2\log_2 n\).
  • Written to recurse into the smaller side and loop over the larger one: never deeper than \(\log_2 n\), because each recursive call at least halves the size. On the sorted input it stayed at depth 1.

So the \(O(\log n)\) is a property of the careful implementation, or an expectation for random input, not a guarantee for the algorithm as usually first written. It has a very practical consequence here. CPython refuses to go more than 1,000 calls deep by default ([Ch14 s05]), so the plain recursive quicksort, handed the easiest input there is (a list already in order), crashes with a RecursionError.

06

Recursion is induction, run as a program

src 00. foundations ↗

A recursive function solves a problem by calling itself on a smaller copy of it. It has two parts: a base case small enough to answer directly, and a recursive case that builds the answer for size \(n\) from answers for smaller sizes. The source’s advice for writing one is to “trust the recursion”: assume the smaller call returns the right answer, and check only that your combining step is right.

✓“Trust the recursion” is the inductive hypothesis

That advice is proof by induction, stated as a coding tip. To prove a statement \(P(n)\) for every \(n\), you prove \(P(0)\), then prove that \(P(n-1)\) implies \(P(n)\). To write a correct recursive function you make the base case right, then make the recursive case right assuming the smaller call is. It is one argument, used once to establish a fact and once to compute it.

Sheet 13 part 1 §04 found the same argument in the simple truss: start from a triangle, add two members and a joint at a time, and \(m = 2j - 3\) holds at every stage. The source’s recursive list reversal has exactly that shape. Reversing an empty or one-node list is trivial; reversing the rest and hooking the first node onto the end reverses the whole. The rule in the pitfall table, “ensure the subproblem is strictly smaller”, is the requirement that the induction actually descends to the base case.

Every recursive program can be rewritten as a loop that keeps its own explicit stack of pending work. Recursion is clearer when the problem is shaped like a tree (part 2 of this sheet is full of them). A loop is safer when the depth could be large, since each level costs a stack record (§05).

A tail call is a recursive call that is the very last thing a function does, so nothing is left to finish afterwards. Some languages spot this and reuse the same stack record, turning the recursion into a loop automatically. Python does not, as the source says: a tail-recursive function called 5,000 deep still raises RecursionError ([Ch14 s06]).

07

Counting Fibonacci’s calls, properly

src 00. foundations ↗

The source motivates dynamic programming with the Fibonacci numbers, \(F_n = F_{n-1} + F_{n-2}\), computed by a function that calls itself twice. It notes, correctly, that fib(5) computes fib(3) twice and fib(2) three times ([Ch14 s07]). Then it says the cost is \(O(2^n)\) and that fib(50) “takes over \(10^{15}\) operations”.

Sheet 13 part 1 §06 promised recurrences done properly, and this is a good one to do, because it can be solved exactly. Let \(C(n)\) be the number of calls made by fib(n). One call for itself, plus everything its two sub-calls make:

$$C(n) = 1 + C(n-1) + C(n-2), \qquad C(0) = C(1) = 1$$

Add 1 to both sides and write \(D(n) = C(n) + 1\). The stray 1 disappears and \(D(n) = D(n-1) + D(n-2)\) with \(D(0) = D(1) = 2\). That is twice the Fibonacci recurrence, started from \(2F_1\) and \(2F_2\), so

$$C(n) = 2F_{n+1} - 1$$

Instrumenting the code confirms it for every \(n\) from 0 to 22 ([Ch14 s07]). Now the growth rate. Guess \(D(n) = r^n\), the same guess you make for \(m\ddot{x} + c\dot{x} + kx = 0\) when you try \(x = e^{rt}\). The recurrence becomes \(r^2 = r + 1\), whose larger root is the golden ratio \(\varphi = (1+\sqrt5)/2 = 1.618\). The ratio of successive call counts at \(n = 40\) is 1.618034, equal to \(\varphi\) to six places. The cost is \(\Theta(\varphi^n)\).

!The source’s number is 27,643 times too large

\(C(50) = 2F_{51} - 1 = 40{,}730{,}022{,}147\), about \(4.07\times10^{10}\). The source’s “over \(10^{15}\)” is \(2^{50} = 1.13\times10^{15}\): it took the upper bound \(O(2^n)\) and used it as the count. \(O(2^n)\) is a true statement, since \(\varphi < 2\), but it is not tight, and over 50 levels the gap between \(1.618^n\) and \(2^n\) compounds to a factor of 27,643. This is §02’s warning about \(O\) and \(\Theta\), with a number attached.

It changes the practical verdict too. At the 73 ns per call measured on the verification machine, naive fib(50) takes about 0.8 hours. Slow and silly, but not the “infeasible” the source calls it. Each extra 10 in \(n\) multiplies the time by \(\varphi^{10} \approx 123\), so fib(60) is about four days and fib(100) is out of reach. The source’s conclusion, that you must stop recomputing, survives. Its number does not.

The fix is to remember answers. Store each fib(k) the first time it is computed and look it up after that; now each value is computed once, and the cost falls to \(O(n)\). That is memoisation, the subject of §10, and the source’s version returns the correct 12,586,269,025 for fib(50). Or fill a table from \(F_0\) upward in a loop, which is tabulation. Both are dynamic programming, and the next three sections are about what that is.

08

Backtracking, and what pruning actually buys

src 00. foundations ↗

Backtracking builds a solution one decision at a time. At each step it makes a choice, goes on to the next decision, and when it comes back it undoes the choice and tries the next one. The source’s three steps, choose, explore, unchoose, are the whole method. It enumerates a tree of partial solutions depth first, and without further help it visits every node of that tree, which is exponential.

Pruning is the help: test a partial solution as soon as a decision is made, and if it already breaks a rule, abandon everything below it. You do this in design without calling it anything. When a gear pair is being chosen, the tooth count is checked for interference the moment it is picked; nobody works out the centre distance, the bearing loads and the housing for a 12-tooth pinion that was never going to mesh. The value of a check depends on how early it can fire.

The source’s example is N-Queens: place \(n\) queens on an \(n\times n\) board so that no two share a row, a column or a diagonal. Place one queen per row, and the decision at each row is which column. Counting nodes visited for \(n = 8\) ([Ch14 s08]):

The critical path you computed was dynamic programming

src 00. foundations ↗

The source defines dynamic programming by two properties. Optimal substructure: the best answer to the whole problem is built from best answers to smaller problems. Overlapping subproblems: the same smaller problems come up again and again, so each should be solved once and remembered. It then gives a five-step recipe: define the state, write the recurrence, fix the base case, choose an order that computes each state after the states it depends on, and discard what is no longer needed.

Here is the forward pass of the critical path method, as you drew it for GATE. Events are numbered so that every activity runs from a lower number to a higher one. The start event has \(TE_1 = 0\). Every other event takes the latest of its incoming arrivals:

$$TE_j = \max_{\text{arrows } i \to j} \big(TE_i + t_{ij}\big)$$

Put the two side by side and there is nothing left to translate.

Both properties hold, and you have relied on them without naming them. Optimal substructure: the longest path to event \(j\) ends with some arrow \(i \to j\), and the part before that arrow must itself be a longest path to \(i\), or it could be swapped for a longer one. Checked: on 300 random project networks, the forward pass equals the longest path found by listing every path, and walking back along the maximising arrows recovers a path of that length ([Ch14 s09]). Nothing here is an analogy. It is one algorithm that operations research and computer science named separately.

The saving is the whole point. A network of \(k\) stages, each splitting into two parallel activities and rejoining, has \(2^k\) start-to-finish paths. The forward pass makes four additions per stage. At \(k = 20\) that is 1,048,576 paths against 80 additions; at \(k = 40\), 1,099,511,627,776 paths against 160 ([Ch14 s09]). Enumerating paths is exponential, and the forward pass is linear in the number of arrows. That gap is what the word “dynamic programming” buys.

The source’s worked example, House Robber, is the same idea on a line: take the largest sum of numbers from a row with no two neighbours taken. The state is the best total using the first \(i\) numbers, and the recurrence chooses between skipping item \(i\) and taking it:

$$dp_i = \max\big(dp_{i-1},\; dp_{i-2} + x_i\big)$$

It matches a brute-force search over every allowed subset on 300 random rows ([Ch14 s09]). It is a forward pass over a network whose events are the positions in the row.

!Where it breaks: PERT, because expectation does not pass through max

PERT runs the same forward pass on expected durations and reports the result as the expected project length. That step is not valid. Two parallel paths, each taking a random time with mean 10 and standard deviation 2, merge at one event. The forward pass on means says the merge happens at 10. The expected merge time is \(E[\max(X_1, X_2)] = 10 + 2/\sqrt{\pi} = 11.128\); simulation gives 11.125. With five parallel paths it is 12.33 ([Ch14 s09]).

DP works because \(\max\) and \(+\) can be applied one stage at a time. The expected value of a maximum is always at least the maximum of the expected values, and strictly more whenever the paths are uncertain. So PERT’s single-critical-path estimate is optimistic, and more so the more near-critical paths the network has. The recurrence is exact for fixed durations and biased for random ones.

!Where it breaks: the network must have no loops

Step 4 of the recipe needs an order in which every state is computed after the states it uses. A precedence loop (A before B, B before A) has no such order: the forward pass cannot start, and a recursive version would never reach its base case. That is why CPM software rejects a loop as an error. It is also why “longest path” is easy here and hard in general: on a graph with loops, the longest path that never repeats a node is NP-hard (reported), and no forward pass finds it.

Memoisation, and the MRP low-level code

src 00. foundations ↗

DP comes in two forms. Top-down, or memoisation: write the plain recursion, and keep a table of answers already computed so that a repeated call becomes a lookup. Bottom-up, or tabulation: work out an order in which every subproblem’s inputs are ready before it, and fill the table in that order with a loop. The source’s comparison table is right on each point. Top-down computes only the subproblems that are actually reached and risks a deep call stack; bottom-up computes everything up to the target and makes it easy to throw away rows no longer needed.

✓An MRP explosion is tabulation in topological order

Explode a bill of materials recursively: for each item, multiply the requirement by the quantity per parent, and recurse into its components. That gives the right totals, but it visits a shared component once for every path by which the product reaches it. On a nine-item product structure where one fastener appears through eight different sub-assembly paths, the recursive explosion visits 27 item-nodes to process 9 items; the totals match ([Ch14 s10]).

MRP avoids this with the low-level code: each item is coded with the lowest level at which it appears in any bill, and items are processed level by level, so that every parent’s demand has been posted before the item itself is exploded, once. That is DP recipe step 4, an order in which each state is computed after everything it depends on, and the saving is the same as in §09. MRP has a second reason the computer science version does not: an item’s requirements must all be in before it is netted against stock and lot-sized, or it would be ordered in fragments.

!The source’s memoised Fibonacci breaks its own rule

The source writes def fib_memo(n, memo={}):. A few pages later its DP pitfall table lists “mutable default argument: def f(memo={}) shares the cache across calls” and says not to do it. Python creates that default {} once, when the function is defined, and every call that does not pass its own shares it.

For fib this is harmless: fib(30) means the same thing on every call, so a stale entry is still correct (and the source’s function returns the right fib(50)). Put the same idiom on a function with a second input and it gives wrong answers. Counting ordered ways to make 10 from coins of 1 and 2 gives 89, correctly; called next with coins of 1 and 5, it returns 89 again, and the answer is 8 ([Ch14 s07]). A cache is valid only for the inputs it is keyed on. Reading a steam table at the right temperature and the wrong pressure is the same mistake.

NP-hard, and solved anyway

src 00. foundations ↗

The source lists knapsack DP among its categories without working one. It is worth working, because it settles a puzzle Sheet 13 left open. Sheet 13 part 1 §09 said line balancing is bin packing, which is NP-hard, and that this is why you were given heuristics. Knapsack is also NP-hard. Yet here is an exact method for a knapsack problem that runs in moments.

Take one station with cycle time \(C\) and a set of task times. Load the station as full as possible without exceeding \(C\), ignoring precedence as Sheet 13 §09 did. That is the subset-sum problem, the simplest knapsack. Keep a table of which total loads are reachable: start with 0, and for each task add every total that task can extend. The table has \(n\) rows and \(C + 1\) columns, and it agrees with brute force over all \(2^n\) subsets on 200 random stations ([Ch14 s11]). With task times in whole seconds and a 60-second cycle, that is 61 columns. Trivial.

Pseudo-polynomial: fast in the value, slow in the digits

The catch is what “\(n \times (C+1)\)” is polynomial in. Complexity is measured against the length of the input, and a number \(C\) takes only about \(\log_2 C\) bits to write. Every extra bit doubles \(C\) and so doubles the table: for 40 tasks, an 8-bit capacity needs 10,280 cells, a 16-bit one 2,621,480, a 24-bit one 671,088,680 ([Ch14 s11]). The method is polynomial in the value of \(C\) and exponential in its length. That is called pseudo-polynomial time, and it is how an NP-hard problem can be routine in practice: its hardness lives in large numbers, and shop-floor numbers are small. The standard term for such problems is weakly NP-hard (reported).

So why does this not rescue line balancing?

Because filling one station was never the hard part. Bin packing is NP-hard even when all the numbers are small (strongly NP-hard, reported), and the difficulty is in how stations interact. A concrete line: tasks of 7, 7, 6, 4, 4 and 4 seconds, cycle time 12.

  • The only way to load one station to exactly 12 seconds, 100% utilisation, is the three 4-second tasks.
  • Take it, and 7, 7 and 6 are left. No two of them fit together under 12, so they need three more stations: 4 in total.
  • The optimum is 3: {7, 4}, {7, 4}, {6, 4}. No station is full, and one fewer station is needed ([Ch14 s11]).
✓What transfers to the shop floor

Maximising each station’s utilisation in turn is a natural rule, and the exact method for it is easy, and it still costs a station here. The three short tasks were the fillers the long tasks needed. This is Sheet 13’s point from the other side: the heuristics exist because the global problem is hard, and even a locally perfect step can be globally wrong. Precedence constraints, which this example ignores, can only add stations, never remove them.

An array is a fixed pitch

src 01. arrays and hashing ↗

An array stores its items side by side in one block of memory, each taking the same number of bytes. So the address of item \(i\) is a calculation, not a search:

$$\text{address}(i) = \text{base} + i \times \text{size}$$
✓Identity: this is a fixed pitch

Holes drilled along a rail at pitch \(p\) from a datum put hole 57 at \(x_0 + 57p\); nobody counts the first 56 to find it. A lead screw converts turns to travel the same way. That is why reading item \(i\) costs \(O(1)\) whatever \(i\) is. A 2-D array is stored row after row, like a tube sheet laid out by rows, so item \([i, j]\) of a \(3\times5\) array of 8-byte numbers sits at \(\text{base} + 40i + 8j\): the pitch between rows is five items. NumPy reports exactly those strides, (40, 8) ([Ch14 s12]).

The same rigidity is the cost. Inserting an item in the middle means every later item must shift one place, which is \(O(n)\), exactly as inserting a new fixture into a line bolted at fixed pitch moves everything downstream. Searching an unsorted array is also \(O(n)\): the pitch tells you where item 57 is, not where the value 57 is.

!Where \(O(1)\) stops meaning “the same cost”

Big O counts steps. It does not price them, and memory makes some steps dearer than others. Sheet 13 part 1 §15 showed that the processor fetches memory in blocks and keeps recently used blocks close. Reading items in order uses every byte of each block fetched; jumping around wastes most of each fetch.

Measured: summing 8.4 million numbers read in order took 31 ms, and the same numbers read in shuffled order took 141 ms, 4.5× slower ([Ch14 s12]). Same count of steps, same Big O. The source puts the gap between arrays and linked lists at 10–100× in practice; that is plausible for following pointers in compiled code, but this sheet measured one machine and one access pattern, and 4.5× is what it found.

A string is an array of characters, with one wrinkle the source gets right. In UTF-8, the usual text encoding, characters take different numbers of bytes: 1 for a, 2 for é, 3 for a Chinese character, 4 for an emoji ([Ch14 s12]). So “the \(i\)-th character” is no longer at a fixed pitch in the bytes. When a problem promises only lowercase English letters, a 26-slot array indexed by letter replaces a hash map, and the pitch is back.

Hashing, and the utilisation cliff

src 01. arrays and hashing ↗

An array finds item number 57 instantly. A hash table extends that to items identified by anything: a name, a part number, a coordinate. A hash function \(h\) turns the key into a slot number, and the item is stored in that slot of an array. Looking it up again means computing \(h\) again and going straight there, \(O(1)\) on average.

✓Filing parts by the last two digits of the part number

A store that files part 4417-2083 in bin 83 is using the hash function \(h(k) = k \bmod 100\). Anyone can find the part again without a catalogue. When two parts land in the same bin, the store either keeps both in it (the hash-table term is chaining) or puts the newcomer in the next bin along that has room (linear probing). Both are exactly the storekeeper’s options.

!Where it breaks: real keys are not random

Part numbers cluster. If every sub-assembly ends in -00, bin 00 overflows while others sit empty. A hash function exists to prevent exactly this: it must scramble the key so that similar keys land far apart and every slot is equally likely. Designing one that does, quickly, is a subject with no counterpart in your training; the bridge table lists it as one to learn cold. Everything below assumes the scrambling works. If an adversary picks keys that all hash to one slot, every operation degrades to \(O(n)\), which is the worst case in the source’s table.

Collisions are not an occasional nuisance. They are certain, and early. This is the birthday problem from your probability syllabus: among 23 people the chance that two share a birthday is 0.5073. In a table of 65,536 slots filled at random, the first collision arrives on average at insert 327, when the table is 0.50% full; theory, \(\sqrt{\pi m/2} + 2/3\), says 322 ([Ch14 s13]). Every hash table needs a collision policy from its first few hundred entries.

How full is too full

The load factor is \(\alpha = n/m\), items per slot. How much a lookup costs depends on \(\alpha\) and on the policy. For linear probing, the expected number of slots inspected is given by Knuth’s formulas, one for finding a key that is present and one for establishing that a key is absent:

$$\text{hit: } \tfrac12\left(1 + \frac{1}{1-\alpha}\right) \qquad \text{miss: } \tfrac12\left(1 + \frac{1}{(1-\alpha)^2}\right)$$

Simulated, at \(\alpha = 0.5\): 1.49 and 2.47 against 1.50 and 2.50. At 0.75: 2.50 and 8.50, exactly. At 0.9: 5.33 and 51.25 against 5.50 and 50.50 ([Ch14 s13]). With chaining, a hit costs \(1 + \alpha/2\) and a miss scans a chain of average length \(\alpha\), which degrades gently and still works past \(\alpha = 1\): at 1.5 the simulation gives 1.750 and 1.500, matching both.

✓The cliff you know from queueing

Sheet 13 part 2 §06 put an M/M/1 queue in front of you: the number waiting is \(\rho/(1-\rho)\), harmless at 50% utilisation and exploding as \(\rho \to 1\). Linear probing has the same cliff for a related reason. Occupied slots form runs, and a new key whose home is anywhere inside a run is placed at its end, making it longer. Long runs catch more newcomers and grow faster, as a long queue at a machine grows once arrivals come close to its capacity.

So the practical rule is the planner’s rule. Nobody loads a machine to 95% and expects short queues, and hash tables are resized well before they fill. Java’s HashMap resizes at 0.75, the figure the source quotes; Python’s dict uses about two thirds (reported). At 0.75 a miss costs 8.5 inspections under linear probing. At 0.9 it costs 50.

!Same shape, different law

The miss cost grows as \(1/(1-\alpha)^2\), a steeper power than the queue’s \(1/(1-\rho)\), and the mechanism is geometry, not randomness in time: nothing in a hash table waits. Carry the shape across and the lesson that follows from it (keep slack in the system), not the formula.

The Bloom filter is a GO gauge

src 01. arrays and hashing ↗

The source mentions Bloom filters in one paragraph. They deserve a little more, because their error structure is one you use every day. A Bloom filter is an array of \(m\) bits, all starting at 0, and \(k\) different hash functions. To add a key, set the \(k\) bits it hashes to. To ask whether a key is present, look at its \(k\) bits: if any is 0 the key was definitely never added; if all are 1 it probably was, because other keys may have set those bits.

✓A cheap one-sided screen before an expensive exact test

A GO plug gauge answers one question in a second. If it will not enter, the hole is definitely undersized, and no measurement is needed to reject it. If it enters, the hole may be good or oversized, and you go on to the NO-GO gauge or the CMM. A Bloom filter sits in front of a database the same way: “definitely absent” saves the slow disk read outright, and “maybe present” costs you only the read you would have made anyway. Neither ever gives a wrong final answer. Each only decides when the expensive test can be skipped.

The false-positive rate, for \(n\) keys in \(m\) bits, and the number of hash functions that minimises it:

$$p \approx \left(1 - e^{-kn/m}\right)^k, \qquad k^* = \frac{m}{n}\ln 2$$

With 10 bits per key, \(k^* = 6.93\), and the best whole number is 7. Simulated with 20,000 keys: 0.854% false positives against 0.819% from the formula, and zero false negatives. With a single hash function the rate is 9.50% against 9.52% ([Ch14 s14]). At the optimum, each extra bit per key multiplies the false-positive rate by \(0.6185\), so every 4.8 bits per key buys another factor of ten.

!Where the gauge analogy breaks

The gauge is one-sided by design: it tests one limit. The filter is one-sided because bits collide, and that brings two behaviours a gauge does not have. Its error rate rises with every key added, so a filter is sized for a planned number of keys and silently degrades past it. And a key cannot be removed, because clearing its bits could erase evidence of other keys that share them.

Two pointers: discarding a row at a time

src 01. arrays and hashing ↗

Given a sorted list, find two entries that add to a target. Put a pointer \(i\) at the smallest entry and \(j\) at the largest. If \(a_i + a_j\) is too small, move \(i\) up; if too large, move \(j\) down; stop when they meet or the sum is found. The source explains why this works in words. Here is the reason as a picture.

Lay out every pair as a grid, rows \(i\) and columns \(j\). Sorting makes the sums increase along each row and down each column. If \(a_i + a_j\) is too small, then \(a_i\) plus anything in the columns still in play, all \(\le a_j\), is too small as well, so the whole of row \(i\) is eliminated in one comparison. Too large eliminates column \(j\) the same way. Each step discards a row or a column, so at most \(n - 1\) steps cover all \(n^2/2\) pairs. On 500 random cases the method agreed with brute force and never took more than \(n - 1\) steps ([Ch14 s15]).

Three Sum (find every triple summing to zero) fixes one entry and runs two pointers on the rest, for \(O(n^2)\) in total. Its hard part, as the source says, is not returning the same triple twice, and its duplicate-skipping code is correct: it matched a brute-force search on 300 small lists full of repeated values ([Ch14 s15]).

!“Optimal” is a conjecture, not a fact

The source says \(O(n^2)\) is optimal for Three Sum “(you must consider all pairs)”. The parenthesis is not an argument: the two-pointer method just shown does not consider all pairs individually, it discards them by the row. And the claim itself is open. That no algorithm beats \(n^2\) by a polynomial factor is the 3SUM conjecture, widely believed and unproven, and algorithms faster than \(n^2\) by logarithmic factors are known (Grønlund and Pettie, 2014; reported, not reproduced here). This is §02’s point about lower bounds: proving that a problem is hard is much harder than proving an algorithm is fast.

Trapping rain water is a spillway

src 01. arrays and hashing ↗

A row of bars of given heights is rained on; how much water stays? The source’s rule: the water level above each bar is the smaller of the tallest bar to its left and the tallest bar to its right, both counting the bar itself.

$$\text{level}_i = \min\Big(\max_{j \le i} h_j,\; \max_{j \ge i} h_j\Big), \qquad \text{water} = \sum_i \big(\text{level}_i - h_i\big)$$
✓Identity: a pond’s level is set by its lower spill point

A pond between two ridges fills until it overflows the lower ridge, and its surface is then flat at that height. The higher ridge does nothing. That is the formula exactly: the water can escape left over the highest bar on the left, or right over the highest bar on the right, and it settles at the lower of those two spill heights. It follows that raising the tallest bar in the source’s example from 3 to 8 traps no extra water at all: 6 units before and after ([Ch14 s16]). A higher weir on the side that does not spill changes nothing.

The source’s two-pointer code finds this in one pass with no extra arrays: work inward from both ends, always advancing the side whose bar is lower, because that side is the one whose spill height is already known. It agrees with the brute-force formula on 2,000 random profiles ([Ch14 s16]).

!A pitfall that is not one

The source warns that “confusing >= with > in the max updates can cause off-by-one water calculations”. In its own code the swap changes 0 of 2,000 random answers, and so does swapping < for <= in the side test ([Ch14 s16]). When a bar equals the running maximum, one version updates the maximum to the same value and the other adds \(\max - h = 0\) water. Both do nothing.

!Where it breaks: in two dimensions water finds a winding path

Real terrain is two-dimensional, and the obvious extension (take the lowest of the tallest heights looking north, south, east and west) is wrong. In a 6×6 grid verified here, a low cell is walled by height-9 ground in all four straight directions, so that rule holds water 8 deep over it. The true level is 1: the water drains down a channel that turns two corners before reaching the edge ([Ch14 s16]).

The correct statement is the one a hydrologist would give. A lake’s level is the lowest saddle on any route to the sea: over every path from the cell to the edge, take the highest point on the path, and the level is the lowest such value. That is a minimax path, and two independent methods for it agree on 150 random grids ([Ch14 s16]). In one dimension there are only two routes out, left and right, so the minimax collapses to the source’s \(\min(\max_L, \max_R)\). The 2-D method is a priority-queue search from the edges inward, and it belongs to the graph algorithms of part 2.

A sliding window needs a monotone constraint

src 01. arrays and hashing ↗

A sliding window is a stretch of consecutive items, from index \(l\) to \(r\). Move \(r\) forward to take in a new item; while the window breaks the rule, move \(l\) forward to shed items. Each index enters once and leaves once, so the whole scan is \(O(n)\) instead of the \(O(n^2)\) of trying every stretch.

The fixed-width version you already know: a moving-average filter on a sensor signal adds the newest sample to a running sum and subtracts the oldest, one step per sample however wide the window. The variable-width version is the new part, and it rests on a condition the source states once and then leaves in the background: the rule must be monotone. Adding an item may only ever push the window toward breaking the rule, and removing one only ever toward satisfying it.

!Where it breaks: negative numbers

Find the shortest stretch whose sum is at least \(K\). With all values \(\ge 0\) the window method matches brute force on 1,000 random cases. Allow negatives and it fails on the list \([-5, 9]\) with \(K = 9\): the window takes in \(-5\), then \(9\), sums to 4, never reaches 9, and reports no answer. The answer is the single item \([9]\) ([Ch14 s17]). With a negative number in the window, shedding an item from the left can raise the sum, so “shrink until the rule holds” no longer has a direction. Problems with negative values need a different method: prefix sums, the subject of the next section.

The source’s two worked windows are correct. Longest substring without a repeated character, which jumps \(l\) straight past the earlier copy of a repeated character, matched brute force on 500 random strings ([Ch14 s17]). In minimum window substring, the counter have tracks how many required characters are present in sufficient number, and its test is == rather than >= so that each character is counted once, on the step it first becomes sufficient. With >= the count runs past the requirement and the window is declared valid too early ([Ch14 s17]).

A prefix sum is a shear-force diagram

src 01. arrays and hashing ↗

A prefix sum array stores running totals: \(P_0 = 0\) and \(P_{i+1} = P_i + x_i\). Built once in \(O(n)\), it answers “what is the sum of items \(l\) to \(r-1\)?” in one subtraction, \(P_r - P_l\), however long the stretch.

✓Identity: you have drawn thousands of these

Walk along a beam from the left end adding up the forces as you pass them, and the running total is the shear force. For point loads the shear-force diagram is a prefix sum of the loads, reactions included, and the net load between two sections is the difference of the shears there. The bending-moment diagram is in turn the running area under the shear diagram, a second prefix sum. And your habitual check, that the diagrams close to zero at the far support, is the check that the prefix sums are right: \(\sum F = 0\) and \(\sum M = 0\).

The source’s medium problem uses the subtraction backwards. To count the stretches that sum to exactly \(k\), note that a stretch from \(l\) to \(r\) sums to \(k\) when \(P_r - P_l = k\), that is, when an earlier running total equals \(P_r - k\). So walk once, keep a count of every running total seen so far in a hash map (§13), and at each step add the count of \(P_r - k\). One pass, \(O(n)\), and unlike the sliding window of §17 it does not care about negative values: it matched brute force on 800 random lists that included them ([Ch14 s18]).

The map must start holding one running total of zero, the empty prefix, as the source warns. Without it, stretches that begin at the very first item are missed: for \([3, 1, 2]\) with \(k = 3\) the count comes out 1 instead of 2. That zero is the reaction at the left support. Leave it off and the shear diagram starts from the wrong datum.

Product of array except self is the same construction with multiplication: a running product from the left and another from the right, multiplied together at each position. It uses no division, so it handles zeros, and it matched brute force on 500 lists containing them ([Ch14 s18]).

!Where it breaks: the difference of two large numbers

On paper \(V(6) - V(1)\) is exact. In a computer the running totals are floating-point numbers, and Sheet 13 part 1 §§10–11 showed they carry constant relative error. Four million random values summed in 32-bit floating point reach about two million, where the spacing between representable numbers is 0.25. A range of ten values, about 5 in total, read as \(P_r - P_l\) came out 3% wrong; adding the same ten values directly is exact to one part in \(10^7\) ([Ch14 s18]). This is catastrophic cancellation, the reason a strain gauge measures the change in length directly rather than subtracting two measured lengths. With integers there is no problem at all. With floats, the prefix-sum trick trades speed for accuracy on short ranges far from the origin.

The tortoise, the hare, and a derivation that skips a case

src 02. linked lists, stacks, and queues ↗

A linked list gives up the fixed pitch. Each item is stored wherever there was room, together with the address of the next item, like a route card that names the next work centre. Inserting or removing an item next to one you already hold means rewriting one or two addresses, \(O(1)\), with nothing shifted. Finding the 57th item means following 56 addresses, \(O(n)\), and each one is a jump through memory of exactly the kind §12 measured as slow. A doubly linked list also stores the previous item’s address, so it can be walked both ways. A sentinel is a dummy first node that removes the special case of changing the head, as a datum removes the special case of the first dimension.

If a bug makes some node point back to an earlier one, following the list never ends: it has a tail of length \(a\) leading into a loop of length \(c\), shaped like the letter \(\rho\). Floyd’s method detects this with two pointers, a tortoise that moves one node per step and a hare that moves two.

✓Two runners on a track: relative velocity

Once both are in the loop, the hare gains one node per step on the tortoise, so the gap closes by one each step and they must meet within \(c\) steps of the tortoise entering, the source’s argument. It is the kinematics of two runners on a circular track, or the hour and minute hands meeting every \(12/11\) hours, done in whole steps. Checked on every list with \(a < 25\) and \(c < 25\): they always meet, and within \(c\) steps ([Ch14 s19]).

The harder question is where the loop starts. The source’s method: after the meeting, send one pointer back to the head, move both one node per step, and they meet at the loop’s first node. Its justification: let the tortoise have walked \(a + b\), \(a\) along the tail and \(b\) into the loop. The hare has walked \(2(a+b)\). “The difference is one full cycle: \(a + b = c\).”

!The difference is a whole number of cycles

When they meet, the hare is ahead by exactly \(a + b\) nodes and at the same place, so \(a + b\) is a multiple of the loop length:

$$a + b = kc, \qquad k \ge 1$$

With a tail of 10 and a loop of 3 they meet after 12 steps, \(b = 2\), and \(a + b = 12 = 4 \times 3\): the hare lapped four times, not once ([Ch14 s19]). The source’s \(a + b = c\) holds only when the tail is shorter than the loop. The conclusion survives anyway, because only the remainder matters: \(a \equiv -b \pmod{c}\), so walking \(a\) steps from the meeting point lands on the loop’s start, as does walking \(a\) steps from the head. The method is right for every list checked. The derivation proves it only for short tails.

It can be said more sharply. At any step \(T\) that is a multiple of \(c\) and at least \(a\), both pointers are in the loop at the same offset, so the first meeting is at the first multiple of \(c\) not less than \(a\). That holds on all 600 lists checked ([Ch14 s19]).

!Where the runners analogy breaks: the speed must be exactly two

Continuous runners meet at any speed ratio. The first draft of this sheet expected a hare moving three nodes per step to jump over the tortoise sometimes and never meet. It does not: from a common start it always meets ([Ch14 s19]). What fails is the second half. With speed \(v\) the meeting step satisfies \((v-1)T \equiv 0 \pmod{c}\), and only for \(v = 2\) does that force \(T\) to be a multiple of \(c\). At speed 3, the restart-from-head trick fails on 36 of 132 small lists, every one with an even loop; with no tail and a loop of 2, the two pointers circle forever without meeting. The detection is kinematics and survives any speed. The location trick is arithmetic and needs exactly 2.

The same pair of pointers finds the middle of a list: when the hare reaches the end, the tortoise is halfway, at the second of the two middle nodes when the length is even.

Stacks, and the monotonic stack

src 02. linked lists, stacks, and queues ↗

A stack allows only two operations: put an item on top, and take the top item off. Last in, first out, like plates. The restriction is the point, because it matches anything that must be undone in reverse order: the call stack of §05, an editor’s undo history, and nested brackets. Checking that ([]{}) is balanced means pushing each opener and, at each closer, checking that the top of the stack is its partner. The source’s reminder to check that the stack is empty at the end is the whole of the ((( case.

A monotonic stack keeps its contents in increasing (or decreasing) order by popping everything that the new item would violate before pushing it. It answers “for each item, where is the next larger one?” in a single pass. For daily temperatures, each day’s index waits on the stack until a warmer day arrives and pops it, recording the gap. Each index is pushed once and popped at most once, so the total work is \(O(n)\) however the pops cluster. It matched brute force on 500 random series, with never more than \(n\) pops ([Ch14 s20]).

The source’s hard problem, the largest rectangle under a histogram, uses the same stack in increasing order. When a bar is popped because a shorter one has arrived, the popped bar’s rectangle is complete: it extends right up to the newcomer. The source’s code matched brute force on 1,000 random histograms ([Ch14 s20]), and its warning about the line start = idx is right. That line lets the shorter newcomer inherit the left edge of the taller bars it displaced; delete it and the histogram \([6, 4]\) returns 6 instead of 8, because the 4-high rectangle is no longer allowed to extend under the 6.

!No counterpart

The plain stack maps onto your world easily. The monotonic stack does not, and an analogy would only blur it. What makes it work is a counting argument: each item is pushed once and popped at most once, so however unevenly the pops fall, the total is at most \(2n\). It is the same argument as the amortised append of §04, and that is the thing to carry away.

Queues, heaps, and the dispatch rule

src 02. linked lists, stacks, and queues ↗

A queue is first in, first out: add at the back, remove from the front. A deque allows both at both ends. The source’s exercise builds a queue from two stacks: push onto one, and when the other is empty to pop from, pour the first into it, which reverses the order. One pop can move hundreds of items, and in a run of 5,000 random operations one pop moved 467. Yet each item is poured at most once, so 2,752 pushes caused only 2,578 moves in total ([Ch14 s21]). Amortised \(O(1)\) again, by the same accounting as §04.

A priority queue always hands out the smallest item, whatever order items arrived in. The standard implementation is a binary heap: a tree stored in an array, the children of slot \(i\) in slots \(2i+1\) and \(2i+2\) (a fixed pitch again), with every parent no larger than its children. Adding or removing an item repairs the order along one root-to-leaf path, \(O(\log n)\).

Building a heap from \(n\) items at once costs only \(O(n)\), which surprises most people. Half the items are leaves and need no work, a quarter can sink at most one level, an eighth at most two, and the sum \(\sum_h h/2^{h+1}\) converges. Measured: 164,904 comparisons to build a heap of 100,000 items, 1.65 per item ([Ch14 s21]).

Kth largest: the source’s reasoning is backwards, and its conclusion half right

To find the \(k\)-th largest of \(n\) numbers, the source keeps a heap of the \(k\) largest seen so far and calls this “the optimal approach” at \(O(n\log k)\). It calls the alternative, heapify everything and pop \(k\) times, “slower: \(O(n + k\log n)\)”. But \(n + k\log n\) is never worse than \(n\log k\), and for small \(k\) it is better. So the Big O argument points the other way. Counting comparisons on 100,000 numbers ([Ch14 s21]):

On random input the source’s method wins after all, for a reason it never gives. A new number only enters the size-\(k\) heap if it beats the current \(k\)-th largest, and for the \(i\)-th number that chance is \(k/i\). Summed, only about \(k\ln(n/k)\) numbers ever get in: 90 against a predicted 92 at \(k = 10\), 4,530 against 4,605 at \(k = 1000\). The rest cost one comparison each. The first draft of this correction said the heapify method always wins; the count on random input refuted it. The honest summary: neither method is “optimal”, the winner depends on the order of the data, and the size-\(k\) heap’s solid advantage is memory, since it needs room for \(k\) items rather than \(n\) and can process a stream too large to store.

Merging \(k\) sorted lists keeps the \(k\) current front items in a heap and repeatedly takes the smallest, \(O(n\log k)\) for \(n\) items in all. The source’s tie-breaker is necessary: without the index in each heap entry, two equal values make Python compare the list nodes themselves, which raises a TypeError ([Ch14 s21]).

✓A heap is how a dispatcher applies SPT

Sheet 13 part 2 §04 showed that shortest-job-first is the SPT dispatching rule, optimal for mean flow time on one machine, and checked it exhaustively. A heap keyed on processing time is how you run SPT when jobs keep arriving: each arrival is an insert, each dispatch removes the minimum, both \(O(\log n)\), where re-sorting the whole queue at every dispatch would cost \(O(n\log n)\).

!Where it breaks: a fast rule is not a right rule

SPT’s optimality assumes every job is waiting at time zero. Let job A (10 minutes) be ready at 0 and job B (1 minute) arrive at minute 1. A dispatcher that never idles starts A, the only job waiting, and the completion times are 10 and 11, total 21. Leaving the machine idle for one minute to run B first gives 2 and 12, total 14 ([Ch14 s21]). With arrival times the single-machine problem is NP-hard (reported). The heap makes the rule fast. It cannot make the rule right.
