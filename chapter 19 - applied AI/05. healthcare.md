# Healthcare

*Healthcare is where machine learning's potential benefits and potential harms are both largest. This file covers the main application areas (medical imaging, clinical prediction from health records, language models in medicine, and monitoring with wearables), the data and how they differ from benchmark datasets, the metrics that matter clinically, survival analysis, why models that test well often fail in hospitals, evaluation from retrospective studies to trials, regulation, and what it takes to deploy safely and fairly*

- Medicine generates enormous amounts of data, from images and lab results to clinical notes and continuous sensor streams, and it is full of decisions made under uncertainty, time pressure and specialist shortage. Models can read retinal photographs for diabetic eye disease, flag suspicious mammograms, draft clinical notes from conversations, and warn of deteriorating patients. The first AI system authorised by the US regulator to make a diagnosis without a clinician reviewing the image (for diabetic retinopathy) was cleared in 2018, and hundreds more medical devices with AI components have followed, most of them in radiology.

- The gap between a promising paper and a tool that helps patients is wide, though. Healthcare data are messy, biased by how care is delivered, and different in every hospital; errors harm people; and a model's value depends on how clinicians actually use it. This file draws heavily on chapter 06's evaluation, uncertainty, causal inference, interpretability and fairness files, because healthcare is where all of their lessons apply at once.

## The Data

- **Electronic health records (EHRs)** combine structured data (diagnoses and procedures coded in standard vocabularies such as ICD-10 and SNOMED CT, laboratory results coded with LOINC, medications, vital signs, demographics) with unstructured clinical notes. The **FHIR** standard defines how such records are exchanged between systems. Images are stored in the **DICOM** format with rich metadata.

- These data were collected to deliver and bill for care, not for machine learning, and that shows:
    - **Informative missingness**: a test is ordered because a clinician is worried, so the *presence* of a lactate measurement is itself a sign of suspected sepsis. Models readily learn this, which is useful for prediction and dangerous if the ordering pattern changes (chapter 06's practical data file on missing-not-at-random data).
    - **Irregular time series**: measurements arrive at uneven intervals that depend on the patient's condition.
    - **Label definitions are hard**: "sepsis", "heart failure" or "readmission" must be defined from codes, labs and timings, and different definitions identify different patients. Codes reflect billing as much as biology.
    - **Leakage through care processes**: a model predicting deterioration can learn from treatments already started *because* clinicians had recognised the deterioration, predicting what doctors already know.
    - **Privacy**: health data are protected by law (HIPAA in the US, GDPR in Europe). De-identification removes direct identifiers, but rich records can still be re-identified, which limits data sharing and motivates federated learning (chapter 20's decentralised AI file).

## Medical Imaging

- Imaging was the first area where deep learning matched specialists on well-defined tasks:
    - **Diabetic retinopathy** from retinal photographs (Gulshan et al., 2016), with high sensitivity and specificity against ophthalmologist panels.
    - **Skin lesions** from photographs (Esteva et al., 2017), at the level of dermatologists on a benchmark set.
    - **Mammography screening** (McKinney et al., 2020), where a system reduced both false positives and false negatives compared with radiologists in retrospective data from the US and UK.
    - **Chest X-rays**, CT and MRI: detecting nodules, haemorrhages, fractures and dozens of findings.

- The models are the CNNs and vision transformers of chapter 08, with domain-specific challenges:
    - **Resolution**: a digital pathology **whole-slide image** can be 100,000 × 100,000 pixels, far too large for a network. It is split into thousands of tiles, and **multiple-instance learning** learns from slide-level labels ("this slide contains cancer") without knowing which tiles are positive, typically by attention-weighted pooling over tile embeddings.
    - **Segmentation** of organs, tumours and lesions uses U-Net-style architectures (chapter 08). **nnU-Net** (Isensee et al., 2021) showed that a self-configuring U-Net pipeline, which adapts preprocessing and architecture to each dataset automatically, matched or beat most specialised methods across many challenges. Segmentation is scored with the **Dice coefficient**, because pixel accuracy is meaningless when the structure of interest is tiny (the first coding task).
    - **3D and temporal data**: CT and MRI volumes, ultrasound video, follow-up comparisons.
    - **Foundation models** pretrained with self-supervision on millions of unlabelled medical images (pathology tiles, radiographs, retinal images) reduce the labelled data needed for each new task (the limited-labels file).

## Clinical Prediction from Records

- Models on EHR data predict deterioration, sepsis, readmission, mortality, length of stay, or the risk of a specific complication, to trigger earlier intervention or allocate resources. Gradient-boosted trees on carefully engineered features (recent values, trends, missingness indicators, counts of events) are strong baselines; sequence models and transformers over the patient's timeline of coded events can learn representations shared across tasks.

- A sobering example shows what can go wrong. A widely deployed proprietary sepsis prediction model was evaluated externally at one large hospital system (Wong et al., 2021): its AUC was 0.63 rather than the 0.76-0.83 reported by the developer, it missed about two thirds of sepsis cases, and it generated alerts for a large share of hospitalised patients, most of them false. Without independent validation, nobody would have known.

### Metrics that matter clinically

- **Sensitivity and specificity** describe a test; the **positive predictive value** (the chance that a positive result is right) also depends on prevalence. The first coding task shows the same excellent test being right 8% of the time when positive in population screening and 89% of the time in symptomatic clinic patients. Report performance in the population where the model will be used.

- **Calibration** matters as much as discrimination when predicted risks drive decisions or are communicated to patients (chapter 06's evaluation file). **Decision curve analysis** (Vickers and Elkin, 2006) evaluates a model by the decisions it would lead to. For a threshold probability $p_t$ at which a clinician would act (which encodes how much worse an unnecessary treatment is than a missed case), the **net benefit** is:

$$\text{NB}(p_t) = \frac{\text{TP}}{n} - \frac{\text{FP}}{n} \cdot \frac{p_t}{1 - p_t}$$

- A model is useful at thresholds where its net benefit beats both "treat everyone" and "treat no one". In the second coding task, a model with exactly the same AUC as a calibrated one, but overconfident, gives less net benefit and at one threshold is worse than treating no one, while a weaker but calibrated model still helps.

### Survival analysis

- Many clinical questions are about **time to an event**: death, relapse, readmission. Some patients have not had the event by the end of follow-up, or leave the study: their times are **censored**, known only to exceed some value. Ignoring censoring biases every estimate. The **Kaplan-Meier estimator** handles it by multiplying, at each event time, the fraction of patients still at risk who survive it:

$$\hat{S}(t) = \prod_{t_i \le t}\left(1 - \frac{d_i}{n_i}\right)$$

- where $d_i$ events occur at time $t_i$ among $n_i$ patients still under observation. **Cox proportional hazards regression** models how covariates multiply the instantaneous risk (the hazard), $h(t \mid x) = h_0(t)\, e^{\beta^\top x}$, without specifying the baseline $h_0$. Machine learning versions include random survival forests and deep survival networks. Prediction quality is measured with the **concordance index** (the fraction of comparable patient pairs in which the higher-risk patient has the event first, a generalisation of AUC to censored data) and with calibration at fixed horizons. The third coding task shows naive handling of censored patients over- or underestimating survival, and Kaplan-Meier recovering it.

## Language Models in Medicine

- Large language models answer medical licensing-exam questions at expert level (Med-PaLM 2 reached about 86% on the MedQA benchmark in 2023, and later general models scored higher). Exam performance is not clinical performance, though: real cases are ambiguous, information arrives incrementally, and errors of omission matter. Research systems for diagnostic conversations have performed well in simulated consultations with actors, which is encouraging but still far from evidence in real care.

- The fastest real adoption has been **ambient documentation**: systems that listen to a consultation (with consent) and draft the clinical note, letting clinicians attend to the patient instead of the keyboard. Other uses include summarising long records, drafting replies to patient messages, coding, prior-authorisation paperwork, and extracting structured data from notes (clinical NLP, chapter 07). Risks include hallucinated findings, omissions, subtle errors that reviewers miss through **automation bias** (trusting the machine too readily), and biased language. Retrieval from trusted sources, citations to the record, and clinician review are standard safeguards.

## Wearables and Monitoring

- Consumer devices measure heart rhythm, activity, sleep and oxygen saturation continuously. Smartwatch electrocardiogram and pulse-wave algorithms can detect **atrial fibrillation**, and large studies have explored their use for screening. In hospitals, continuous monitoring and early-warning scores aim to catch deterioration hours earlier. The challenges are signal quality (motion artefacts), huge volumes of data with rare events, **alarm fatigue** (too many alerts and clinicians stop responding), and the evidence question: does earlier detection actually change outcomes? Sensors can also be biased: pulse oximeters have been found to overestimate oxygen saturation in patients with darker skin, which affected treatment decisions.

## Why Models Fail in the Hospital

- **Shortcut learning.** Models exploit whatever predicts the label in the training data, including artefacts of how data were collected. A pneumonia model learned to recognise which hospital an X-ray came from, and that hospital's disease rate (Zech et al., 2018); skin cancer models used surgical skin markings and rulers; models trained on images from very sick patients learn the look of portable scanners and tubes. A review of over 60 published COVID-19 imaging models found none ready for clinical use, mostly because of biased data and inadequate validation (Roberts et al., 2021). The fourth coding task reproduces the pattern: a model that relies on a scanner marker scores 0.93 at its training hospital and 0.66 at another, while a model restricted to genuine signal holds up.

- **Distribution shift** between sites (patient mix, devices, protocols, coding practice) and over time (new treatments, policy changes, a pandemic) is the rule, not the exception (chapter 06's robustness file). **External validation** on data from other hospitals and later time periods is essential, and models need monitoring and recalibration after deployment.

- **Leakage** of future or care-process information makes retrospective results look better than any real deployment.

- **Fairness**: underrepresented groups get worse performance, and labels that encode unequal access to care produce models that perpetuate it. The best-known case, an algorithm that used health costs as a proxy for health needs and so underestimated the needs of Black patients (Obermeyer et al., 2019), is discussed in the fairness file. Evaluate across subgroups, and question every proxy label.

- **The model is not the intervention.** An accurate prediction helps only if it reaches the right person at the right time and changes what they do. Integration into clinical workflow, alert design, and how clinicians weigh the model's output determine the effect. Studies of clinician-AI collaboration have found that people sometimes ignore correct advice and follow wrong advice; showing explanations does not reliably help (the interpretability file).

## Evaluation and Regulation

- The evidence ladder for clinical AI rises from retrospective internal validation, through external validation, prospective silent deployment (the model runs, but its outputs are not shown, to compare with what happens), to **randomised trials** comparing care with and without the tool on patient outcomes (chapter 06's causal inference file explains why randomisation is needed). Reporting guidelines exist for each stage (TRIPOD+AI for prediction models, CONSORT-AI and SPIRIT-AI for trials, DECIDE-AI for early clinical evaluation). Relatively few AI tools have been tested in randomised trials, though the number is growing.

- **Regulation.** Software that diagnoses, treats or informs clinical management is regulated as a medical device: by the FDA in the US (as software as a medical device), and under the Medical Device Regulation and the AI Act in the EU. By 2025 well over a thousand AI-enabled devices had been authorised in the US, roughly three quarters of them in radiology. A distinctive regulatory problem for machine learning is that models are retrained; the FDA's mechanism of a **predetermined change control plan** lets a manufacturer specify in advance how a model will be updated and validated without a full new submission for each update.

## Deploying Safely

- A practical checklist for a clinical model:
    1. Define the clinical decision, the intended users, the population and what changes if the model is right or wrong.
    2. Choose labels and features that will exist, with the same meaning, at prediction time.
    3. Validate externally (other sites, later periods) and across subgroups; report calibration and net benefit, not just AUC.
    4. Design the human interface: who sees the output, when, in what form, and how disagreement is handled; limit alert burden.
    5. Run silently first, then evaluate prospectively, ideally in a trial.
    6. Monitor inputs, outputs, performance and subgroup metrics continuously; recalibrate or retire models when they drift.
    7. Document the model (model cards), its limits, and who is accountable when it errs.

1. Clinical metrics: how the positive predictive value of the same test changes with prevalence, and why segmentation uses the Dice coefficient rather than pixel accuracy.
```python
import numpy as np

# 1. The same test in different populations. Sensitivity and specificity belong to the test;
#    the positive predictive value (how likely a positive result is right) depends on prevalence.
sens, spec = 0.90, 0.95
print(f"a test with sensitivity {sens:.0%} and specificity {spec:.0%}")
print(f"  {'setting':38s} {'prevalence':>10s} {'PPV':>6s} {'NPV':>7s}   false alarms per true case")
for setting, prev in [("population screening", 0.005), ("high-risk screening", 0.05),
                      ("symptomatic patients in clinic", 0.30)]:
    tp, fn = sens * prev, (1 - sens) * prev
    fp, tn = (1 - spec) * (1 - prev), spec * (1 - prev)
    print(f"  {setting:38s} {prev:10.1%} {tp / (tp + fp):6.1%} {tn / (tn + fn):7.2%}   {fp / tp:8.1f}")
print("In screening, most positives are false even for an excellent test: report PPV for the intended"
      "\npopulation, not just sensitivity and specificity from a study enriched with disease.")

# 2. Segmentation: pixel accuracy versus the Dice coefficient for a small lesion
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
H = W = 256
yy, xx = np.mgrid[:H, :W]
lesion = (yy - 120) ** 2 + (xx - 140) ** 2 < 12 ** 2              # about 0.7% of the image
predictions = {
    "predicts no lesion at all": np.zeros_like(lesion),
    "right place, half the size": (yy - 120) ** 2 + (xx - 140) ** 2 < 8.5 ** 2,
    "right size, shifted 10 px": (yy - 130) ** 2 + (xx - 140) ** 2 < 12 ** 2,
    "near-perfect outline": (yy - 121) ** 2 + (xx - 140) ** 2 < 12 ** 2,
}
dice = lambda p, t: 2 * (p & t).sum() / (p.sum() + t.sum() + 1e-9)
print(f"\nlesion covers {lesion.mean():.2%} of the image")
for name, p in predictions.items():
    print(f"  {name:28s} pixel accuracy {np.mean(p == lesion):6.2%}   Dice {dice(p, lesion):.2f}")
print("Pixel accuracy rewards predicting background everywhere. Dice (and the related Jaccard / IoU) measures"
      "\noverlap with the structure that matters, which is why segmentation is reported with them.")
```

2. Decision curve analysis: compare the net benefit of a calibrated model, an overconfident model with the same AUC, a weaker calibrated model, and the treat-all and treat-none policies.
```python
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

# Patients with a true risk of a complication; a preventive treatment is given if predicted risk > threshold.
rng = np.random.default_rng(1)
n = 50000
true_risk = 1 / (1 + np.exp(-(rng.normal(-2.0, 1.2, n))))          # about 15% have the event
y = rng.uniform(size=n) < true_risk
logit = np.log(true_risk / (1 - true_risk))
models = {
    "calibrated model": true_risk,                                     # predicts the true risk
    "overconfident model (same ranking)": 1 / (1 + np.exp(-(2.5 * logit + 2.5))),
    "weaker but calibrated model": 1 / (1 + np.exp(-(0.6 * logit - 0.8 + rng.normal(0, 0.6, n)))),
}

def auc(score, y):
    r = np.empty(len(score)); r[np.argsort(score)] = np.arange(1, len(score) + 1)
    n1 = y.sum(); return (r[y].sum() - n1 * (n1 + 1) / 2) / (n1 * (len(y) - n1))

def net_benefit(treat, y, pt):
    """True positives per patient, minus false positives weighted by the harm-to-benefit ratio pt / (1 - pt)."""
    return (treat & y).mean() - (treat & ~y).mean() * pt / (1 - pt)

thresholds = np.linspace(0.02, 0.6, 59)
plt.figure(figsize=(9, 5))
plt.plot(thresholds, [net_benefit(np.ones(n, bool), y, t) for t in thresholds], 'k--', label="treat everyone")
plt.axhline(0, color='k', linestyle=':', label="treat no one")
for (name, p), c in zip(models.items(), ['#27ae60', '#e74c3c', '#3498db']):
    nb = [net_benefit(p > t, y, t) for t in thresholds]
    plt.plot(thresholds, nb, color=c, label=f"{name} (AUC {auc(p, y):.2f})")
plt.ylim(-0.05, 0.17); plt.xlabel("threshold probability (harm of unnecessary treatment relative to benefit)")
plt.ylabel("net benefit"); plt.legend(); plt.title("Decision curve analysis"); plt.show()

print(f"event rate {y.mean():.1%}")
print(f"{'model':38s} {'AUC':>5s}   net benefit at threshold 0.10 / 0.20 / 0.40")
for name, p in models.items():
    print(f"{name:38s} {auc(p, y):5.2f}   " + " / ".join(f"{net_benefit(p > t, y, t):+.3f}" for t in [0.1, 0.2, 0.4]))
for t in [0.1, 0.2, 0.4]:
    print(f"   treat everyone at {t}: {net_benefit(np.ones(n, bool), y, t):+.3f}")
print("\nThe overconfident model ranks patients exactly as well as the calibrated one (same AUC), yet acting on"
      "\nits probabilities gives less benefit, and at a threshold of 0.4 it is worse than treating no one, while"
      "\neven the weaker but calibrated model still helps. A clinical model should be judged by the decisions"
      "\nit leads to, which needs calibration as well as discrimination.")
```

3. Survival analysis with censoring: Kaplan-Meier from scratch against three naive ways of handling patients who leave a study, and a concordance index for a risk score.
```python
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

# Time from diagnosis to an event (e.g. relapse) for 2000 patients in two treatment groups.
# The study stops after 5 years, and some patients leave early: their event times are CENSORED.
rng = np.random.default_rng(2)
n = 2000
group = rng.integers(0, 2, n)                                  # 0 = standard care, 1 = new treatment
event_time = rng.exponential(np.where(group == 1, 6.0, 3.5))   # true times (years), unknown beyond follow-up
dropout = rng.uniform(0.5, 8.0, n)                            # patients lost to follow-up
follow_up = np.minimum(dropout, 5.0)                           # administrative end of study at 5 years
time = np.minimum(event_time, follow_up)
observed = event_time <= follow_up                             # False = censored
print(f"{np.mean(~observed):.0%} of patients are censored")

def kaplan_meier(t, e):
    """Survival curve: at each event time, multiply by (1 - deaths / number still at risk)."""
    order = np.argsort(t); t, e = t[order], e[order]
    times, surv, s = [0.0], [1.0], 1.0
    at_risk = len(t)
    for ti, ei in zip(t, e):
        if ei:
            s *= 1 - 1 / at_risk
            times.append(ti); surv.append(s)
        at_risk -= 1                                           # censored patients leave the risk set too
    return np.array(times), np.array(surv)

def surv_at(times, surv, x):
    return surv[np.searchsorted(times, x, side="right") - 1]

truth = lambda g, x: np.exp(-x / (6.0 if g else 3.5))
print(f"\n{'estimate of 3-year event-free survival':48s} standard   new treatment")
for label, fn in [
    ("true value", lambda g: truth(g, 3)),
    ("naive: treat leaving the study as the event", lambda g: np.mean(time[group == g] > 3)),
    ("naive: treat everyone who left as event-free", lambda g: 1 - np.mean(observed[group == g] & (time[group == g] <= 3))),
    ("naive: drop censored patients", lambda g: np.mean(time[(group == g) & observed] > 3)),
    ("Kaplan-Meier", lambda g: surv_at(*kaplan_meier(time[group == g], observed[group == g]), 3)),
]:
    print(f"  {label:46s} {fn(0):8.3f}   {fn(1):8.3f}")

# Concordance index for a risk score: of comparable pairs, how often does the higher-risk patient fail first?
risk = -np.where(group == 1, 6.0, 3.5) + rng.normal(0, 1.5, n)   # a noisy risk score
def c_index(time, observed, risk, max_pairs=200000):
    i = rng.integers(0, n, max_pairs); j = rng.integers(0, n, max_pairs)
    comparable = observed[i] & (time[i] < time[j])               # i had the event before j's time
    return np.mean(risk[i][comparable] > risk[j][comparable])
print(f"\nconcordance index of a noisy risk score: {c_index(time, observed, risk):.2f}  (0.5 = random ranking)")

plt.figure(figsize=(8, 4.5))
for g, c, name in [(0, '#e74c3c', "standard care"), (1, '#3498db', "new treatment")]:
    t_, s_ = kaplan_meier(time[group == g], observed[group == g])
    plt.step(t_, s_, where="post", color=c, label=f"Kaplan-Meier, {name}")
    xs = np.linspace(0, 5, 100); plt.plot(xs, truth(g, xs), '--', color=c, alpha=0.6)
plt.xlabel("years"); plt.ylabel("event-free survival"); plt.legend(); plt.show()
print("Censored patients carry information: they were event-free at least until they left, and no more is known."
      "\nCounting their exit as an event, or dropping them, understates survival; counting them as event-free"
      "\nforever overstates it. Kaplan-Meier uses each patient for exactly as long"
      "\nas they were observed. Survival models (Cox regression, survival forests, deep survival models) and"
      "\nthe concordance index extend the same idea to prediction.")
```

4. Shortcut learning and external validation: a model trained at one hospital learns a scanner marker linked to disease there, and fails at a hospital where it is not.
```python
import numpy as np
from sklearn.linear_model import LogisticRegression

# Chest X-ray features (stand-ins for what an image model extracts). At hospital A, very sick patients are
# imaged in bed with a PORTABLE scanner, whose images carry a visible marker. At hospital B they are not.
rng = np.random.default_rng(3)
def hospital(n, prevalence, p_portable_if_sick, p_portable_if_well):
    y = rng.uniform(size=n) < prevalence
    lung = rng.normal(0, 1, (n, 10)) + 0.35 * y[:, None]           # weak, genuine disease signal
    portable = rng.uniform(size=n) < np.where(y, p_portable_if_sick, p_portable_if_well)
    marker = portable + rng.normal(0, 0.1, n)                       # strong, easy, spurious signal
    return np.c_[lung, marker], y
XA, yA = hospital(8000, 0.30, 0.85, 0.10)       # training hospital: marker strongly tied to disease
XA_test, yA_test = hospital(4000, 0.30, 0.85, 0.10)
XB, yB = hospital(4000, 0.10, 0.30, 0.30)       # external hospital: marker unrelated, lower prevalence

def auc(s, y):
    r = np.empty(len(s)); r[np.argsort(s)] = np.arange(1, len(s) + 1)
    n1 = y.sum(); return (r[y].sum() - n1 * (n1 + 1) / 2) / (n1 * (len(y) - n1))

print(f"{'model':34s} {'AUC at A':>9s} {'AUC at B':>9s} {'mean predicted risk at B':>25s}")
for name, cols in [("all features (learns the marker)", slice(None)), ("lung features only", slice(0, 10))]:
    m = LogisticRegression(max_iter=1000).fit(XA[:, cols], yA)
    pB = m.predict_proba(XB[:, cols])[:, 1]
    print(f"{name:34s} {auc(m.predict_proba(XA_test[:, cols])[:, 1], yA_test):9.2f} {auc(pB, yB):9.2f} "
          f"{pB.mean():18.2f} (true {yB.mean():.2f})")
m = LogisticRegression(max_iter=1000).fit(XA, yA)
print(f"\nweight on the marker: {m.coef_[0, -1]:.2f}; largest weight on any lung feature: {np.abs(m.coef_[0, :10]).max():.2f}")
print("Internal validation rewards the shortcut. At a new hospital it stops working, and the model's"
      "\nprobabilities are also miscalibrated because the prevalence differs. Only external validation on data"
      "\nfrom elsewhere reveals this; checking what the model relies on (the interpretability file) can warn earlier.")
```
