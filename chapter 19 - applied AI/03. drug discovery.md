# Drug Discovery

*Finding a new medicine takes over a decade and more than a billion dollars, and most candidates fail. Machine learning now touches every stage of the process. This file covers the drug discovery pipeline, how molecules are represented for learning, property and ADMET prediction, the evaluation traps specific to chemistry, virtual screening by similarity and docking, binding affinity, generative design, retrosynthesis, closed-loop discovery, and what AI has and has not yet delivered*

- Drug discovery is a search problem in an enormous space under severe constraints. The number of drug-like small molecules has been estimated at around $10^{60}$; a pharmaceutical company's physical screening collection holds a few million. A successful drug must bind its target strongly and selectively, reach it in the body, survive metabolism long enough, not be toxic, and be manufacturable. Bringing one to market has been estimated to cost one to two billion dollars or more, including the cost of failures, and roughly nine in ten candidates that enter clinical trials never get approved.

- Machine learning promises to make the search cheaper (evaluate molecules on a computer before making them), broader (explore chemistry no chemist would have tried) and smarter (decide which experiment to run next). This file uses chapter 12's graph networks, chapter 06's evaluation, uncertainty and limited-label files, and the previous file's protein structure methods.

## The Pipeline

- The stages, roughly in order:
    1. **Target identification and validation**: find a protein (or RNA, or pathway) whose modulation should treat the disease, using genetics, omics data, literature mining and knowledge graphs.
    2. **Hit discovery**: find molecules with any activity against the target, by high-throughput screening of physical libraries, by **virtual screening** of much larger computational ones, or by fragment-based and DNA-encoded library methods.
    3. **Hit-to-lead and lead optimisation**: iteratively modify the best hits to improve potency, selectivity and drug-like properties, in **design-make-test-analyse (DMTA)** cycles, each taking weeks.
    4. **Preclinical development**: safety and pharmacology in cells and animals.
    5. **Clinical trials**: Phase I (safety in a small group), Phase II (efficacy and dosing), Phase III (large confirmatory trials), then regulatory review.

- The late stages are the most expensive, and most failures there are for lack of efficacy or for safety. That is why better target selection and earlier prediction of toxicity and pharmacokinetics could matter more than faster hit finding, even though hit finding receives most of the attention.

## Representing Molecules

- **SMILES** strings write a molecule as a line of text: atoms as element symbols (aromatic atoms in lower case), branches in parentheses, ring closures as matching digits, bonds as `-`, `=`, `#`. Aspirin is `CC(=O)Oc1ccccc1C(=O)O`. SMILES make molecules usable by sequence models (chapter 07), but one molecule has many valid SMILES (canonicalisation picks one), and small edits often produce invalid strings. **SELFIES** is an alternative grammar in which every string decodes to a valid molecule, convenient for generative models.

- **Molecular graphs**, atoms as nodes and bonds as edges, are the natural input for graph neural networks (chapter 12). Message passing over the graph, with atom features (element, charge, aromaticity, hydrogen count) and bond features (order, ring membership), learns task-specific representations. Directed message-passing networks (for example the Chemprop software) are a strong standard baseline for property prediction.

- **Fingerprints** encode a molecule as a fixed-length bit vector. **Extended-connectivity fingerprints** (ECFP, also called Morgan fingerprints) give each atom an identifier from its own properties, then repeatedly update each identifier by combining it with its neighbours' (the same idea as message passing, but with hashing instead of learning), and set a bit for every identifier seen up to a given radius. Each bit therefore marks the presence of a particular circular substructure. The first coding task writes a small SMILES parser and an ECFP-style fingerprint from scratch.

- **Similarity** between fingerprints is measured with the **Tanimoto coefficient** (the Jaccard index of their bit sets), $T(A, B) = |A \cap B| / |A \cup B|$. The similar property principle (similar molecules tend to have similar properties) makes this the workhorse of medicinal chemistry: in the first coding task aspirin pairs with salicylic acid, ibuprofen with naproxen, and caffeine with theophylline.

- **Descriptors** summarise physical chemistry: molecular weight, lipophilicity (logP), polar surface area, hydrogen-bond donors and acceptors, rotatable bonds. **Lipinski's rule of five** (1997) flags oral drug candidates likely to be poorly absorbed: more than 5 hydrogen-bond donors, more than 10 acceptors, molecular weight over 500, or logP over 5. **3D representations** (conformers, or the protein-ligand complex) matter for binding, and need the equivariant models of chapter 12.

## Predicting Properties

- **Quantitative structure-activity relationship (QSAR)** modelling, predicting a molecule's activity or property from its structure, is decades older than deep learning. Today it covers potency against the target, selectivity against related proteins, and **ADMET**: absorption, distribution, metabolism, excretion and toxicity (solubility, permeability, metabolic stability, inhibition of the hERG channel linked to heart arrhythmias, liver toxicity, and more). Public benchmarks include MoleculeNet and the Therapeutics Data Commons.

- Chemical data have awkward properties for machine learning:
    - **Small and noisy**: a project may have a few hundred measured compounds, and the same compound measured in different labs can differ by several-fold.
    - **Biased sampling**: molecules were made because chemists thought they were promising, so datasets cluster around a few chemical series.
    - **Activity cliffs**: tiny structural changes (a single atom) sometimes cause huge activity changes, violating the smoothness every model relies on.
    - **Mixed data**: censored values ("IC50 > 10 µM"), different assays and units.

- Fingerprint-based random forests and gradient boosting remain strong baselines; graph networks tend to win with more data; and pretrained molecular representations (language models on SMILES, graph models pretrained by self-supervision, see the limited-labels file) help when labelled data are scarce. **Multitask** learning across related assays shares information. Uncertainty estimates (deep ensembles, Gaussian processes, conformal prediction; chapter 06's uncertainty file) are essential for deciding which predictions to trust.

- A well-known success: Stokes et al. (2020) trained a graph network on growth inhibition of *E. coli* for about 2,300 molecules, used it to screen over 100 million virtual molecules, and identified **halicin**, a structurally novel antibiotic confirmed experimentally. The same approach later found abaucin, a narrow-spectrum antibiotic against *Acinetobacter baumannii*.

### How to split chemical data

- A random train/test split of a chemical dataset is almost always too optimistic. Molecules come in series of close analogues, so every test molecule has near-twins in training. The real question in a project is usually "how will the model do on the next series?". **Scaffold splits** group molecules by their core ring system (the Bemis-Murcko scaffold) and keep each scaffold on one side; **time splits** train on compounds made before a date and test on later ones, mirroring how the model will be used. This is chapter 06's group and time-series cross-validation applied to chemistry. In the second coding task the same model scores $R^2 = 0.71$ under a random split and $0.17$ under a scaffold split.

## Virtual Screening

- **Virtual screening** ranks a large library computationally and sends only the top few hundred or thousand molecules for testing. Libraries have grown enormously: "make-on-demand" catalogues list tens of billions of molecules that can be synthesised reliably from available building blocks.

- **Ligand-based** screening needs only known actives: rank by fingerprint similarity to them, by a pharmacophore (the 3D arrangement of features needed for binding), or by a QSAR model. It is fast and works without a protein structure, but it tends to find more of what you already have. In the second coding task, similarity to five known actives gives a 16-fold enrichment of actives in the top 1% of the ranking, but every active it finds shares a scaffold with the queries, while other active scaffolds go unfound. Finding actives on new scaffolds (**scaffold hopping**) is the harder and more valuable problem.

- **Structure-based** screening uses the 3D structure of the target (experimental, or predicted by the methods of the previous file). **Molecular docking** searches for the pose (position, orientation, conformation) of each molecule in the binding pocket that optimises a scoring function approximating binding energy. Docking billions of molecules is now feasible: Lyu et al. (2019) docked 170 million molecules against two targets and found new potent ligands, with hit rates that improved as the library grew. Active learning (chapter 06) makes ultra-large screens cheaper: dock a sample, train a model to predict docking scores, dock only what the model ranks highly, and repeat.

- **Machine learning for docking and pose prediction.** Diffusion models such as DiffDock (chapter 12) generate binding poses directly, and co-folding models such as AlphaFold 3 predict the protein and ligand together. Careful benchmarks (PoseBusters) found that many deep learning methods produced physically implausible poses (clashing atoms, wrong bond geometry) even when their RMSD looked good, a reminder to check physical validity, not only distance metrics, and to test on proteins unlike those in training.

- **Evaluation** uses the ranking metrics of chapter 06: the **enrichment factor** at the top $x\%$ (the active rate there divided by the overall active rate), ROC-AUC, and early-recognition metrics such as BEDROC that weight the top of the list, since only the first few hundred picks will ever be tested. Benchmark decoys (assumed inactives) are often easy to tell apart from actives for trivial reasons, such as size, which inflates performance.

## Binding Affinity

- Predicting **how strongly** a molecule binds (affinity, usually as $-\log_{10}$ of a dissociation or inhibition constant) is much harder than predicting whether it binds or how. Docking scores correlate only weakly with measured affinity. Physics-based **free energy perturbation (FEP)** calculations, which alchemically transform one ligand into another in molecular dynamics simulations, reach errors of about 1 kcal/mol (roughly a five-fold error in affinity) for congeneric series, at substantial compute cost. ML models trained on structural data (PDBbind and similar) often perform well on benchmarks partly by memorising ligand or protein similarity rather than learning the physics of interactions; careful splits reveal this. Combining ML with physics, for example ML force fields (chapter 12) or ML-guided selection of which FEP calculations to run, is an active direction.

## Generative Design

- Instead of screening an enumerated library, **generative models** propose new molecules optimised for multiple properties at once:
    - **Sequence models** on SMILES or SELFIES, fine-tuned with reinforcement learning (chapter 06, file 04) towards a reward combining predicted activity and properties (the REINVENT approach).
    - **Variational autoencoders** (Gómez-Bombarelli et al., 2018) map molecules to a continuous latent space where gradient-based or Bayesian optimisation can search; junction-tree VAEs build molecules from valid substructures.
    - **Graph generation** (chapter 12) adds atoms and bonds step by step.
    - **3D, pocket-conditioned diffusion** generates molecules directly inside a protein's binding site.
    - **Fragment growing and linking** with learned scoring.
    - Large language models are increasingly used as chemistry assistants and molecule proposers.

- Generative design is optimisation against learned models, so it inherits their failure modes. Generated molecules can satisfy the reward while being unstable, unsynthesisable or chemically absurd (**reward hacking**); predictions are least reliable far from the training data, where optimisation tends to lead. Practical systems add synthesisability scores, structural filters, similarity constraints and uncertainty penalties, and they keep chemists in the loop.

- Which molecules to make next is ultimately a **sequential decision**: each round of experiments updates the model. The third coding task simulates design-make-test-analyse campaigns starting from data on four scaffolds: model-guided selection finds much better molecules than random choice within a few rounds, and an uncertainty bonus shifts picks towards unexplored scaffolds, with a payoff that depends on the problem. This is Bayesian optimisation and active learning (chapter 06's uncertainty and limited-labels files) applied to molecules.

## Retrosynthesis and Synthesis Planning

- A designed molecule is useless if it cannot be made. **Retrosynthesis** works backwards from a target to purchasable starting materials through a sequence of known reaction types.
    - **Template-based** methods apply reaction rules extracted from reaction databases, with a neural network ranking which rule to apply.
    - **Template-free** methods treat reactions as translation between SMILES strings with sequence-to-sequence transformers (the Molecular Transformer), or edit molecular graphs directly.
    - **Search** strings single steps into full routes. Segler, Preuss and Waller (2018) combined neural networks with Monte Carlo tree search (the planning algorithm behind AlphaGo, which grows a search tree by repeatedly simulating the most promising branches) and produced routes that chemists rated on par with literature routes.

- **Forward reaction prediction** (what will this reaction produce?) and **reaction condition** prediction (solvent, catalyst, temperature) complete the toolkit. Combined with robotic chemistry, they enable **self-driving laboratories** that plan, run and analyse experiments in closed loops, so far mostly for well-defined chemistry and materials problems.

## Biologics and Beyond Small Molecules

- Many new drugs are **biologics**: antibodies, peptides, enzymes and other proteins. Their design uses the protein methods of the previous file: language models trained on antibody sequences, structure prediction for antibody-antigen complexes, inverse folding and diffusion-based binder design, plus models of developability (aggregation, viscosity, immunogenicity). Other modalities (RNA therapeutics, cell and gene therapies) bring their own sequence and design problems.

## Where AI Stands

- AI has become routine infrastructure in pharmaceutical research: property prediction, virtual screening, structure prediction and synthesis planning are standard tools, and several companies built pipelines around them. Molecules discovered or optimised with heavy AI involvement have entered clinical trials, and some have reported positive mid-stage results, such as Insilico Medicine's rentosertib, whose target and molecule were both identified with AI methods, in a Phase 2a trial for idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis. As of mid-2026, no drug discovered primarily by AI had completed the full path to approval, and early analyses suggest AI may raise early-phase success rates more than late-phase ones.

- The honest summary is that ML has shortened and cheapened parts of the early pipeline, especially hit finding and lead optimisation, while the dominant causes of failure (choosing the wrong target, unforeseen toxicity, lack of efficacy in patients) are only beginning to be addressed. Better biological data (human genetics, single-cell and spatial omics, patient-derived models) may matter as much as better models.

```python
import numpy as np
import re
from hashlib import blake2b

def parse_smiles(smi):
    """A small SMILES parser for organic molecules: atoms (incl. aromatic lower case and [bracket] atoms),
    branches ( ), ring-closure digits, and bond symbols - = #. Hydrogens are implicit.
    Returns atom symbols and a bond list (i, j, order); aromatic bonds get order 1.5."""
    tokens = re.findall(r"\[[^\]]+\]|Cl|Br|[BCNOSPFI]|[cnosp]|\(|\)|=|#|-|%\d\d|\d", smi)
    atoms, bonds, stack, rings = [], [], [], {}
    prev, order = None, None
    for tok in tokens:
        if tok == "(":
            stack.append(prev)
        elif tok == ")":
            prev = stack.pop()
        elif tok in "-=#":
            order = {"-": 1, "=": 2, "#": 3}[tok]
        elif tok.isdigit() or tok.startswith("%"):
            if tok in rings:
                j, o = rings.pop(tok)
                arom = atoms[prev].islower() and atoms[j].islower()
                bonds.append((j, prev, order or o or (1.5 if arom else 1)))
            else:
                rings[tok] = (prev, order)
            order = None
        else:
            sym = tok.strip("[]").rstrip("+-0123456789H@")
            atoms.append(sym)
            i = len(atoms) - 1
            if prev is not None:
                arom = atoms[prev].islower() and sym.islower()
                bonds.append((prev, i, order or (1.5 if arom else 1)))
            prev, order = i, None
    return atoms, bonds

def morgan_fingerprint(smi, radius=2, n_bits=2048):
    """ECFP-style circular fingerprint: each atom starts with an identifier from its own properties,
    then repeatedly absorbs its neighbours' identifiers (with bond orders); every identifier seen at
    every radius is hashed into a bit position."""
    atoms, bonds = parse_smiles(smi)
    nbrs = [[] for _ in atoms]
    for i, j, o in bonds:
        nbrs[i].append((j, o)); nbrs[j].append((i, o))
    h = lambda s: int(blake2b(s.encode(), digest_size=8).hexdigest(), 16)
    ids = [h(f"{a.lower()}|{a.islower()}|{len(nbrs[i])}") for i, a in enumerate(atoms)]
    bits = set(x % n_bits for x in ids)
    for _ in range(radius):
        ids = [h(str((ids[i], sorted((o, ids[j]) for j, o in nbrs[i])))) for i in range(len(atoms))]
        bits.update(x % n_bits for x in ids)
    fp = np.zeros(n_bits, bool); fp[list(bits)] = True
    return fp

tanimoto = lambda a, b: (a & b).sum() / (a | b).sum()

drugs = {
    "aspirin":       "CC(=O)Oc1ccccc1C(=O)O",
    "salicylic acid":"OC(=O)c1ccccc1O",
    "paracetamol":   "CC(=O)Nc1ccc(O)cc1",
    "ibuprofen":     "CC(C)Cc1ccc(cc1)C(C)C(=O)O",
    "naproxen":      "COc1ccc2cc(ccc2c1)C(C)C(=O)O",
    "caffeine":      "Cn1cnc2c1c(=O)n(C)c(=O)n2C",
    "theophylline":  "Cn1c2nc[nH]c2c(=O)n(C)c1=O",
    "nicotine":      "CN1CCCC1c1cccnc1",
}
for name, smi in drugs.items():
    atoms, bonds = parse_smiles(smi)
    print(f"{name:15s} {len(atoms):2d} heavy atoms, {len(bonds):2d} bonds")
fps = {k: morgan_fingerprint(v) for k, v in drugs.items()}
names = list(drugs)
print("\nTanimoto similarity (ECFP4-style fingerprints)")
print(" " * 15 + "".join(f"{n[:7]:>8s}" for n in names))
for a in names:
    print(f"{a:15s}" + "".join(f"{tanimoto(fps[a], fps[b]):8.2f}" for b in names))
print("\nStructurally related pairs score highest: aspirin and salicylic acid, ibuprofen and naproxen (both"
      "\narylpropionic acids), caffeine and theophylline. Fingerprint similarity is the workhorse of ligand-based"
      "\nvirtual screening: molecules similar to a known active are more likely to be active too.")
```

scaffolds = {"benzene": "c1cc({R1})ccc1{R2}", "pyridine": "c1cc({R1})cnc1{R2}", "piperidine": "C1CC({R1})CCN1{R2}",

    for a, r1 in enumerate(subs):
        for b, r2 in enumerate(subs):
            smiles.append(tpl.replace("{R1}", r1).replace("{R2}", r2))
            y.append(s_eff[name] + r1_eff[a] + r2_eff[b] + 0.3 * rng.normal())   # hidden "activity" (e.g. pIC50)

def cv_r2(splitter, groups=None):
    pred = np.zeros_like(y)
    for tr, te in splitter.split(X, y, groups):
        rf = RandomForestRegressor(n_estimators=60, max_features=0.2, min_samples_leaf=2, random_state=0, n_jobs=1).fit(X[tr], y[tr])
        pred[te] = rf.predict(X[te])
    return 1 - np.mean((pred - y) ** 2) / np.var(y)
print(f"\nrandom-split cross-validated R^2:   {cv_r2(KFold(4, shuffle=True, random_state=0)):.2f}")
print(f"scaffold-split cross-validated R^2: {cv_r2(GroupKFold(4), group):.2f}")

      "\nquestion for a new chemical series, and the model does far worse there.")

actives = y > np.quantile(y, 0.97)                       # the top 3% are "active"
known = rng.choice(np.flatnonzero(actives), 5, replace=False)   # five actives we already know about
rest = np.setdiff1d(np.arange(len(y)), known)
sims = np.array([[(X[i] & X[k]).sum() / (X[i] | X[k]).sum() for k in known] for i in rest]).max(1)
order = rest[np.argsort(-sims)]
def enrichment(order, frac):
    top = order[:int(len(order) * frac)]
    return actives[top].mean() / actives[rest].mean()
print(f"\n{actives.sum()} actives in the library; screening the rest by similarity to 5 known actives:")
for frac in [0.01, 0.05]:
    print(f"   enrichment factor in the top {frac:.0%}: {enrichment(order, frac):.1f}x  (random screening = 1x)")
top = order[:int(0.05 * len(order))]
names = list(scaffolds)
scaf = lambda idx: sorted({names[g] for g in group[idx]})
print(f"   scaffolds of the 5 known actives:        {scaf(known)}")
print(f"   scaffolds of actives found in the top 5%: {scaf(top[actives[top]])}")
print(f"   scaffolds of all actives:                 {scaf(np.flatnonzero(actives))}")
print("Similarity search finds actives efficiently but mostly near what is already known; finding active"
      "\nmolecules on NEW scaffolds ('scaffold hopping') is the harder, more valuable problem.")

# Design-make-test-analyse cycles. We start with 50 measured molecules, all from the first four scaffolds,
# and can afford to synthesise and test 20 new molecules per round.
def campaign(strategy, seed, rounds=6, batch=20, kappa=1.5):
    r = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    measured = list(r.choice(np.flatnonzero(group < 4), 50, replace=False))
    best = [y[measured].max()]
    for _ in range(rounds):
        untested = np.setdiff1d(np.arange(len(y)), measured)
        if strategy == "random":
            pick = r.choice(untested, batch, replace=False)
        else:
            rf = RandomForestRegressor(n_estimators=40, max_features=0.2, min_samples_leaf=2,
                                       random_state=seed, n_jobs=1).fit(X[measured], y[measured])
            per_tree = np.array([t.predict(X[untested]) for t in rf.estimators_])
            mu, sd = per_tree.mean(0), per_tree.std(0)
            score = mu if strategy == "greedy (best prediction)" else mu + kappa * sd
            pick = untested[np.argsort(-score)[:batch]]
        measured += list(pick)
        best.append(y[measured].max())
    return np.array(best), np.mean(group[measured[50:]] >= 4)
