# Datacentres in Space

*AI's appetite for electricity has revived an old idea: put the computers in orbit, where sunlight is nearly constant and there is no land, grid or water to compete for. This file works through the physics and economics from first principles: power from sunlight, cooling by radiation alone, orbits and latency, communication between satellites and with the ground, radiation and reliability without repair, launch costs, what is being attempted, the strongest objections, and what is practical today*

- Training and serving large models has turned data centres into one of the fastest-growing sources of electricity demand. The International Energy Agency estimated that data centres used about 415 TWh in 2024, around 1.5% of global electricity, and projected roughly a doubling by 2030, with AI the main driver. New data centres are limited less by chips than by grid connections, power plants, cooling water, land and permits.

- Space offers sunlight about 1,361 watts per square metre above the atmosphere, with no night in the right orbit, no weather and no seasons to speak of, while a solar farm on the ground typically produces 15-25% of its peak capacity averaged over a year. It offers no land to buy, and an unlimited cold sky to radiate heat into. In 2025 several companies, research labs and government programmes announced orbital computing projects. This file asks what the physics and the arithmetic say, which is the right way to approach any bleeding-edge claim.

## Power

- A solar array in orbit delivers the solar constant times the cell efficiency times a derating for ageing, pointing losses and temperature: about 350 W/m² for 30%-efficient cells at end of life. One megawatt of computing therefore needs about 3,000 m² of array, a square around 55 metres on a side, plus margin for everything else on the spacecraft (the first coding task).

- **Orbit choice decides whether the panels see the Sun.** In most low Earth orbits a satellite spends up to 35-40% of each 90-100 minute orbit in Earth's shadow and would need heavy batteries to run through it. A **dawn-dusk sun-synchronous orbit** is tilted so that the orbital plane stays roughly perpendicular to the Sun-Earth line all year: the satellite rides along the boundary between day and night and is in sunlight almost continuously, which makes it the natural choice (the second coding task computes eclipse fractions).

- Mass is what costs money at launch, so the key technology figure is **specific power**, watts delivered per kilogram of array. Modern flexible and roll-out arrays reach on the order of 100-200 W/kg, meaning 5-10 tonnes of array per megawatt before radiators, structure and computers.

## Cooling: The Hard Part

- Every watt the computers use ends as heat, and in a vacuum there is no air or water to carry it away. Heat can leave only by **thermal radiation**, governed by the Stefan-Boltzmann law:

$$P = \varepsilon\, \sigma\, A\, \left(T^4 - T_{\text{sink}}^4\right)$$

- with emissivity $\varepsilon$ (close to 0.9 for good radiator coatings), $\sigma = 5.67 \times 10^{-8}$ W/m²/K⁴, radiator area $A$ and temperature $T$. Deep space is very cold, but radiators also absorb some sunlight and infrared from Earth, and chips must be kept below roughly 80-90 °C, so radiators run at perhaps 300-350 K. At 300 K a two-sided radiator rejects around 680 W/m² net, so one megawatt needs well over 1,000 m² of radiator, about half the area of the solar array (the first coding task). The $T^4$ law rewards hotter radiators, but the heat must be carried from chips to radiators by pumped fluid loops or heat pipes, each step losing temperature.

- On Earth, a data centre rejects heat with chillers, cooling towers and evaporating water; the radiator problem is unique to space, and it is a large part of the mass, the deployment complexity (large structures that must unfold reliably) and the engineering risk.

## Orbits, Latency and Communication

- **Latency to users** depends on altitude. A low Earth orbit at 550 km is about 4 ms round trip straight overhead and around 12 ms at low elevation angles; geostationary orbit, 36,000 km up, adds about a quarter of a second (the second coding task). For model inference, LEO latency is competitive with ground data centres reached over long fibre routes; for interactive applications GEO is slow.

- **Bandwidth to the ground** is limited by radio spectrum, weather and the number of ground stations in view; optical ground links are faster but blocked by clouds. Moving training data up and model weights down at terabyte scale is a real constraint, which favours workloads that need little input and output relative to computation.

- **Bandwidth between satellites** is the deeper problem for training. Distributed training (chapter 06's distributed deep learning file) needs enormous bandwidth between accelerators, terabits per second within a cluster. Optical inter-satellite links carry tens to hundreds of gigabits per second over long distances today. The proposed solution is **formation flying**: many satellites in very tight clusters, a kilometre or less apart, so that optical links can be short and fast, which in turn demands precise orbit control and careful collision avoidance. Algorithms that communicate less often (local updates with infrequent synchronisation, as in the decentralised AI file) fit this setting naturally.

## Radiation and Reliability Without Repair

- Above the atmosphere, electronics are bombarded by charged particles from the Sun and cosmic rays. Effects include **total ionising dose** (gradual degradation), **single-event upsets** (a particle flips a stored bit) and **single-event latch-up** (a destructive short circuit unless power is cut quickly). Space-qualified processors are hardened against these, but they lag commercial chips by many years; orbital AI proposals instead use commercial accelerators with shielding, error-correcting memory and fault-tolerant software. Google reported in 2025 that its TPU accelerators, tested in a proton beam, tolerated doses well beyond those expected over a five-year mission in its proposed orbit without permanent failure, with the high-bandwidth memory the most sensitive component.

- Nobody can replace a failed part in orbit. Every hard failure permanently removes capacity, so a constellation must launch spares and degrade gracefully; with 5% of accelerators failing per year, about a third extra capacity is needed to keep a target size for five years with high confidence (the fourth coding task). Soft errors are handled with **error-correcting codes**, frequent memory **scrubbing**, and checkpointing so that corrupted computations can be redone. Meanwhile AI hardware improves so quickly that a terrestrial data centre replaces its accelerators every few years; an orbital one is frozen at launch.

## Launch Costs and the Economics

- Launch cost has fallen from tens of thousands of dollars per kilogram (the Space Shuttle era) to a few thousand with reusable rockets, and fully reusable heavy launchers aim for a few hundred. The economics of orbital computing depend almost entirely on that number.

- The third coding task compares the cost of power and cooling: on the ground, electricity at $0.05-0.12 per kWh comes to roughly $500-1,300 per kW-year; in orbit, the launch cost of the power and thermal systems (arrays, radiators, batteries, structure) amortised over their lifetime. At 20 kg per kW over five years, launch costs per kW-year are about $10,800 at $2,700/kg, $2,000 at $500/kg and $800 at $200/kg. A simple uncertainty analysis finds orbit cheaper than ground electricity in under 1% of plausible scenarios at $1,000/kg and in about half at $200/kg. Google's Project Suncatcher study (2025) reached a similar conclusion: if launch prices fall below about $200/kg by the mid-2030s, the launch and operating costs could become comparable to the energy costs of an equivalent terrestrial data centre.

- That comparison is generous to orbit: it leaves out manufacturing the satellites, ground infrastructure, insurance, failures, deorbiting, and the shorter useful life of hardware that cannot be upgraded. The case for orbital computing is therefore a bet on very cheap launch, very lightweight power and thermal systems, and terrestrial constraints (grid connections, permitting, water) becoming expensive enough to matter more than the extra engineering.

## What Is Being Tried

- **Onboard processing for Earth observation** is already practical. Satellites generate far more imagery than they can downlink, so running models in orbit to discard cloudy images, detect ships or fires, or compress data saves bandwidth; ESA's Φ-sat-1 demonstrated AI-based cloud detection on board in 2020, and the approach is now common. This is edge computing in orbit (chapter 17's edge inference file), not data centres.

- **Orbital data centre projects** announced around 2025 include Google's Project Suncatcher, a research effort to fly TPU-equipped satellites in tight formation in a dawn-dusk orbit, with prototype satellites planned with Planet for around 2027; Starcloud, a start-up that launched a satellite carrying an NVIDIA H100 GPU in late 2025 and reported running and training small language models in orbit; and a Chinese constellation of computing satellites whose first batch was launched in 2025. Several launch and satellite companies have described plans for much larger systems. These are early demonstrations and studies, and their timelines and claims should be read with the usual caution for announcements.

## The Strongest Objections

- **Thermal mass and area** scale linearly with power, and megawatt- to gigawatt-scale radiators are vast, deployable structures with no flight heritage at that size.
- **Maintenance and obsolescence**: no repairs and no hardware refresh.
- **Bandwidth**: training-scale interconnect needs formation flying at unprecedented precision.
- **Space debris and sustainability**: thousands of large satellites raise collision risk and the danger of a debris cascade (Kessler syndrome), and they need reliable deorbiting; launches and re-entries have atmospheric effects that are not yet well understood; large bright structures affect astronomy.
- **Opportunity cost**: the same investment on the ground (new generation and transmission, nuclear power, sites near abundant renewables, more efficient chips and models; chapter 17) may deliver more computing sooner. Earlier unconventional ideas, such as Microsoft's experimental underwater data centre (2015-2020), showed interesting reliability results without becoming mainstream.

- None of these is obviously fatal, and all are engineering and economic questions with numbers attached. That is what makes the topic a good exercise: estimate the dominant terms, compare against the baseline, list what was left out, and update as real demonstrations report results.

1. Size the solar arrays and radiators for 1 MW of computing in a continuously sunlit orbit, and see how radiator area depends on its temperature through the Stefan-Boltzmann law.
```python
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

# Sizing the power and cooling for 1 MW of computing in a continuously sunlit orbit.
SOLAR = 1361.0             # W/m^2, sunlight above the atmosphere
SIGMA = 5.670e-8           # Stefan-Boltzmann constant, W/m^2/K^4
P_it = 1.0e6               # watts of servers (every watt ends up as heat)

# --- Power: solar arrays ---
eff, degradation, other_loads = 0.30, 0.85, 1.10      # cell efficiency, end-of-life factor, +10% for everything else
array_w_per_m2 = SOLAR * eff * degradation
array_area = P_it * other_loads / array_w_per_m2
print(f"solar array: {array_w_per_m2:.0f} W/m^2 delivered -> {array_area:,.0f} m^2 for 1 MW "
      f"(a square about {np.sqrt(array_area):.0f} m on a side)")
for w_per_kg in [100, 200]:                              # specific power of the array, a key technology number
    print(f"   at {w_per_kg} W/kg the arrays weigh {P_it * other_loads / w_per_kg / 1000:,.1f} tonnes")

# --- Cooling: in vacuum, heat leaves only by radiation, P = emissivity * sigma * area * (T^4 - T_sink^4) ---
emissivity, sides, absorbed = 0.90, 2, 150.0           # two-sided panel; ~150 W/m^2 of absorbed sunlight/Earth IR
def radiator_area(T):
    net = sides * emissivity * SIGMA * T ** 4 - absorbed  # net watts rejected per m^2 of panel
    return np.where(net > 0, P_it * other_loads / net, np.inf)
print("\nradiator temperature | panel area needed")
for T in [280, 300, 330, 360, 400]:
    print(f"   {T} K ({T - 273:3d} C)   | {radiator_area(T):8,.0f} m^2")
print("\nChips must stay below roughly 80-90 C, and heat must be carried from chips to radiators (with some"
      "\ntemperature drop), so radiators run cooler than the chips. At a radiator temperature of 300 K the"
      f"\nradiator needs {radiator_area(300) / array_area:.0%} of the solar array's area: the same order of size, and every"
      "\nsquare metre has to be launched, deployed and kept pointed away from the Sun.")

Ts = np.linspace(260, 420, 100)
plt.figure(figsize=(8, 4.5))
plt.plot(Ts - 273, radiator_area(Ts), color='#e74c3c', label="radiator area")
plt.axhline(array_area, color='#f39c12', linestyle='--', label="solar array area")
plt.yscale('log'); plt.xlabel("radiator temperature (C)"); plt.ylabel("m^2 per MW")
plt.title("Rejecting heat by radiation alone needs very large radiators"); plt.legend(); plt.show()
```

2. Orbital mechanics for computing: orbital periods from Kepler's third law, the worst-case fraction of each orbit in Earth's shadow, and round-trip latency to the ground for low and geostationary orbits.
```python
import numpy as np

MU = 3.986e14            # Earth's gravitational parameter, m^3/s^2
R = 6371e3               # Earth's radius, m
C = 299_792_458.0        # speed of light, m/s

def period(h):           # Kepler's third law for a circular orbit
    return 2 * np.pi * np.sqrt((R + h) ** 3 / MU)

def eclipse_fraction(h):
    """Fraction of a circular orbit in Earth's shadow when the Sun lies in the orbital plane (worst case),
    treating the shadow as a cylinder: the satellite is shadowed for an arc of 2 * arcsin(R / (R + h))."""
    return np.arcsin(R / (R + h)) / np.pi

def slant_range(h, elevation_deg):
    """Distance from a ground station to a satellite seen at the given elevation above the horizon."""
    e = np.radians(elevation_deg)
    return np.sqrt((R + h) ** 2 - (R * np.cos(e)) ** 2) - R * np.sin(e)

print(f"{'orbit':26s} {'period':>8s} {'max eclipse':>12s} {'round trip, overhead':>21s} {'at 10 deg elevation':>20s}")
for name, h in [("ISS-like LEO (400 km)", 400e3), ("Starlink-like LEO (550 km)", 550e3),
                ("higher LEO (1,200 km)", 1200e3), ("geostationary (35,786 km)", 35786e3)]:
    rtt0 = 2 * h / C * 1000
    rtt10 = 2 * slant_range(h, 10) / C * 1000
    print(f"{name:26s} {period(h) / 60:7.1f}m {eclipse_fraction(h):11.0%} {rtt0:18.1f} ms {rtt10:17.1f} ms")
print("(light in optical fibre travels about 1.5x slower than in vacuum, and terrestrial routes are longer than"
      "\n straight lines, so a LEO hop can be competitive with long-distance fibre, while GEO adds ~0.25 s.)")

print("\nA dawn-dusk sun-synchronous orbit keeps the orbital plane almost perpendicular to the Sun, so the"
      "\nsatellite rides the day-night line and is in sunlight nearly all the time (apart from short seasonal"
      "\neclipses). In the worst-case geometry above, a 550 km satellite would spend "
      f"{eclipse_fraction(550e3):.0%} of each orbit in darkness,"
      "\nneeding batteries sized for about "
      f"{period(550e3) * eclipse_fraction(550e3) / 60:.0f} minutes of full load per orbit.")

# A training cluster needs very high bandwidth between nodes, so satellites would fly in tight formation.
for d_km in [1, 100, 1000]:
    print(f"light delay between satellites {d_km:5d} km apart: {d_km * 1e3 / C * 1e6:8.1f} microseconds")
```

3. A simple economic model: launch cost of power and cooling per kW-year in orbit against terrestrial electricity prices, with an uncertainty analysis.
```python
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

# A deliberately simple economic comparison: the cost per kW-year of POWER AND COOLING.
# The computers themselves cost about the same on the ground or in orbit, so they are left out,
# as are satellite manufacturing, ground stations, insurance and failures (all of which favour the ground).
HOURS = 8760

def terrestrial_cost(price_per_kwh, pue=1.2):
    """Electricity for 1 kW of IT for a year, including cooling overhead (power usage effectiveness)."""
    return price_per_kwh * HOURS * pue

def orbital_cost(launch_per_kg, kg_per_kw, lifetime_years):
    """Launch cost of the power and thermal system (arrays, radiators, batteries, structure) per kW, amortised."""
    return launch_per_kg * kg_per_kw / lifetime_years

print(f"terrestrial electricity: ${terrestrial_cost(0.05):,.0f} to ${terrestrial_cost(0.12):,.0f} per kW-year "
      "(at $0.05-0.12 per kWh)")
print("\norbital launch cost per kW-year, 5-year life:")
print(" $/kg launched | 10 kg/kW | 20 kg/kW | 40 kg/kW")
for price in [10000, 2700, 1000, 500, 200]:
    print(f" {price:13,d} | " + " | ".join(f"{orbital_cost(price, m, 5):8,.0f}" for m in [10, 20, 40]))
print("(roughly: $10,000/kg was the historical norm, about $2,700/kg a reusable Falcon 9 list price,"
      "\n and a few hundred dollars per kg is the hope for fully reusable heavy launchers)")

# Uncertainty: sample plausible ranges and ask how often orbit beats the ground
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
n = 100000
for label, price in [("$1,000/kg", 1000), ("$500/kg", 500), ("$200/kg", 200)]:
    kg_per_kw = rng.uniform(8, 40, n)
    life = rng.uniform(3, 8, n)
    ground = terrestrial_cost(rng.uniform(0.04, 0.12, n))
    space = orbital_cost(price * rng.lognormal(0, 0.3, n), kg_per_kw, life)
    print(f"launch at {label:10s}: orbit cheaper than ground electricity in {np.mean(space < ground):5.1%} of scenarios")

prices = np.geomspace(100, 10000, 100)
plt.figure(figsize=(8, 4.5))
for m, c in [(10, '#27ae60'), (20, '#3498db'), (40, '#e74c3c')]:
    plt.loglog(prices, orbital_cost(prices, m, 5), color=c, label=f"orbit, {m} kg/kW, 5 years")
plt.axhspan(terrestrial_cost(0.05), terrestrial_cost(0.12), color='#95a5a6', alpha=0.3, label="ground electricity")
plt.xlabel("launch price ($/kg)"); plt.ylabel("$ per kW-year"); plt.legend(); plt.show()
print("\nThe case for orbital computing rests on launch prices falling by roughly an order of magnitude and on"
      "\nvery light power and thermal systems. Even then, this comparison ignores most of the orbital costs.")
```

4. Reliability without repair: how many spare accelerators a constellation needs as hardware fails, and how error-correcting codes and memory scrubbing contain radiation-induced bit flips.
```python
import numpy as np
from math import comb, exp

# 1. No repairs in orbit: accelerators that fail stay failed. How much spare capacity is needed?
rng = np.random.default_rng(1)
n_chips, years = 1000, 5
print("annual failure rate | capacity left after 5 years (expected) | spares for 95% confidence of >= 1000 working")
for lam in [0.02, 0.05, 0.10]:                        # failures per chip per year (hard faults: radiation, thermal cycling)
    p_survive = exp(-lam * years)
    for spares in range(0, 2000, 10):                 # smallest launch size that keeps 1000 working chips
        n = n_chips + spares
        alive = rng.binomial(n, p_survive, 20000)
        if np.mean(alive >= n_chips) >= 0.95: break
    print(f"{lam:19.0%} | {p_survive:38.1%} | {spares:6d} extra chips ({spares / n_chips:.0%})")

# 2. Soft errors: radiation flips bits. Error-correcting codes (SECDED) fix one flipped bit per word and
# detect two; periodic "scrubbing" (read, correct, rewrite) stops single flips accumulating into doubles.
words = 80e9 / 8                     # 80 GB of accelerator memory in 64-bit words
upsets_per_bit_per_day = 1e-10       # an illustrative upset rate for memory in low Earth orbit
print(f"\nwithout error correction: about {words * 64 * upsets_per_bit_per_day:.0f} silently flipped bits per day in 80 GB")
for scrub_hours in [24, 1, 0.01]:
    p_bit = upsets_per_bit_per_day * scrub_hours / 24          # chance a given bit flips between scrubs
    p_word_double = comb(72, 2) * p_bit ** 2                   # two flips in one 72-bit codeword (64 data + 8 check)
    per_day = words * p_word_double * (24 / scrub_hours)
    print(f"scrub every {scrub_hours:5} h: expected uncorrectable words per day {per_day:.2e}")
print("\nHard failures are planned for with spares and graceful degradation; soft errors with ECC, scrubbing,"
      "\nand checkpointing so a corrupted computation can be redone. Both add mass, power and complexity that a"
      "\nground data centre handles with a technician and a replacement part.")
```
